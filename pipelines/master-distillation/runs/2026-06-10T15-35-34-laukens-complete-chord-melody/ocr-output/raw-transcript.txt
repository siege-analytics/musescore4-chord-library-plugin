Here’s a ‘bird’s eye overview’ on how to arrange a chord melody.

We'll be going into much more detail later in the course, but in this section, you will learn what you
need to do in a nutshell for creating your own chord melody arrangements:

e Pick a suitable tune

e Change the key if necessary for better range and playability
e Learn the melody on top 2 (or 3) strings

e Choose chord voicings to add below melody notes
Step 1 - Picking a Suitable Tune

Not all tunes are playable as chord melodies or can be difficult.

For your first arrangement, I recommend you stick to ballads, rather than the faster bebop tunes. The
slower tempo and longer-held melody notes in ballads will be much easier for your first project.

I’m going to use Autumn Leaves for this example as it ticks all these boxes - it can be played as a
ballad and it has some long melody notes, a perfect choice.

Step 2 - Change Key if Necessary for Range and Playability

On guitar, finding a suitable range for your arrangement is important:

e If the arrangement is too high, it will sound like it doesn’t have enough depth.
e On the other hand, if your arrangement is too low in the register, it will sound too dark and
muddy.

Finding the ideal range is usually what will dictate your choice of key. You'll need to experiment to
find a key that works.

A good rule of thumb:

Search for a key where you can play the melody on the top 3 strings, roughly anywhere from the
3rd to the 9th fret.

Some of those notes of Autumn Leaves in Gm are too low - notice in the previous example how many
notes are on the 4th and Sth strings.

There is a way to combine a standard plectrum with fingerstyle, called hybrid picking.

In this case, you hold a plectrum as usual but use your free fingers (the middle, ring, and pinky) to
play chord voicings whilst using the plectrum to pluck the lowest note in the chord.

Personally, I find this technique awkward especially due to the shorter length of the pinky finger.

So, for chord melody, we usually recommend using either fingerstyle or using a regular plectrum.

Action Steps: think about which approach you would like to use for this course and what you are
familiar with technique-wise already. Most of the examples in the course will be fingerstyle, but
you can easily tweak the examples to use block chords suitable for the strumming plectrum style.
Module 2 - Chord
VocabularyThe Three Harmonic Families

in music, there are three primary colors when it comes to harmony.
A chord must belong to one of the following classifications:

e Major — e.g. CMaj7, C6, CMaj9
e Minor — e.g. Cm7, Cm9, Cm7b5
e Dominant — e.g. C7, C9, C13, C7(b9)

3 Harmonic Families

The above diagram is a good generalization, however it’s helpful to add a further sub-classification
to minor and dominant chords as follows:

3 Harmonic Families

Minor:

e ‘m7’ sub-family - e.g. Cm7, Cm9, Cm13
e ‘m7b5’ sub-family - e.g. Cm7b5, Cm1ib5

Dominant:

e ‘7’ or ‘natural dominant’ sub-family - e.g. C7, C9, C13
e ‘7Zalt’ or ‘altered dominant’ sub-family - e.g. C7(b9). C7(b13b9), C7(b5), C7alt

I’m pointing this out early on in this course because it can often sound wrong playing a natural
dominant instead of an altered dominant or playing a m7 instead of a m7b5 chord depending on the
harmonic situation.

We'll cover these issues in more detail in the following lessons.

Keep the above concepts in mind as we head to the next lesson on your first chord voicing ‘collection’
you'll use to arrange your first chord melody.
Your First Chord Collection

Alright — time to get our hands dirty and learn some chord voicings.

In this lesson, you’re going to come away with a chord collection that you can use when harmonizing
major chords in a chord melody.

What Is a Chord Collection?

A chord collection is a group of chords that can be played over a particular chord quality or
progression.

They are useful because the sequence of chords in the collection includes every diatonic interval as
the top note of the chords.

Which means: no matter which note is in the melody line, you can harmonize it by referring to the
chord collection.

Major ii V I Chord Collections

In the previous lesson, we learned how to apply major chords to each note in a C major scale.
But we are going to need more than just major chords for chord melody arranging.

Let’s refer back to our 3 Harmonic Families for a moment:

3 Harmonic Families

As you can see from the above diagram, we also need minor and dominant chords in addition to
major chords if we are going to be able to make chord melody arrangements.

One of the best ways to learn useful combinations of major, minor, and dominant chords is to learn
voicings that suit major ii V Iprogressions.

The reason:
The ii-V-I progression contains a chord from each harmonic family:

e ii — minor
e V - dominant
e I - major

So let’s build out our knowledge further by learning suitable minor and dominantchord voicings that
will work in the key of C major. Then, we'll combine the voicings to make ii V I progressions in each
area of the fretboard.
The aim of all this is to help you build out a library of useful voicings that you can use in your
chord melody arrangements.

So far in this course, you have played a great many different types of chords included in all those

It’s important we look at how to decode these chord symbols, as it can help you not only understand

What Is a Chord Symbol?

| A chord symbol is an attempt to describe the type of sound produced by a group of notes.

Each symbol you see stands for a formula of intervals:

So Cmaj7 =CEGB

The formulas generally follow a predictable pattern (with some exceptions).

Labelling Extensions and Altered Tensions

An extension (sometimes called a ‘natural’ or ‘diatonic’ tension) is an interval of the chord which
naturally occurs in the parent scale of the key of the progression. Extensions are included as part of
the chord name. Examples are G9, G13, and GMaj9.

Altered tensions are notes that are outside the parent scale of the key of the progression, and are
(usually) added in parentheses after the chord name. Examples are GMaj7(#11), G7(b13#9).

Tip - Pencil & Paper vs Software

For writing out your arrangement, there’s a wide range of options when it comes to software: Guitar
Pro, Sibelius, Finale, Musescore — the list goes on.

However, the cheapest and most straightforward method is good ol’ fashioned pencil and paper.

Although it can take a little longer with this method, you will train your ears much more compared to
relying on software.

But:

Feel free to use software if you’re already familiar with a software package, or if you’re keen to give
software a try. If you’re in this boat, I can highly recommend Guitar Pro as a solid choice (and an
affordable one at that).

Whatever method you choose, it’s important to write down your arrangement somehow. Writing gives
you the ability to keep track of your ideas and thus refine them more easily (which can be difficult to
do if you are arranging entirely by ear).

Analyze the Harmony

Our next step in our arranging journey is to gain an understanding of how the chord progression
works for our chosen jazz standard.

We are aiming to get 3 outcomes from this:

1. To work out the quality of each chord in the progression (major, minor, dominant).
2. To observe any key changes in the tune.
3. To see if we can spot common chord sequences in the progression, such as ii - V — Is etc.

Analysis Conventions

Harmonic analysis uses the following conventions:

e Roman numbers are designated to indicate a chord’s function.
e Upper case Roman numerals indicate a chord being major or dominant.
e Lower case Roman numerals indicate a chord being minor.

The reason:

The interval number we give a melody note must relate to the current chord in the

1, b9, 9, b3, 3, 11, #11, 5,b13, 13, b7, 7

Often in jazz melodies, the chromatic notes in a melody can be considered passing tones if they
are not on a strong downbeat and simply lead to a stronger chord tone, so you can use the label
PT for those.

for Solo GuitarIntroduction to Arranging for Solo Guitar

You're at an exciting point in this course — solo jazz guitar arranging is where a lot of creative
possibilities can open up in your guitar playing.

There’s nothing quite like the feeling of being able to play a rich, intricate arrangement as a complete
performance by yourself, on a single guitar.

The good news:

You’ve done most of the hard work already. All the techniques we learned for basic “trio” chord
melody arranging we covered earlier in the course can still be applied to solo jazz guitar versions of
tunes.

We only need to add a few extra elements to make your arrangements sound good on guitar
unaccompanied.

The “Illusion” of Solo Jazz Guitar

The aim of solo jazz guitar performance is to give the audience an experience of a whole band on a
single guitar - bass, rhythm section, and top melody line.

Here’s the thing:

It is not necessary to have a// these elements at all times in an arrangement. (That usually makes the
arrangement way too difficult).

A good solo guitar arranger simply suggests these elements throughout the performance. Play a
measure of walking bass, followed by a chord line, followed by unaccompanied single lines —
alternating through these different devices gives the ‘illusion’ to the audience of a complete band.

The listener’s mind will fill in the gaps.
Pick vs Fingers for Solo Guitar

As you will see, many of the voicings you are about to learn are quite difficult to play with a pick if
you intend to sound the notes simultaneously without tricky muting techniques. For this reason, I
gravitate to using fingerstyle picking for solo guitar.

But, you can still use these voicings with a pick. Some players strike the bass note first with their pick
then follow up with a strum of the upper notes in the voicing - creative techniques like this can open
up possibilities for using a plectrum in a solo guitar setting.

Note that Joe Pass was primarily a plectrum player in his earlier career but moved exclusively to
fingerstyle for solo guitar which became his focus later in his career - presumably because it was
easier to make intricate solo arrangements with the fingerstyle technique.

Check out Appendix 2 of this course for some effective fingerstyle drills if you’re keen to build your
skills in this area.

Key Selection and Open Strings

With the addition of the bass notes, fingerings of chord voicings can be more difficult with solo guitar.
The transition from chord to chord can become burdensome for the fretting hand.

An excellent way around this is to choose a key for your arrangement that allows the use of open
strings - especially for the bass notes.

Choosing a key like A, E, or D gives you essentially another set of free fingers. Without needing to fret
the occasional bass note, you can make things much easier technically.

The other advantage is that open strings enable you to hit bass notes in the lower register when you
play high chord voicings on top.

A common issue with many jazz guitar arrangements is that the bass is too close to the upper
harmony, which results in a thin sound.

For this reason, guitarists like George Van Eps and Lenny Breau added an additional 7th string to the
guitar so that those lower bass notes would be accessible in every position of the guitar.

when a recap of a melodic motive happens, so that the audience can still recognize the melody
despite the unorthodox harmony.

Melodic common tone substitution rule: substitute any chord for another chord, as long as
the new chord has a note in common with it that you use as the melody note on top.

Task: experiment with this colorful substitution method in your arrangements — there is a
dynamic universe of sound within this simple technique.

The substitutions we have covered in the previous lessons will open your arranging up to an ocean of
possibilities - explore each one in turn as you move forward with your arrangements.

In the next lesson, you'll look at alternative ways of spicing up a chord melody performance through
the use of jazz licks and chord phrases...
Adding Jazz Licks and Prases

Here’s another technique for spicing up your arrangements of chord melodies — but it’s one of the
most important ones.

Here we get to experiment with swapping out the original melody with our own single lines and

It may seem counterintuitive to abandon the melody in your arrangement (it is called ‘chord melody’
after all).

But:

Especially when it comes to solo jazz guitar, things can get repetitive quickly, and ways to create
more variety are an essential part of your toolbox.

Joe Pass would often not play the complete melody in an arrangement and place his own phrases
in place to keep the audience on its toes.

Whenever you get a jazz chart with a melodic figure that repeats (e.g. a repeat of an A section) - this
can be a good place to use this technique.

Completing Your Arrangement and Getting Ready to Perform

Now that you have all the tools in your toolkit to be a proficient chord melody arranger, there are just
a few final steps we need to cover to ensure success on the concert stage.

Arranging a chord melody is only 50% of the work - the other 50% is turning your arrangement
into a great performance on your guitar.

Here are some tips to get your performance of your chord melody arrangement in tip-top shape.

Using a metronome, especially for solo jazz guitar, is crucial to developing a strong performance.

Because solo jazz guitar is unaccompanied, it’s very easy to let the rhythm slide more and more
behind the beat.

Lagging too far behind the beat gives the whole arrangement a ‘sleepy’ feel which can result in a
lackluster performance.

A little flexibility is fine here and there, but overall the rhythm needs to be alive and steady.
Practice your arrangement with a metronome as follows:

e Have the metronome click on every beat.
e Then try the metronome clicking on beats 1 and 3 only.
e Then have a go with the metronome on beats 2 and 4 only.

The final version is most desirable for jazz as it helps give the music more of a swing.
Make Time to Practice

Simple but often overlooked:

If you don’t apply yourself daily to learning an arrangement until it’s mastered, you'll find your
progress to be frustratingly slow.

Make sure if you are committing to perform your chord melody, you apply yourself each day, even for
a short duration of 10-15 minutes — it is the proven path for getting results on your instrument.

Set Small Goals for Each Session

Don’t try to get the whole arrangement perfect in a single practice session.

Instead, methodically work through just 1 or 2 bars per session. Spend time slowly practicing the
fingering and carefully checking the notes are accurate and that everything is moving comfortably
under your fingers.

When 1-2 bars are flowing and feel good under the hands, move to the next 2 bars in your next
session and so on, gradually learning the arrangement one step at a time.

Don’t be in a rush to get the whole arrangement correct right away - that will come with daily
consistent application.

Appendix 2 -
Fingerstyle TrainingIntroduction to Fingerstyle Guitar

Fingerstyle technique will open up a whole world of creative possibilities for your jazz guitar playing.

This technique allows you to easily play multiple voices at once on one guitar — enabling you to create
complex chord melody arrangements and chord solos.

Note: you don’t need a particular type of guitar for fingerstyle. This plucking technique can be used
on steel string, nylon string, electric and acoustic instruments.
Finger Naming

Throughout this section on fingerstyle exercises, you'll notice lettering like this on the diagrams:

| 4 4 4
Gifs ss 3 [ss 3 sj
a ee ee
[2b 3b fb]
Si § § $ §
———_—=<_——

These letters stand for the five picking hand fingers, based on the Spanish names for each finger:

e p = thumb (“pulgar” in Spanish)

i = index (“indice” in Spanish)

m = middle (“medio” in Spanish)

a = ring finger (“anular” in Spanish)

c = pinky finger (“chiquito” in Spanish)

(This is the same naming convention as used by classical guitarists).

In the above diagram, I’ve indicated to play the exercise with alternating i and m fingers, then once
you are comfortable with that try with p and i. The ‘sim’ means to continue the finger pattern for the
exercise.

It’s very important for fingerstyle that, right from the start, you adopt a hand position that
promotes agility and freedom of movement.

Doing this will set you up for success later on as you develop this technique.
Tips for Correct Fingerstyle Hand Placement

e Keep the wrist naturally curved in your plucking hand —- avoid flatting or overarching the wrist too
much.

e Keep the wrist in alignment with the arm, avoid kinking the wrist at an angle (it’s ok to strike the
strings slightly diagonally).

e Keep your hand relaxed as possible, take frequent breaks while you are still learning these
techniques.

e Fingernails can be helpful for getting more depth and tone of the sound, but is entirely optional
(especially on electric guitar).

Now that you’re up to speed with the names of each finger and the key points on good fingerstyle
technique, now head to the next lesson for some essential fingerstyle workouts.
Rest Stroke vs Free Stroke

Now that you’ve covered the correct hand and arm placement for fingerstyle technique, in this lesson
you're going to have a closer look at the picking motion of the fingers themselves.

There are two methods of plucking the string with fingerstyle:

e “Rest stroke” —- when you play a string and then rest your finger on the string behind the one
you plucked at the end of the motion.

e “Free stroke” — when you pluck the string and let your finger fly “freely” above the other strings
as you end the motion.

Let’s do some exercises which will work out your fingerstyle technique!

Walking Finger Workouts

“Walking finger” exercises are a great way to build overall coordination and speed for the plucking
hand.

They involve playing basic patterns whilst alternating either the i and m fingers, or i and a fingers.

Note: Although it can feel initially more awkward, I personally have found I get much more speed
with alternating i and a, rather than i and m. But everyone’s hands are different, so practice both
combinations for now - you decide on your main picking combination down the track.

The following exercises are straightforward. But - even if you are already familiar with fingerstyle use
these as an opportunity to check that you have a good hand and arm position and that you have
optimal plucking motion of the fingers.

Task: practice one of the walking finger workouts daily for 5 minutes until you feel the alternating
finger motion becoming smooth and easy. Master each exercise before moving to the next.

Using the Thumb

For a typical fingerstyle jazz guitarist, the thumb is generally reserved for playing bass notes in
chords or to play basslines alongside melodies on the higher strings.

Wes was more using his thumb as a plectrum substitute rather than a typical fingerstyle technique.)

The motion should come from the lowest “3rd” joint of the thumb, with a small circular motion as you
pluck — similar to the motion of a paddle on a rowing boat.

Let’s get into some exercises to get our thumb fit and agile. Ensure that your hands feel relaxed and
free from tension and that you’ve adopted a good hand position as you play each exercise.

thumb becoming smooth and easy. Master each exercise before moving to the next.

Two-String Exercises

In this and the following lessons, we’re going to build upon what you’ve covered so far and do a deep
dive on plucking across multiple strings.

These are very important techniques to learn as a jazz guitarist, as they will enable you to play
intricate chord melody arrangements with multiple moving voices.

Tips for Plucking Multiple Strings

Ensure that your wrist is rolled slightly more towards the headstock to allow more space between the
index finger and thumb. If your hand position is incorrect, these two fingers will obstruct each other’s
motion when those fingers pluck notes together.

| The main ways to pluck adjacent strings is with the p and i fingers, or i and m fingers.

Regardless of which fingers you use, ensure that both fingers sound the notes simultaneously.

For 3 notes at once, it’s preferable to use pi m, or i ma, so it’s worth practicing both these
combinations.

I rarely use the little finger (c) on the right hand with fingerstyle technique — I tend to stick to 4-note
voicings as a maximum which means you will only need to use your four strongest fingers - pi ma.

Now that you've got familiarity with the basic movements that you need to do as a fingerstyle player
when picking multiple strings, let’s get to work with the following exercises which will build your
technique in this area step by step.

For most of these exercises, I suggest you try another plucking finger combination on the repeats to
help work out more picking combinations.

Task: practice one of the two string exercises daily for 5 minutes until fluent. Master each
exercise before moving to the next.

Three-String Exercises

Now that you've covered plucking two strings, let’s add more strings to the mix!

In this lesson, you’re going to look at working out your picking hand while you tackle negotiating
three strings.

The following patterns you’re going to learn are very useful for comping and solo guitar chord melody,
so they are essential exercises to know for that reason.

If you find these exercises a challenge, it would be worth revising the general tips on hand position
and finger motion covered in the first lesson of this module. Remember to keep your hand as relaxed
as possible.

Task: practice these three string exercises daily for 5 minutes until fluent. Master each exercise
before moving to the next.

Here’s an exercise to get you started plucking 3 strings simultaneously — make sure all notes sound at

This exercise will also give your position shifting a good workout too. It reminds me a bit of

Etude 2 - Bossa Nova Comping Etude

This is another useful exercise that I recommend to consolidate your multiple-strings technique.

This exercise is a great workout for fingerstyle technique and enables you to quickly gain facility in
the plucking hand as well as the independence of the thumb.

The exercise comprises a straightforward ii V I sequence, moving down two frets at a time.

I would suggest practicing the picking pattern over a single chord for a while until you get it under
your fingers first. After that, start to build the pattern over the full chord progression.

Task: get started with this exercise by working out how to change the chords with the fretting
hand. Once confident, build the picking pattern in the plucking hand step-by-step.

About The Author

Hi, my name is Greg O’Rourke.

Award-winning Australian guitarist Greg O’Rourke received his Bachelor Of
Music Honours degree in 2006 and was a scholarship holder at the Australian
National University School Of Music.

Originally a trained classical guitarist, Greg’s main specialty is now in jazz
guitar. Greg’s versatile ability on the guitar is credited to several teachers he
has studied with over the years, including Mike Price and Don Andrews, well-known performers and
teachers in the Australian jazz guitar scene. Greg also undertook extensive study with Tim Kain, one
of Australia’s leading classical guitar performers and teachers.

Greg has given prizewinning performances at the Australian National University Chamber Music
Competition and was awarded 1st prize in the Chamber Music division of the 2004 Australian
International Guitar Competition.

These days, Greg’s main focus is as head instructor and director of the jazz guitar website
FretDojo.com. Greg’s mission is to empower guitarists worldwide with the tools to make great music
by providing high-quality step-by-step courses and materials. He has thousands of subscribers to his
website and Youtube channel, helping guitarists worldwide improve their jazz guitar playing.

Greg has also been featured on several other high-profile guitar websites including Jazz Guitar Online,
Fundamental Changes, and Takelessons.
