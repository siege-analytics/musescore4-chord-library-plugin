DISCLAIMER/WARNING/CAUTIONIS

Please be advised that the author and publisher take no responsibility whatsoever for
any (and all) accidental and inadvertent self-inflicted injuries that may occur from exploring
the material in this book. (This is especially relevant in regard to the left hand fingers, wrist,
forearm, shoulder, muscles, tendons, bones, cartilage, nerves, etc., et al.)

Any guitarist who works with this book is going to be exposed to literally hundreds of
chords that involve stretches and fingering configurations that will be totally new and unfa-
miliar. Be particularly cautious with descending voice-leadings that move down the finger-
board. With each new descending chord, the distance between the frets gradually increases.

Each (and every) guitarist must continuously monitor the sensations they perceive in
their left (fingering) hand. Error on the side of being overly careful. “No pain—is the way to
go.” Perhaps it would be even better to put it this way: “Not even the slightest hint of any
discomfort—is the way to go.”

The problem lies with the fact that a large percentage of this material is going to be new
to pretty much every guitarist who begins to work with it. When guitarists get a chance to
play a whole bunch of stuff that is not only new to them, but also has a uniquely different and
compelling sound to it, there is a tendency to want to overdo it. All these new sounds can
have an almost narcotic-like effect. The ear perks up; the mind comes into sharp focus; the
emotions begin to soar; the imagination begins to project a future when you'll be able to use
all this great stuff to completely blow people away(!)...all at the expense of your left hand
which, at this point, starts to feel like it’s just been run over by a truck.

You just got caught up in “seduction by novelty.” You stopped monitoring the sensa-
tions in your left (fingering) hand. You did too much. You over-extended your physical limits.
You over-stretched. You had a nice ride, for sure. But now you'll have to pay the price for
days, weeks, possibly months. (Believe me, I’m not exaggerating!) In some cases, the damage
could involve years of recovery time. Occasionally, it could cause damage beyond recovery
time. The word for that is “permanent.” The phrase in sports terminology is “career-ending.”

Think about it —Picture yourself with permanent damage—Now what?

Q: Mr. Goodchord, are you trying to scare us?
A: You got that right!

You must exercise your sensitivity, your intelligence, your caution and your good
judgement. No one else can do this for you. Only you can. And when you do, there should be
no problem.

You have been WARNED)! (Please take good care of your hands!)

This present book finishes the task (begun with Volume I) of completing “The Mother
Lode.” (Fellow guitarist Joe Diorio came up with this name when he received an advance copy of

In Volume I, all the chords have names.

In Volume I, none of the chords have names.

You may be asking “Why is that?”

I would answer that it seemed like a good idea at the time...

Actually, the reason that all the chords in Volume II are left nameless is that the names one
might use get complicated. [Cmaj9th(noSth), for example.] Also, these names could easily tend
to limit how the chords could be used. Since the chords in Volume II revolve around structures of
4th intervals and 2nd intervals, they are more ambiguous sounding than the structures in Vol-
ume I. Also, in many cases they are more flexible in terms of multiple functions. But the truth be
told, “it just seemed like a good idea at the time...” (and I’m going to stick with it!)

MORE ABOUT 4THS

As mentioned earlier, 3-part 4ths in Melodic and Harmonic Minor have some tricky con-
siderations. [the diminished 4th] Fortunately, those problems don’t occur in the Major Scale.

Here's a really great thing to do that is very “guitar-friendly.” After you've spent some
time exploring the 3-part 4ths in C Major, tune the low E string down a whole step to D. Use
the low D string as a pedal, [you could also use the A string] and play through all the cycles! I
think you'll be very pleased with the results. {you’d be playing in D dorian mode; since there
aren’t any “avoid” notes in Dorian, pretty much anything and everything you play will work
very nicely!} This approach could also be applied to all the other modes of the C Major Scale,

but this D Dorian thing is by far the easiest!

While I’m on the subject, you might also consider the possibility of ARPEGGLIATING
the 4th voicings to arrive at WAY COOL melodies! [The next time someone calls “So What,” or

another tune of that ilk, you'll be ready...]

I think that it becomes very obvious in the exploring of all of this material, that guitar-
ists who play finger-style have a huge advantage over those who play only with a pick.

In exploring options like counterpoint, 3-part chords and unusual spread voicings,
finger-style guitarists can do much more of this material.

Finger-style guitarists can use barres and 1/2 barres and 1/3 barres to get the job done.

OCTAVE INSERTION (another small but important consideration...)

Perhaps we could have used the term “octave displacement,” but I prefer to use newer
terms when I get the chance to make them up. [A true improviser, I guess......]

Octave insertion is just what you might guess: inserting an octave between any 2 voices
within a 3-part chord or a 4-part chord. [Actually, I guess that we could include 2-note voicings
as well...what we're calling counterpoint.]

In Volume I, we provided 6 voicings for a 3-part chord. Lets use the C Major triad as an
example :

AUSEFUL TIP

In order to explore the 2-part and 3-part options that have been discussed in the easiest
possible manner, I would recommend the following:

1) Take a blank 4x6 index card and cut it into eight 1/2 inch x 6 inch strips.
[You could also use paper, but the index card is better because of its thickness. ]

2) Trim off just a bit of the width of all 8 strips.

3) Use these strips to cover up different lines [melodies] that need to be omitted in
working with the 2-part and 3-part options.

[NOTE: 4 strips would be enough for the 3-part material, but you'll need all 8 strips to
do the 2-part stuff. {counterpoint} ]

Believe me when I say that it makes it so much easier to work with these other options.

bound was a good one...]

So far, within the fabric of the M-Lode, we have identified and used two 3-part chord
families: Triads and 3-part 4th voicings. [If you remember, we described these as “supple-
ments” for the 4-part chords.]

There are, in fact, 3 other members of the 3-part chord families. They would be the
following: 3-part Spread Clusters, 7th Chord [no 5th] and 7th Chord [no 3rd]. These 3-part
chords have not been IDENTIFIED as such, but they are there, within the M-Lode...(which is
to say that they have been FOUND). All of them can be discovered by exploring the 3-part
options of the 4-part chords. [If you remember, it just amounts to leaving out one of the
voices...] There are 4 possible configurations. [as has been mentioned earlier...]

These possibilities turn out to be some of the most “guitar-friendly” options for us
guitarists. Almost all of them are much easier to play, from a guitarists’ perspective. And they
still have that compelling “MUSIC-REPLICA” sound and effect, which is the result of the
consistent [and, I believe, excellent] voice-leading. These 3-part voicings have not been
NAMED because that’s the NAME of Volume 2: DO NOT NAME THAT CHORD.

Trust me, in Volume 3, we'll take a stab at giving them some names....

“HOUSTON, WE HAVE A PROBLEM...”

When you begin to work with the spread cluster material in Volume II, you may notice
a small problem. Cycles 2 and 7 have two different voice-leadings. [One for 4-way close, and
another one for the other five voicing types...] {Neither of these voice-leadings is particularly
pleasing from a sound perspective...}

We felt that this was necessary [especially for guitarists] because spread clusters present
unique voice-leading problems that don’t occur in the other 4 chord families. It involves the
interval of a diatonic 5th that occurs in the movement from one inversion to the next within
the same chord. For guitarists, this could lead to potential problems with voices crossing each
other if we had chosen to uses voice-leadings that were more pleasing from a sound perspec-
tive. Consequently, we opted for voice-leadings that at least presented the material in a useful
and somewhat “guitar-friendlier” manner, since much of this is going to be completely new to
a large percentage of the whole guitar world.

All of these spread cluster voicings are extremely interesting. [And mostly unknown,
until now...] And many of them are difficult to play on the guitar, so BE VERY CAREFUL!!!
[Other choices for voice-leading options will be presented in Volume 3.]
