## technique vs. musical context

> Technique might best be thought of as a means to an end. ‘That is to say, strong technique is a prerequisite for jazz improvisa-
> tion; but, for the purposes of practicing, technique must always have
> a musical context. To get the point across Barry says, “practice your
> playing—not your practicing.”

**Significance (p. 10).** This is the foundational pedagogical rule of the entire method: technique is subordinate to musical context, setting the frame for every exercise that follows.

## re-creating rhythm section in practice

> A lot of practice time is spent
> alone, so it is essential to re-create the rhythm section for yourself as
> you play. Hear the drums, the bass and the chord changes. Practice
> in tempo, and envision how the rhythm section would accompany
> you.

**Significance (p. 10).** This is a concrete rule for solo practice that defines the expected mental environment for all exercises in the method.

## 'up and down' scale definition

> ‘Up and down’ refers to a scale played from its tonic up to its 7th
> degree (either dominant or major 7th), and then straight back down.
> This produces a 2 bar phrase.

**Significance (p. 11).** This defines the fundamental unit of scale practice used throughout the book, establishing the precise meaning of a term referenced in every subsequent scale exercise.

## three important arpeggios on dominant 7th

> The three important arpeggios on the dominant 7th scale are found
> on the tonic, the Sth and the 7th. Illustrated in its entirety is the arpeg-
> gio on the tonic (C). Practice the arpeggios on the 5th and 7th (Gm and
> Bb, shown in root position) the same way.

**Significance (p. 13).** This names a specific structural rule of the Harris method — that only three arpeggio positions on the dominant 7th scale are 'important' — which guides the student's priorities in practice.

## pivoting technique definition

> Pivoting is a multi-purpose technique that is an important tool for all
> instruments. It is especially useful for horn/string players and vocalists
> enabling them to perform a melodic line of any length and/or range
> while remaining within the boundaries of their instruments. Notice that
> the accentual structure of the line changes where the pivot creates new
> highest and lowest notes, which naturally feel rhythmically stronger
> within the melodic line.

**Significance (p. 13).** This defines 'pivoting' — a signature Harris technique — explaining both its mechanical function and its rhythmic consequence.

## Harris Half-Step Practice Model definition

> Half-steps typify the language of the bebop player. With this in mind,
> Barry developed the half-step practice model to teach the art of play-
> ing rhythmically. In their basic role, they appear as notes added to the
> descending form of the scale. Through various applications, (shown
> below), the half-steps become an endless source from which to gener-
> ate improvisational ideas. The dominant 7th scale, the major and the
> minor scales each have their own set of ‘rules’ for adding half-steps.

**Significance (p. 17).** This is the master definition of the method's central concept — the half-step practice model — establishing both its bebop lineage and its role as the generative engine of improvisation throughout the book.

## major scale half-step rules

> The first rule for descending from the 1-3-5 and major7 is: 1
> added half-step between the 6-5.
>
> The second rule applying to the 3-5 and major7 is: 3 added half-
> steps between the 3-2; 2-1; 6-5. Another way to practice this second
> tule is by placing the half-steps between the 3-2; major7-6; 6-5.
>
> The first rule for descending from the 2-4-6 is: no added half-
> steps. Play straight down the scale from the various starting notes.
>
> The second rule for the 2-4-6 is: 2 added half-steps which can
> come between the 2-1; 6-5 or between the major7-6; 6-5. (Practice both
> sets.)

**Significance (p. 23).** These four rules constitute the complete major-scale half-step system, parallel to the dominant 7th rules but with distinct placements that must be memorized separately.

## minor scale half-step rules

> The minor scale that the half-steps are added to is the melodic minor in
> ascending form-tonic - 2 - b3 - 4 - 5 - 6 - major7.
>
> The added half-steps for this scale fall in the same places as do
> the half-steps for the major scale. The one exception to this is where
> the major scale rules add a b3rd. Because this note is already present in
> the melodic minor scale, any other note may be used in its place.
> Notice that this may also be achieved by using interval jumps and by
>
> repeating the same note twice.

**Significance (p. 25).** This defines the specific minor scale form used in the Harris system (ascending melodic minor) and states the single exception rule that differentiates it from the major scale half-step application.

## finding related diminished chord

> The rule of thumb for finding a related diminished chord is to
> build it a major 3rd above the dominant note. If you are looking for the
> related diminished chord to a dominant 7th chord, just go up a major
> 3rd from the root of the dominant 7th chord

**Significance (p. 27).** This is the single most-used rule of thumb in the Harris method, providing the practical formula for locating the related diminished chord from any dominant 7th.
