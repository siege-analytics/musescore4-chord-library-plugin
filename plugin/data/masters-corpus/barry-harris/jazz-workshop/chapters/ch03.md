## Scale for Chording Concept

> Introduced
> here is a 'scale for chording,' and a method of
> assigning virtually any chord to one of two
> scales—the major 6 diminished and the minor 6
> diminished.

**Significance (p. 66).** This is the foundational methodological claim of the entire chapter, naming the two scales that organize all subsequent chord-movement exercises.

## Harmonic Rigidity Misconception

> The misconception that chords are fixed
> points in the tune, bound by vertical harmonic
> movement, may be the culprit behind the ten-
> dency to play the same two or three voicings
> over and over for the same change.

**Significance (p. 66).** Harris names the specific bad habit his method corrects, giving players a diagnostic frame for why movable chords matter.

## C6 Diminished Scale Construction

> This scale is actually comprised of all 3 diminished chords. The
> C and A belong to one diminished, the E and G to a second, and the B
> diminished chord provides the other four notes of the scale.

**Significance (p. 67).** Defines the internal architecture of the C6 diminished scale by mapping its eight notes onto three interlocking diminished chords.

## m7 and m7b5 Re-thinking

> Every minor 7 chord is an inversion of a major 6th chord, and every
> minor? flat5 chord is an inversion of a minor 6th chord.

**Significance (p. 69).** States the two axiomatic equivalences that allow Harris to collapse the m7 and m7b5 chord families into the 6th-chord scales, unifying the harmonic vocabulary.

## Moving Voicing Rule

> Remember to move each note to the next note of the scale.

**Significance (p. 71).** The single mechanical instruction that generates all movable chord progressions in this method.
