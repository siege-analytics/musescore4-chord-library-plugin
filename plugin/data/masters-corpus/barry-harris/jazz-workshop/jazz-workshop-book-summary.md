# Barry Harris's Jazz Workshop: Master Class in Harmonic Thinking and Scalewise Improvisation

Barry Harris's *Jazz Workshop* teaches a unified system where **technique always serves musical context** and every harmonic complexity reduces to scalewise rules and movable positions. The pedagogical core is encapsulated in his dictum "practice your playing—not your practicing," which orients every exercise toward re-creating ensemble sound and phrasing independent of the rhythm section. The book's architecture moves from foundational scales and arpeggios (Chapter 1) through solo construction methodology (Chapter 2), then generalizes that thinking to harmonic voicing systems (Chapters 3–4), and finally demonstrates how those systems apply to the vocalist's technical mastery (Chapter 5).

The book establishes **three load-bearing harmonic definitions** that function as universal calculation rules throughout: the **Related Diminished Chord** (a major 3rd above the dominant note), the **Important Minor** (the minor 7th chord on the 5th degree of a dominant 7th scale), and the **Minor 6 Diminished Scale** (a fusion of a minor 6 chord with its related diminished chord). These are not merely named chords but generators—each one unfolds into systematic pathways for melody, harmony, and voicing movement. The *Related Diminished Chord* in particular crystallizes one of Harris's signature discoveries: four dominants built from the same diminished triad become interchangeable, permitting the D7–Gmaj7 substitution where F#-diminished yields D7, F7, Ab7, and B7 as functionally equivalent harmonic colors.

Harris's **half-step practice model** is the generative engine of the book. Rooted in bebop language, it applies scale-degree-specific rules across dominant 7th, major, and minor (melodic) scales to create endless improvisational vocabulary without relying on rote memorization. The **Three-Step Solo Building** method (choose scales, generate phrases in all keys and tempos, transpose across tunes) transforms abstract theory into a reproducible practice routine. The characteristic mechanical directive—**remember to move each note to the next note of the scale**—converts harmonic theory into tactile execution for both pianists and guitarists.

Chapter 3 dismantles the **harmonic rigidity misconception** that chords are fixed vertical objects. Harris shows that virtually all chords collapse into one of two foundational scales: the **major 6 diminished** and **minor 6 diminished**. By recognizing that every m7 chord is an inversion of a major 6th and every m7b5 is an inversion of a minor 6th, players see chords not as isolated sonorities but as positions within a continuous scalar landscape. The book also establishes **named harmonic units** that become transportable across songs: the **major-minor-minor/6 voice-leading move** carries a specific bass-line formula; the *Stella by Starlight* progression generalizes into a Roman-numeral blueprint that applies wherever that progression appears.

The vocalizing chapter demonstrates that technical mastery of **scalar and rhythmical skills** is the liberation pathway to expressive freedom. The singer must develop **independent time and phrasing** separate from the rhythm section, and must master harmonic definitions like the **augmented arpeggio** and **whole tone scale** as functional tools. Throughout, Harris emphasizes **re-creating the rhythm section mentally during solo practice**. The book's thesis is that jazz improvisation is learnable through systematic application of three harmonic definitions, two foundational diminished scales, and one core directional rule.
