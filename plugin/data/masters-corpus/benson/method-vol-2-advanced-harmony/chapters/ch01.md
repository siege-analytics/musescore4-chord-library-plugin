## Harmonic Regions defined

> The Harmonic Regions de1ine and organize the roles of each chord with regard to the key
>     of a song, in addition to providing various replacement options for each of them. We have
>     3 harmonic regions: ("C" major as reference)
>
>
>     1- Root Region Chords (Tonic): Cmaj7 (I), Em7 (IIIm), Am7 (VIm).
>     2- Subdominant Region Chords: (this group in divided into two groups):
>     • Major: Fmaj7 (IV), Dm7 (IIm), Bbmaj7 (bVII).
>     • Minor: Fm7(6) (7+) (IVm), Bb7 (bVII), Abmaj7 (bVI), Dbmaj7 (bII).
>
>     3- Dominant Region Chords: G7 (V), Bø (VII).

**Significance (p. 1).** This passage is the foundational taxonomy of the entire chapter, naming all three harmonic regions and every chord that belongs to each, establishing the substitution logic that drives every subsequent section.

## Functions of Harmonic Regions

> • Root Region(Tonic):
>      It gives the sense of resolution, rest or "being home" (key).
>
>     • Subdominant Region:
>      It gives the sense of movement and distancing, moving away from the tonic.
>
>     • Dominant Region:
>     It gives the sense of tension and instability, and wants to resolve to the tonic. With these three
>     functions ( IV / V / I = distancing, tension and rest ), we generate what we call a cadence, which
>     is a chord progression with a resolution effect that defines the music's path.

**Significance (p. 1).** This passage defines the functional meaning of each harmonic region — tonic as rest, subdominant as distancing, dominant as tension — and introduces the term 'cadence' as the governing concept for all subsequent progression analysis.

## Lydian dominant rule

> Dominant 7th chords that are not the Vth degree or a tritone substitution, are always
> considered lydian dominant. In other words, we can use the following tensions on them:
>
> • 9, 13, or #11. Even though this is a rule, remember that it can be broken, mainly when
> we have modal harmonies.

**Significance (p. 2).** This is an explicit harmonic rule distinguishing how to tension non-functional dominant chords (lydian dominant: 9, 13, #11) from functional V-chord dominants, directly governing which extensions are safe to use.

## Harmonic Region substitution rule

> In theory, any chord of a given Harmonic Region may be replaced by another of the same region.
>     Hence we will find the most famous cadence in popular music: IIm / V/ I. But notice that we
>     had to replace the IVth degree by the IIm, which belongs to the same region.
>     It is important to understand that a cadence does not have to be completed all the time.
>     Sometimes we cannot play II / V/ I in the same bar, therefore we can simply play V/ I. Actually,
>     a subdominant chord is used to delay the Dominant chord, giving more colors to the harmony.

**Significance (p. 3).** This is the core substitution rule of the method — same-region chords are interchangeable — and explains why the IIm can replace IV, which is the theoretical basis for every II/V/I substitution chart that follows.

## Altered tensions in minor cadences

> An important detail to notice in the cadence II/V/I in minor keys (IVm/V7/Im) is that the
> dominant seventh chords almost always use altered tensions such as: b9, # 9, # 11, b13. The
> major 13th can be used with some regularity in the chords, but it is still an exception. In the key
> of Cm we can have the cadence: G7(b9)13 leading to Cm7 for example.

**Significance (p. 4).** This rule specifies that minor-key V7 chords characteristically take altered tensions (b9, #9, #11, b13), distinguishing their treatment from major-key dominant chords and guiding harmonic choices throughout the minor cadence exercises.

## Comping vs. improvising freedom

> Even though there are many possibilities, we can never forget that the most important thing
> when comping is to follow the melody, which will “tell” us what the best chord options are.
> However, when improvising we can be more free because we will create the melodies, so it
> means that even if we have a pre-set harmony dictated by the music, we can use all the “colors”
> we have learned before, playing single notes or even block chords.

**Significance (p. 4).** This passage states a foundational performance rule distinguishing the constraint on comping (melody governs chord choice) from the greater freedom available when soloing, which frames how all the voicing options in the chapter should actually be applied.

## Major/minor II/V/I similarity

> II degree = Dm7 is a dorian chord, which means that it could be Dm6 as well. Now comes the
> tricky thing: the third inversion of Dm6 is Bø which is the II degree from the Am cadence. So
> we conclude that both II degree are the same.

**Significance (p. 6).** This analytical passage proves degree-by-degree that the major and minor II/V/I cadences share the same underlying chord structures, providing the theoretical justification for treating them as interchangeable in substitution contexts.

## Secondary dominant defined

> As we know, dominant 7th chords resolve to their ‘I’ chord. However, in the diatonic chord
>     series, we have only one dominant chord, (G7) and it resolves to the tonic chord (C). So,
>     secondary dominants are dominant chords that resolve to the other harmonic >ield degrees.
>     They don't belong to the harmonic >ield and have nothing to do with it, except for the chord
>     that they were assigned to resolve to.

**Significance (p. 7).** This passage defines the secondary dominant concept precisely — a non-diatonic dominant whose sole relationship to the key is the degree it resolves to — establishing the term and logic used throughout the secondary dominant exercises.

## Subdominant delays dominant

> It is important to understand that subdominant chords are a way to delay the dominant
> chord, that is why subdominant chords and dominant chords are interchangeable.

**Significance (p. 8).** This concise rule explains the functional equivalence of subdominant and dominant chords in the II/V/I framework, directly authorising the many substitution swaps demonstrated in the exercises that follow.

## Dominant chord alteration rule for degrees

> Notice that, for a matter of organization and didatic, we apply dominant chords with altered
> notes only for minor degrees. But actually, we can use any alteration to any dominant chord
> leading to major or minor. However, we must make sure the altered notes we choose can be
> applied over the song we are playing, because the melody will always determine what we
> can and can not use. On the other hand, when we are improvising, we are much more free to
> add any chord substitution in order to create movement and tension to the solo.

**Significance (p. 10).** This passage corrects the pedagogical simplification used in the exercises and states the real rule: melody governs which alterations are permissible over any dominant chord, while improvising permits freer substitution choices.

## Tritone substitution defined

> We can replace a dominant 7th chord by another dominant 7th chord just by changing the
>      bass of the chord with its tritone. I.e. if the chord is G7, the replacement will be Db7. The
>      reason we can do this exchange is that both have the same tritone:

**Significance (p. 13).** This passage gives the precise definition and mechanical rule for tritone substitution — replace the root with its tritone interval — and provides the theoretical justification (shared tritone) for why the exchange works harmonically.

## Tritone sub voicing adjustment rule

> Notice that dominant 7th chords such as 7(b9), 7(#9), and 7(b13), can be considered as
> being the same, therefore they have the same function. Another important thing to notice
> is that depending on the dominant chord we apply the tritone substitution to, it may not
> sound correct or great. Example:
>
> • C7(9) using a tritone substitution would be Gb7(b13), which most of time does not work.
> So we replace the Gb7(b13) by Gb7(#11). However, in a cadence leading to Fmaj7 such
> || Gm7 | C7(9) | Fmaj7 || we should also change the voicing from the 1st chord as well.
> Therefore, we would have: || Gm7(11) | Gb7(#11) | Fmaj7 ||.

**Significance (p. 14).** This passage adds a critical practical rule to tritone substitution: when the substituted chord sounds wrong, adjust both the tritone chord's tension and the preceding II chord's voicing, illustrated with a concrete before/after example.
