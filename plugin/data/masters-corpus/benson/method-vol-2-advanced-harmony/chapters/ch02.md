## Diminished chord symmetry

> Diminished Chords have an interesting characteristic, they repeat themselves every minor
>      3rd. In other words, for every diminished chord, we will always have four options.
>      Example: "C˚" can be replaced by "Eb˚", "Gb˚" or "A˚", because they contain the same notes,
>      therefore are the same chord.

**Significance (p. 19).** This establishes the core symmetry property of diminished chords that makes all subsequent substitution examples possible.

## Fake Diminished chord definition

> 1) Fake Diminished Chord:
>      This type of diminished chord can be analyzed as an inverted dominant 7th (b9) chord.
>      Below we have as example an A7(b9) chord which has the intervals: Root, b7, b9, 3, from
>      low to high in the order they appear, followed by 4 diminished chords that are inversions
>      of it. But to better understand this relashionship, let’s consider each interval from each
>      diminished chord as belonging from A7. (Notice that the root note (A) was removed from
>      the inverted chords, because there is no need for it).

**Significance (p. 19).** Defines the "Fake Diminished" category by explaining its functional identity as an inverted dom7(b9), which is the theoretical basis for using diminished chords as dominant substitutes.

## Leading-tone VII7 substitution for V7

> In the old days, jazz musicians used to substitute the V, I progression (G7, C6) by VII7,
> I (B7, C6). The reason why we can do this is quite simple: all the notes of B7 can work as
> leading tones to the notes of C6, because they are half step from each other, creating a
> sensation of tension and resolution.

**Significance (p. 20).** Explains the voice-leading rationale for substituting VII7 for V7, which underpins both the diminished and tritone substitution techniques discussed throughout the chapter.

## Tritone substitution in minor II/V/I

> Note: Notice that in a minor II/V/I cadence such || Aø | D7(b9) | Gm7 || if we use a
>      tritone substitution over the dominant 7th chord, the voicings work perfect and we do not
>      have to change anything. Example: || Aø | Ab7(#11) | Gm7 ||. But, remember that
>      for the major II/V/I cadences, the chord tensions are optional.

**Significance (p. 17).** Gives a concrete rule distinguishing minor from major II/V/I behavior under tritone substitution, noting that voicings transfer intact in the minor case but tensions are optional in the major case.

## Perfect Authentic Cadence definition

> 1) Perfect Authentic Cadence:
> A perfect authentic cadence is made from a subdominant chord plus a dominant chord
> followed by the tonic chord (IV/V/I).

**Significance (p. 22).** Provides the precise functional definition (IV/V/I) that distinguishes perfect authentic from all other cadence types in the chapter's taxonomy.

## Imperfect Authentic Cadence definition

> An imperfect authentic cadence is made from a subdominant chord plus a dominant
> chord followed by the tonic chord (IV/V/I or V/I ), but at least one of them will be inverted
> or using a tritone substitution.

**Significance (p. 22).** Defines the distinguishing criterion — inversion or tritone substitution on at least one chord — that separates this cadence type from the perfect authentic cadence.

## Plagal Cadence definition

> A plagal cadence is made from a subdominant chord followed by a tonic chord (IV /I). This
>      kind of cadence can happen with both chords inverted, or just one of them.

**Significance (p. 23).** Concisely defines the plagal cadence by its IV/I motion and explicitly permits inversions on either or both chords.

## Deceptive Cadence definition

> A deceptive cadence is made from a subdominant chord plus a dominant chord followed
>      by any chord other than the expected tonic. This kind of cadence also can land on a chord
>      out of the harmonic Eield (IV/ V/ “X” )

**Significance (p. 23).** Defines the deceptive cadence and extends it to allow resolution outside the harmonic field entirely, which is a broader definition than the classical version.

## Half-Cadence definition

> 5) Half-Cadence:
>      The half cadence ends on the dominant chord (II /V) with no resolution.

**Significance (p. 23).** Completes the five-cadence taxonomy with the most open-ended type, defined strictly by the absence of tonic resolution.

## Tonal vs Modal Harmony rule

> The rule is quite simple: using
>      cadences = Tonal Harmony, no cadences = Modal Harmony. However, some songs that we
>      might consider modal are actually "hybrid" even without having cadences, because
>      somehow the sequence of chords surround a determined key. Therefore, we can conclude
>      that we have:
>
>      1) Tonal Harmony
>      2) Modal-Tonal Harmony
>      3) Modal Harmony

**Significance (p. 25).** Delivers the operational definition distinguishing tonal, modal-tonal hybrid, and pure modal harmony — the classification framework used throughout the chapter's repertoire examples.

## Modal Interchange definition

> Therefore, what we call modal interchange refers to borrowing chords from parallel modes
> without abandoning the established key of the song. This concept has been used since the
> Baroque era, and it is a tremendous asset for re-harmonizing songs, creating new “colors” to the
> harmony, making them non predictable and much more interesting than just using the basic
> chord sequences or progressions.

**Significance (p. 28).** Gives the definitive one-sentence definition of modal interchange and states its primary practical application — re-harmonization — which the exercises in the rest of the chapter implement.

## Parallel Modes definition

> Although it is related with a major
> tonality and a minor tonality sharing the same tonic (“C”major and “C” minor, for example),
> we can extend the concept by considering every mode sharing the the same tonic as a
> parallel mode. See the example below:
>
>
> • C (Ionian) → C (Aeolian) → C (Lydian) = Parallel Modes

**Significance (p. 28).** Expands the parallel-mode concept beyond the common major/minor pairing to include all modes sharing a tonic, which is the theoretical basis for the modal interchange chord table that follows.

## Harmonic region chord columns rule

> • Root region chords = I, III, VI
>
> • Dominant region chords = V, VII
>
> • Subdominant region chords = II, IV

**Significance (p. 28).** States the harmonic-region groupings that govern which columns of the modal interchange table are interchangeable, making this the operational key to all the chord-substitution exercises.

## Melodic Minor as dominant substitute

> Just like we have learned and applied, the chords from the dominant region can be replaced by
> the ones from the subdominant region (major or minor) and vice-versa. As we know, the minor
> subdominant region is based on a minor chord which can be considered melodic minor if we
> want. However, we should know that the chords derived from the melodic minor scale can
> replace the dominant region chords with the subdominant region chords (major or minor), it
> means that we can use the melodic minor chord family to replace the dominant region chords.
> However, the melodic minor V degree chord might not work sometimes. For example if we
> have a cadence Dm7,G7, Cmaj7 we could not use the C7(9/b13){V degree of F melodic minor}
> to replace G7, because it has the same root note as the key (Cmaj7), and the cadence would
> disappear: Dm7, C7(9/b13), Cmaj7.

**Significance (p. 40).** Introduces melodic minor chords as a new substitution resource for dominant chords and gives a concrete rule for when the V degree of the melodic minor scale is prohibited — a critical guard against obliterating the cadence.
