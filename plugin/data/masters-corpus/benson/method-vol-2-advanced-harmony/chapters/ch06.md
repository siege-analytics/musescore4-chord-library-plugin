## Quartal harmony definition

> Unlike tertian harmony, where the chords are built based on the superimposition of thirds,
>   the concept of quartal harmony or quartal chord voicing is the stacking of superimposed
>   4ths (perfect 4th, augmented 4th or diminished 4th). When we use this type of harmonic
>   structure, the chords end up becoming hybrids, because they no longer sound major
>   (happy), minor (sad) or even dominant (unstable), they just sound quartal, i.e. without a
>   deAined characteristic.

**Significance (p. 201).** This is the foundational definition of quartal harmony that the entire chapter builds on, distinguishing it from tertian harmony and explaining its ambiguous, 'hybrid' sonic quality.

## Quartal harmony historical context and key recordings

> Although this had already been explored by composers of musical impressionism such as
>   Debussy, Ravel and many others, in jazz the use of the quartal harmony became famous in
>   the hands of the brilliant pianist McCoy Tyner, who developed the quartal voicings in a
>   unique and wonderful way. We would suggest you listen to some speciAic albums where he
>   gives us a lesson on how to use this kind of chord structure.
>   a) A Love Supreme ( classic John Coltrane's recording )
>   b) Reaching Fourths (McCoy Tuner's album Reaching Fourths)
>   c) Impressions (McCoy Tuner's album Trident)
>   But previous to the recordings of Tyner and Coltrane trumpet player and composer Miles
>   Davis was a pioneer in the use of such harmony in jazz, when he launched, in August 1959,
>   the album "Kind of Blue", one of the most brilliant albums in jazz history. In the well
>   known composition "So What" by Miles Davis, we can hear very clearly the quartal
>   voicings played by pianist Bill Evans.

**Significance (p. 201).** Provides the canonical listening references that define the quartal harmony tradition in jazz, giving students essential aural models and historical lineage for the technique.

## Comping register rule for quartal chords

> When we are comping with the guitar, we most often use the chords starting from the 3rd,
> 4th and 5th strings, because the chords rooted from the 6th string might not work.
> Sometimes, even the chords starting from the 5th string might not work if used in low areas
> of the instrument. Also, sometimes the 4 note quartal chords must be replaced by the 3
> note quartal chords.
> However, it is always good to know all of them starting from all the strings, because they
> can help us to better visualize the guitar fretboard.

**Significance (p. 204).** Gives a practical register/voicing rule for comping with quartal chords on guitar, specifying which string sets work and when to substitute tetrads with triads.

## Minor blues scale quartal harmonization method

> Minor Blues Scale Quartal Voicings
> To harmonize the minor blues scale, we use the same quartal chord shape (1, 4, b7), where
> the scale notes will always be on the top voice of each chord. By doing this, we give
> strength to the chord melody, since they sound like "power chords" as they contain
> intervals of a 5th and 4th between each note.
>
> But using the same chord shape we are required to use some notes not belonging to the
> scale, but they do not cause any harmonic problem because they are just part of the chord
> shapes that we are using to harmonize them with.
