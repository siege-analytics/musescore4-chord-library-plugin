## quartal voicings over D half-diminished

> We can use 3 different quartal chord families over a"D" half diminished chord. Two of them
>   are derived from two different major scales: “Eb” major and “Db” major, and the last one is
>   derived from a melodic minor scale: F melodic minor.

**Significance (p. 275).** This establishes the foundational rule for choosing quartal chord families over a half-diminished chord, specifying the three source scales.

## Db major quartal as blues/tritone substitution

> The “Eb” major quartal chord family brings the locrian sound to Dø. However the "Db"
>   major quartal family is quite "tricky" because we transformed the Dø into D7(#9) chord,
>   giving it a blues vibe. Then using the tritone substitution for D7(#9), we can Aind Ab7(13),
>   that we can consider mixolydian. Therefore, having this mixolydian chord, we can Aind the
>   "Db" major scale quartal chord voicings.

**Significance (p. 275).** This explains the harmonic transformation chain—half-diminished to dominant 7(#9) to tritone sub—that justifies using Db major quartal voicings, a core analytical move of the method.

## lower note caution in quartal voicings

> Note: Be careful of the lower notes. They can make the chord sound undesirable.

**Significance (p. 275).** This is a direct rule of thumb about voice-leading and register when applying quartal voicings, alerting the player to a common pitfall.

## D half-diminished as VI of F melodic minor

> As we know the Dø chord is the VI degree of F melodic minor. Therefore, we can use the F
>   melodic minor quartal chord voicings over it.

**Significance (p. 277).** This defines the modal relationship that licenses use of F melodic minor quartal voicings over D half-diminished, a key conceptual link.

## non-family chord substitution for Fm(maj7)

> The first degree of "F" melodic minor quartal chord family [Fm(maj7) add4] sometimes can be
>   replaced by a chord not belonging to that family: D#/E, which is actually a F#7 (13) b9 over E.
>   This is not a rule, it is just a chord voicing that works well with the other voicings even though
>   it does not belong to this chord family.

**Significance (p. 277).** This gives an explicit exception to the chord-family system, showing that practical voice-leading can override theoretical family membership.

## 3-note voicing to fix low-register problems

> As a matter of fact, some chords on the lower strings might not sound desirable, in these cases
> we can take out the lower note and play a 3 note quartal voicing.

**Significance (p. 276).** This provides a concrete remediation technique—dropping the lowest note—for quartal voicings that sound muddy on lower strings.

## dorian quartal over static/modal interchange harmonies

> Usually the dorian chords are related to static harmonies. However, they can be a
>   tremendous asset when used over non traditional harmonic situations, giving a dorian
>   modal vibe to the chord.

**Significance (p. 279).** This defines the primary use-case for dorian quartal voicings and opens the door to their application in modal interchange contexts.

## lydian dominant (mixolydian #4) usage rule

> A dominant chord that is used incorrectly most of the time is the lydian dominant (also
>   called mixolydian #4). It can only be used over dominant 7th chords that are not leading to
>   any other chord. The only exception is when we have a sequence of many dominant 7th
>   chords one after another

**Significance (p. 279).** This states a strict prescriptive rule about when the lydian dominant voicing is and is not appropriate, directly governing its application in II/V/I contexts.

## mixolydian voicings: major vs. minor cadence rule

> On the Vth degree of a minor cadence, we can use all the possibilities we have learned for
>   dominant 7th chords, except with the mixolydian chord voicings, which can only be used
>   when they are leading to major chords.

**Significance (p. 279).** This is a critical restriction rule distinguishing which dominant voicing families are available over the V chord in minor vs. major cadences.

## chord ideas vs. groove ideas

> It is important to understand that we will be learning two different things at the same
> time: chord ideas and rhythmic ideas which we will call "groove ideas". Therefore we can
> use block chords applying another groove (rhythm) or use one of the groove ideas
> extracted from one of the examples but apply different chords.

**Significance (p. 290).** This defines the method's separation of harmonic content from rhythmic content, establishing that chords and grooves are independently interchangeable variables.

## improving rhythmic ideas: Big Band listening

> The best thing we can do is to listen to "Big Bands" as often as possible, because the
> "grooves" are very clear and easy to understand. However, in order to make them become
> part of us, we should memorize the songs we like, always singing with the recordings.

**Significance (p. 290).** This gives the method's prescribed practice technique for internalizing groove vocabulary: active listening and singing along with Big Band recordings.
