# Prescriptive lessons -- benson x method-vol-2-advanced-harmony

## maj7

Benson treats maj7 as the primary Rest/Tonic-region chord, freely interchangeable with Em7 and Am7 (IIIm and VIm). Melody is the hard constraint during comping -- it determines which of the nine available tension colours (9, 6, 6/9, #11, etc.) may be used at any moment -- while solo improvisation permits free choice among all colours. Guitar-specific architecture requires internalizing every voicing concept from both 5th-string and 6th-string root positions, and open-string maj7 shapes must be memorized as foundational vocabulary before advancing.

- [ch.1 p.1] Cmaj7, Em7, and Am7 all belong to the Root/Tonic region (rest) and are freely interchangeable as same-region substitutes.
- [ch.1 p.2] Full tension palette available over maj7: Cmaj7, Cmaj7(9), C6, C6(9), Cmaj7(#11), C6(#11), C6(9)#11, Cmaj7(6), Cmaj7(6)9 -- melody selects among them when comping.
- [ch.1 p.4] When comping, melody governs chord selection; when improvising, all learned colour extensions are freely available.
- [ch.1 p.11] All voicing concepts must be learned from both 5th-string and 6th-string root positions -- this is a universal positional requirement, not optional.
- [ch.2 p.29] At the I degree (maj7 tonic), modal interchange substitutions are restricted to chords from the 1st, 3rd, or 6th columns of the interchange table.
- [ch.2 p.33] In parallel-mode exercises, the first chord of the sequence must always come from the major key; subsequent chords may draw from the parallel minor.
- [ch.5 p.114] Open-string maj7 shapes are foundational vocabulary and must be memorized before advancing in Chapter 5.
- [ch.6 p.210] Over a Cmaj7 chord, use G major pentatonic quartal voicings, C major pentatonic quartal voicings, or a mixture -- F major pentatonic is excluded because it contains the avoid note F.
- [ch.6 p.263] For a lydian colour over Cmaj7, borrow quartal chords from B minor pentatonic or D major pentatonic via modal interchange.

## min7

Benson places min7 across all three harmonic regions: IIIm and VIm are Tonic-region members, IIm (Dm7) is the standard Subdominant-region representative that displaces IVmaj7 in the II/V/I, and Cm7 is the minor tonic. The melody-governs-voicing rule applies identically here -- the full tension palette (9, 11, 9/11, maj7/9, 6, 6/9) is available but melody-gated during comping. Quartal dorian voicings belong primarily to static-harmony contexts, and tritone substitution over the dominant requires adjusting the preceding IIm7 voicing as well.

- [ch.1 pp.1,4] Em7 (IIIm) and Am7 (VIm) are Tonic-region members; Dm7 (IIm) is Subdominant-region; Cm7 (Im) is minor tonic -- same-region interchangeability governs all substitutions.
- [ch.1 p.2] Full tension palette for min7: Cm7, Cm7(9), Cm7(11), Cm7(9)11, Cm(maj7)9, Cm6, Cm6(9) -- melody governs selection during comping.
- [ch.1 p.3] IIm7 replaces IVmaj7 as the Subdominant partner in the II/V/I because both belong to the same harmonic region -- this is the foundational rationale for the cadence itself.
- [ch.1 p.11] Minor 7th voicings must be internalized from both 5th-string and 6th-string root positions (illustrated in Cm and Gm).
- [ch.1 p.14] When applying a tritone substitution over the V chord (e.g., C7(9) to Gb7(#11)), the preceding IIm7 voicing must also be adjusted -- e.g., Gm7 becomes Gm7(11).
- [ch.2 p.29] At the II degree (IIm7), modal interchange substitutions are restricted to chords from the 2nd, 4th, 5th, or 7th columns of the interchange table.
- [ch.7 p.279] Dorian quartal voicings are primarily for static-harmony contexts but can be used over non-traditional harmonic situations to impart a dorian modal vibe.

## min-maj7

Benson treats min(maj7) primarily as a colouristic tension extension of the minor tonic rather than a standalone chord quality. Its main appearances are as Cm(maj7)9 within the min7 tension palette, and as a block-chord voicing that can be replaced by a non-family chord when voice-leading demands it.

- [ch.1 p.2] Cm(maj7)9 appears within the minor 7th tension palette -- in minor II/V/I contexts the tonic minor chord may be voiced as min(maj7) with added 9th.
- [ch.7 p.277] The Fm(maj7)add4 on the first degree of the F melodic minor quartal family over Dø may be replaced by D#/E (actually F#7(13)b9 over E) -- a non-family chord that works by voice-leading logic alone, not by rule.

## min7b5

Benson assigns the half-diminished chord to two harmonic regions: VII degree in the Dominant region (major keys) and II degree in the Subdominant region (minor keys). Its most important theoretical property is dual melodic-minor membership -- Dø functions as both the VI of F melodic minor and, via tritone reframing, as a D7(#9) whose tritone sub unlocks Db major quartal voicings. Block-chord application over Dø draws from three distinct quartal families, each with a different harmonic flavour.

- [ch.1 pp.1,4] Bø/Dø belongs to the Dominant region as VII degree in major keys; Dø is the II (Subdominant region) in minor keys.
- [ch.1 p.2] Tension palette for half-diminished: Dø, Dø(9), Dø(11), Dø(9)11, Dm7(b6) -- the Dm7(b6) variant underscores the locrian-major-2nd reading.
- [ch.1 p.6] The major IIm7 (Dm7/dorian) and minor IIø (Bø) are analytically the same chord: the third inversion of Dm6 is Bø, so both II degrees are interchangeable.
- [ch.3 p.47] In a minor II/V/I (Dø/G7/Cm7), treat Dø as the VI degree of F melodic minor (locrian major 2nd) -- this unlocks the entire F melodic minor chord family as a substitution resource over the IIø.
- [ch.6 p.244] When harmonizing over a half-diminished chord with minor blues scale quartal shapes (1, 4, b7), scale notes must always sit in the top voice to strengthen the chord melody; non-scale notes are permitted in inner voices.
- [ch.7 p.275] Three quartal families work over Dø: Eb major (locrian sound), Db major (D7(#9) blues reframe via tritone sub), and F melodic minor (Dø as VI degree) -- each yields a distinct colour.
- [ch.7 p.275] The Db major quartal family over Dø works by transforming the chord to D7(#9), finding its tritone sub Ab7(13) as mixolydian, then sourcing Db major scale quartal voicings -- a three-step derivation, not a direct scale match.

## dom7b9

Benson treats dom7(b9) as structurally identical to dom7(#9) and dom7(b13) -- all three are considered the same chord and carry the same harmonic function. In minor cadences, b9 is nearly obligatory; in major cadences it is one option among many. The dom7(b9) upper structure (b7, b9, 3 without root) is the direct source of the Fake Diminished chord.

- [ch.1 p.4] The V7 chord in a minor cadence almost always takes altered tensions -- b9, #9, #11, b13 -- with major 13th as an occasional exception.
- [ch.1 p.14] Dom7(b9), dom7(#9), and dom7(b13) are considered the same chord and share the same harmonic function.
- [ch.3 p.47] In a minor II/V/I (Dø/G7/Cm7), treat G7 as the VII degree of Ab melodic minor -- this unlocks the entire Ab melodic minor chord family as a substitution resource over the dominant.

## dom7alt

Benson derives dom7alt voicings by treating the chord as locrian (its arpeggio is half-diminished), which unlocks the major scale a half-step above as a quartal source. The minor pentatonic is explicitly prohibited over locrian quartal voicings because of the conflict with the perfect 5th; the minor blues scale with #4 emphasis is the prescribed remedy.

- [ch.6 p.254] Treat dom7alt as locrian to unlock the major scale a half-step above as a quartal voicing source -- G locrian unlocks all chords built from the Ab major scale.
- [ch.6 p.226] Do NOT use the minor pentatonic over locrian quartal voicings -- the perfect 5th conflicts with the mode. Use the minor blues scale with #4 emphasis instead.

## dom7

Benson's dom7 treatment is the most architecturally dense section of the book, governing tritone substitution, secondary dominants, fake/hybrid diminished substitutes, lydian dominant rules, quartal voicing restrictions, and the all-dominant turnaround. Three governing rules cut across all contexts: (1) melody governs alteration choice, (2) every voicing concept must be learned from both 5th- and 6th-string roots, and (3) the lydian dominant quartal voicing is banned over resolving dominants except in a pure all-dominant chain. The blues is identified as the structural model for the all-dominant approach.

- [ch.1 pp.1,8] Dom7 (V degree, G7) belongs to the Dominant region -- function is tension demanding resolution; dominant and subdominant region chords are interchangeable for delay and colour.
- [ch.1 p.2] Full tension palette for V7 leading to major: G7, G7(9), G7(b9), G7(#9), G7(13), G7(b13), G7(b9)b13, G7(#9)b13, G7(9)13, G7(b9)13, G7(#11), G7(b9)#11, Gsus9, Gsus13, Gsus(b9) -- melody determines selection.
- [ch.1 p.2] Any dom7 that is neither the V degree nor a tritone substitution is classified as lydian dominant and takes tensions 9, 13, or #11 only.
- [ch.1 p.7] Secondary dominants resolve to non-tonic scale degrees and have no relationship to the harmonic field except via the chord they are assigned to resolve to.
- [ch.1 p.10] Any alteration may be applied to any dominant chord leading to major or minor -- the earlier rule (altering only minor-resolving dominants) is a simplification. Melody always determines what can and cannot be used.
- [ch.1 p.11] All dom7 voicing concepts must be internalized from both 5th-string and 6th-string root positions.
- [ch.1 p.13] Tritone substitution replaces any dom7 with the dom7 a tritone away -- both chords share the same tritone and are functionally equivalent.
- [ch.1 p.14] When a tritone substitution sounds poor at its original tension (e.g., Gb7(b13) fails), adjust the tritone chord's extension and the preceding IIm7 together -- use Gb7(#11) and change Gm7 to Gm7(11).
- [ch.2 p.17] In a minor II/V/I, the tritone sub over the dominant (e.g., Ab7(#11) replacing D7(b9)) requires no voicing adjustment -- tensions work as-is, unlike the major II/V/I case.
- [ch.2 p.19] Fake Diminished: remove the root from dom7(b9) -- the remaining upper structure (b7, b9, 3) maps to four diminished inversions, each a valid V7 substitute.
- [ch.2 p.20] Hybrid Diminished: usable either as a connecting chord between chords or as a direct substitute for V7 in a cadence.
- [ch.2 p.20] VII7 substitutes for V7 on the basis of chromatic voice-leading -- all notes of B7 are a half-step from notes of C6, creating the same tension-resolution sensation as G7/C6.
- [ch.2 p.21] In a bass pedal context, replace V7 with a tonic-root diminished chord -- the bass stays on the tonic while the chord moves, yielding C° resolving to C6 instead of G7/C6.
- [ch.2 p.29] At the V degree, modal interchange substitutions are restricted to the 5th, 7th, 2nd, or 4th columns of the interchange table.
- [ch.2 p.40] Dom7 chords derived from a melodic minor scale can replace dominant-region chords -- but the V degree of that melodic minor cannot be used when it shares the root of the tonic, or the cadence dissolves.
- [ch.2 p.40] The melodic minor chord family is a valid resource for substituting dominant-region chords (both major and minor subdominant equivalents) -- the V-degree exception is the only prohibition.
- [ch.4 p.83] In blues and Brazilian music, convert every chord in a turnaround to dominant 7th -- each dom7 leads to the next until resolving to tonic.
- [ch.4 p.91] Blues, in its simple form, is harmonically based entirely on dominant 7th chords; the blues scale summarizes the entire harmonic content of the form.
- [ch.4 p.91] To internalize blues-form dom7 progressions, snap fingers on beats 2 and 4 and sing the scale degrees of each chord -- these forms must be known without thinking.
- [ch.6 p.204] Quartal dom7 voicings for comping start from the 3rd, 4th, or 5th strings -- 6th-string roots are often too muddy; even 5th-string roots fail in low positions.
- [ch.6 p.208] Learning all quartal tetrad voicings from every string is a fretboard-visualization exercise for improvisation and harmonization -- not an immediate comping deployment target.
- [ch.6 p.216] Modal quartal voicings are prescribed primarily for static-harmony contexts (single-chord songs like "So What" and "A Love Supreme") but each chord in a progression may also receive a separate modal quartal treatment.
- [ch.6 p.216] Bass-note selection determines modal identity over a fixed quartal voicing sequence -- the same chord sequence becomes Phrygian, Dorian, etc. depending solely on which bass note is sustained below it.
- [ch.6 p.217] When practicing quartal triads: never lose the guide chords from view; visualize each chord by its root note; lower-voiced chords may not work for comping; inverted quartal chords use simple letter-name symbols.
- [ch.6 p.227] Melodic pedal point for quartal voicings: hold the top voice fixed while lower voices shift -- the fixed-top pattern can be played over any mode generated by the source scale.
- [ch.6 p.259] Practice protocol: study quartal dom7 voicings in one key thoroughly before transposing; memorize only the voicing colours preferred rather than cataloguing all possibilities.
- [ch.7 p.275] When quartal tetrad voicings sound muddy on lower strings, remove the lowest note and play a 3-note quartal voicing instead.
- [ch.7 p.279] Lydian dominant (mixolydian #4) quartal voicings are banned over any dominant that resolves to another chord -- exception: use freely when every chord in the sequence is dominant one after another.
- [ch.7 p.279] Mixolydian quartal voicings on the V chord may only be used when resolving to a major chord -- they are prohibited over V chords leading to minor.
- [ch.7 p.290] Block chord examples must be memorized as coherent units, not assembled analytically in performance -- chord ideas and groove ideas are independently interchangeable variables.

## dim7

Benson organizes all diminished chords into exactly two functional categories -- Fake Diminished (inverted dom7(b9) standing in for V7) and Hybrid Diminished (connector or V7 substitute) -- and treats diminished symmetry as the primary resource for expanding substitution options. Both types are dominant-region substitutes; neither is treated as an independent harmonic colour.

- [ch.2 p.19] Diminished chords repeat every minor third -- C° = Eb° = Gb° = A° -- giving four equivalent substitution options for every diminished chord.
- [ch.2 p.19] Fake Diminished is the upper structure of dom7(b9) with root removed -- maps to four equivalent diminished voicings, each a usable V7 replacement.
- [ch.2 p.20] Hybrid Diminished serves two roles: connecting chord (passing between chords) or direct substitute for V7 in a cadence.
- [ch.2 pp.19--20] Both Fake and Hybrid Diminished belong to the Dominant region -- they are dominant substitutes, not independent harmonic colours.

## sus4

Benson treats sus4 voicings as members of the dominant tension palette rather than as a distinct chord quality. Sus variants (sus9, sus13, sus(b9)) appear alongside standard dom7 tensions for V7 leading to major; melody governs their selection during comping.

- [ch.1 p.2] Sus voicings -- Gsus9, Gsus13, Gsus(b9) -- appear in the V7 tension palette for dominant chords leading to major; melody determines which is usable when comping.
