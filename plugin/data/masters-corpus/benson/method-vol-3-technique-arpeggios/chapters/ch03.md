## Major/minor pentatonic chord identity

> The major pentatonic scale creates a major 6th chord (C, E, G, A), and the minor pentatonic
> scale creates a minor 7th chord (A, C, E, G). So, for now we have the chords: C6 and Cm7.
> However, if we change the key of the minor pentatonic from Cm to Am, we would have the
> same scale and the same chord, because C6 is an inverted Am7 (1st inversion). See below:

**Significance (p. 108).** Harmonic identity of pentatonics; C6 and Am7 are same notes.

## Relative major/minor pentatonic relationship

> With all that background, we could say that there is a relative major/minor relationship
> between both pentatonic scales:
>
> • C6       →     Am7 (relative minor chord of C6)
>
> • Am7 →           C6 (relative major chord of Am7)
>
> Although both pentatonic scales contain the same notes, it is extremely important to
> visualize them separately (major and minor), because we will use them to split the guitar
> fretboard into 5 zones. So depending where we are on the freatboard we will use one or the
> other.

**Significance (p. 108).** Core rule: visualize major and minor pentatonics separately to navigate 5-zone fretboard.

## 5-zone system introduction

> Just like we did with the Ionian and Aeolian scales, let’s use the 5 vertical zones for

> fretboard. However, some of these chords have been changed to better @it the scales
> presented. This is not a rule. We could use the same chords we learned before, because they
> are only to guide us into the 5 zones, nothing else.

**Significance (p. 110).** Guide chords are navigational aids only, not harmonic rules.

## Skipping zones to preserve hand connection

> If we are in the 1st zone going from low to high, and we want to move to another zone, we
> must skip the 2nd zone thus going to the 3rd zone. This might look difGicult, but actually it
> is much easier, otherwise the connection between both hands would be lost.

**Significance (p. 114).** Concrete rule: skip a zone to preserve hand sync.
