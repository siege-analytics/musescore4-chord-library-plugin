## The Secret of the Two Chords — premise

> The Secret of the Two Chords
>   As mentioned before, the natural major scale is one of the 5irst subjects we must study to
>   start improvising. It can summarize the whole harmonic 5ield into one scale. However, the
>   avoid note (perfect 4th) can create some melodic problems, especially over the root chord
>   and the relative minor chord (Cmaj7 and Am7 in the key of “C”).

**Significance (p. 269).** Sets the central problem the chapter solves: the natural major scale's avoid note is problematic over the I and VIm chords.

## Two chords focus — solving avoid note

> But for now, let’s concentrate on two chords: Cmaj7 and Am7, ignoring for a moment the
>   rest of the chords from the harmonic 5ield. In order to solve the avoid note issue, we will
>   not use it for the time being. Therefore, we will create melodies for Cmaj7 that can 5it over
>   Ionian or Lydian chords, and melodies for Am7 that can 5it over Dorian or Aeolian chords.

**Significance (p. 269).** Two-chord strategy yields melodies covering Ionian/Lydian (Cmaj7) and Dorian/Aeolian (Am7).

## Where the avoid note still works

> However this scale works perfectly when played over Dm7 (Dorian), Em7 (Phrygian), Fmaj7
>   (Lydian), G7 (mixolydian), Bø (locrian), even while using the avoid note. We must be
>   careful with our note choices.

**Significance (p. 269).** Justifies the two-chord narrowing — other harmonic-field chords tolerate the avoid note.

## Triads/tetrads needed beyond scales

> As mentioned before, it is not enough to only use the scales of the chords to create
>   melodies, we must add stronger melodic structures to them such as triads and tetrads .

**Significance (p. 269).** Scales alone are insufficient; stronger structures required for melodic substance.

## Major 7th and Minor 7th scale arpeggios

> Now that we have learned arpeggios and scales all over the guitar fretboard, it is time to
>   learn what we will call “Major 7th and Minor 7th Scale Arpeggios”. In the key of “C”, it
>   applies to Cmaj7 and Am7 descending chord arpeggios with added notes.

**Significance (p. 269).** Names the chapter's signature device: descending Cmaj7/Am7 arpeggios with added notes.

## Guide ideas from Benson solos

> For each fretboard zone we will have a “guide idea” starting from the highest note and
>   ending in the lowest note. All the ideas presented below were taken from George Benson
>   solos and organized in a didactic way.

**Significance (p. 269).** Provenance: zone-by-zone guide ideas transcribed from Benson's own solos.

## Superimposition definition

> The word superimpose means to join, add, propose an idea or concept based on another
>   existing idea. In music, it means using more than one chord simultaneously in a melodic or
>   harmonic form. Harmonically, it is the joining of two or more chords forming a single chord.
>   Melodically it is to play notes from two or more different chords over a determined chord.

**Significance (p. 275).** Foundational definition of superimposition (harmonic + melodic).

## Chord stacking on guitar vs piano

> If we play Gmaj7 (G, B, D, F# ) over the Cmaj7, we would have a “C “ Lydian sound
>   melodically, and a Lydian chord harmonically. However it is difHicult to play these
>   superimposed chords correctly on the guitar. The only instrument that can apply this
>   harmonic concept correctly is the piano. To achieve this on the guitar, we must arpeggiate
>   two chords, one after

**Significance (p. 275).** Pragmatic rule: superimposition is arpeggiated on guitar (chord stacking is piano-native).

## Stack with unrelated bass note

> We can also stack two or three chords and choose another bass note that does not have
> any connection to them.

**Significance (p. 275).** Bass note need not relate to the stacked chords.

## Ascending arpeggios define zones

> Notice that the ascending arpeggios will de;ine the fretbord zones, because the
>   descending arpeggios will almost always use two zones, because we play descending
>   arpeggios vertically.

**Significance (p. 275).** Zone identity set by ascending arpeggios; descending span two zones.

## Pattern caveat — predictability

> In the beginning we will learn some simple intervallic patterns that will help us get familiar
>   with the two scales we have just visualized all over the guitar neck. But DO NOT rely on
>   these. They are exercises and can begin to sound very predictable and extremely boring.

**Significance (p. 270).** Patterns are scaffolding; do not rely on them mechanically.

## Rhythmic displacement motif rule

> However, we can make them sound better when we use them applying some rhythmic
>   tricks. Next we have some rhythmic "motifs" forming what we call "rhythmic displacement".
>   Every intervallic pattern should be applied using them. In order to make some patterns
>   sound more musical, we can start every motif repeating the last note of the previous motif.

**Significance (p. 270).** Defines rhythmic displacement and motif-overlap trick that musicalizes mechanical patterns.

## Music as language — listen and copy

> Music is exactly like a language, if we want to learn it, we must know the words, the
> phrases, the intonations and so on. So, the best way to learn is to listen and copy from the
> greatest artists we love, until we develope our own way to communicate, combining
> everything we know.

**Significance (p. 300).** Overarching pedagogy: improvisation as language acquisition through imitation.

## Sing the solos

> It is important to understand that we must learn to sing melodies of songs and solos of
> our favorite artists. It does not matter what the style is.

**Significance (p. 300).** Singing-first applied to solo study.

## Purpose of the book — transcend transcription

> The purpose of this book is not just proving the importance of memorizing many licks. It
> is to show how to understand, organize and "transcend" what we transcribe =inding out
> the musical concepts behind them. For example, a simple "lick" can teach us a complete
> harmonic concept, allowing us to create countless new lines from it.

**Significance (p. 300).** Author's meta-goal: extract harmonic concepts from licks to generate new lines.
