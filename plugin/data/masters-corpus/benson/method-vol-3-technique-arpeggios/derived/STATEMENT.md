# Vol 3 Technique & Arpeggios — Statement of Outputs

## Overview

*The George Benson Method, Vol. 3 (Technique, Arpeggios & The Secret of the Two Chords)* is a comprehensive guitar method book that teaches George Benson's complete improvisational system across seven chapters, progressing from foundational physical mechanics through advanced harmonic superimposition. The book's pedagogical arc is deliberately sequential: it begins with picking technique and hand coordination (Chapter 1), moves through chord arpeggio organization (Chapter 2), pentatonic and blues scale systems (Chapters 3–4), lick vocabulary and blues application (Chapter 5), natural major scale application (Chapter 6), and culminates in the harmonic superimposition language that defines Benson's signature sound (Chapter 7). The book is explicit that it is one installment in a larger integrated curriculum, requiring parallel study of Volumes 1 and 2 for the rhythmic vocabulary essential to tasteful variation (Chapter 7 summary).

Throughout all seven chapters, a single master principle recurs: technique and musicality are inseparable. Fingering and picking choices are never arbitrary — they exist in service of hand coordination and musical internalization. Great improvisation is framed consistently as a language built from internalized sound, body memory, harmonic concept extraction, and deliberate multi-idea combination, never from mechanical pattern execution. The book's load-bearing terms — *digital memory*, *guide root*, *guide chord*, *lick*, *superimposition*, *rhythmic displacement* — are not decorative vocabulary but the operative concepts through which the system is taught and applied.

The book's scope encompasses four interlocking systems: a universal fretboard navigation framework (the five-zone system), an arpeggio construction and transposition system, a blues scale combination system governing how minor and major blues scales interact, and a superimposition and vocabulary system governing how the player builds and deploys improvisational language. These four systems together constitute the method's complete architecture, and the chapters are organized to introduce and deepen each in sequence.

## Systems

### Five-Zone Fretboard Navigation System

The Five-Zone Fretboard Navigation System is the book's central spatial framework for organizing the guitar neck. The fretboard is divided into five vertical zones, each anchored by a guide chord shape — either Cmaj7 (for Ionian-side zones) or Am7 (for Aeolian-side zones) — that serves as a navigational landmark rather than a harmonic instruction. The governing principle, stated explicitly in Chapter 6, is that the player *visualizes vertically but plays horizontally*: vertical zone structures orient position, but melodic lines should connect zones horizontally rather than remaining locked within a single zone. This system applies uniformly to pentatonic scales (Chapter 3 summary), blues scales (Chapter 4 summary), and natural major and minor scales (Chapter 6 summary), making it the book's universal fretboard-organization framework.

**Members:**
- Zone 1 — first vertical zone, anchored by a guide chord shape
- Zone 2 — second vertical zone; typically skipped when traversing from Zone 1
- Zone 3 — third vertical zone; default destination when skipping from Zone 1
- Zone 4 — fourth vertical zone
- Zone 5 — fifth vertical zone, completing full fretboard coverage
- Cmaj7 Guide Chord Shape — navigational anchor for Ionian-side zones
- Am7 Guide Chord Shape — navigational anchor for Aeolian-side zones

**Traversal Rules:**

- **Zone-Skip Rule** (`PositionContinuity`): When moving between zones, skip the immediately adjacent zone (e.g., from Zone 1 go to Zone 3) to preserve the connection between both hands. *"we must skip the 2nd zone thus going to the 3rd zone…otherwise the connection between both hands would be lost"* (Chapter 3 summary; p. 114).

- **1st/6th String Zone-Change Rule** (`StringSetTransition`): Zone transitions using alternate picking are cleanest when executed at the 1st or 6th string; other string boundaries introduce picking-direction hazards. *"pentatonic connections with alternate picking work perfectly when we use the 1st or 6th string to change from one zone to another"* (Chapter 3 summary; p. 114).

- **4-1-4-1 Zone-Transition Fingering** (`_pending:zone-transition-fingering`): When crossing zone boundaries, use the non-standard 4-1 finger alternation rather than conventional sequential fingering. *"when we change from one zone to another we have a non common type of fingering (4, 1, 4, 1, 4, 1 and 4, 1)"* (Chapter 3 summary; p. 118).

- **String-Change Upstroke Rule** (`StringSetTransition`): Every time a string change occurs, the preceding stroke must be an upstroke to maintain picking-hand/fretting-hand synchronization. *"everytime we want to change from one string to another, we should play an up stroke"* (Chapter 1 summary; p. 3).

- **Guide-Chord Zone Anchoring** (`PositionContinuity`): Each zone is associated with a fixed chord shape (Cmaj7 or Am7) that anchors memory for that zone's root position; the chord root guides navigation, not harmony. *"Each zone will be related with a chord shape (Cmaj7 or Am7). The chords will guide us"* (Chapter 6 summary; p. 246); *"they are only to guide us into the 5 zones, nothing else"* (Chapter 3 summary; p. 110).

- **Horizontal-Over-Vertical Play Preference** (`PositionContinuity`): After using vertical zone visualization to orient position, melodic lines should connect zones horizontally. *"we visualize vertically but play horizontally most of the time"*; *"Usually, the vertical ideas do not work as well as the horizontal ideas"* (Chapter 6 summary; p. 246).

**Modification Rules:**

- **Two-Notes-Per-String Vertical Fingering** (`_pending:notes-per-string-constraint`): Pentatonic scale zones are fingered with exactly two notes per string to enable clean vertical playing within each zone. *"pentatonic scales work perfectly when played vertically because they can be played using two notes per string"* (Chapter 3 summary; p. 110).

- **Note-Count-Per-String Picking Direction Modifier** (`_pending:notes-per-string-constraint`): The number of notes played on each string determines the picking direction for that string; any change to fingering that alters this count requires a corresponding picking adjustment. *"the number of notes we play per string…can completely change the right hand picking, destroying the connection between both hands"* (Chapter 1 summary; p. 3).

### Chord Arpeggio Construction System

The Chord Arpeggio Construction System organizes all triad and tetrad arpeggios by which string carries the root note: the 5th-string root group and the 6th-string root group, each containing three distinct shapes. The system extends to inverted arpeggios, which are visualized relative to a guide root on either the 5th or 6th string depending on fretboard position. In Chapter 7, the system is further extended to the "Major 7th and Minor 7th Scale Arpeggios" — descending Cmaj7 and Am7 arpeggios with added color tones derived from transcribed Benson solos — as the primary device for navigating the avoid note (perfect 4th) over the I and VIm chords. A structural distinction governs zone behavior: ascending arpeggios define fretboard zones, while descending arpeggios characteristically span two zones (Chapter 7 summary).

**Members:**
- 5th-String Root Group — three shapes for triads and tetrads rooted on the 5th string
- 6th-String Root Group — three shapes for triads and tetrads rooted on the 6th string
- Triad Arpeggio — three-note arpeggio (root, 3rd, 5th) played melodically from root position
- Tetrad Arpeggio (7th Chord Arpeggio) — four-note arpeggio including the 7th
- Inverted Arpeggio — arpeggio whose bass note is not the root; visualized relative to a guide root
- Major 7th Scale Arpeggio (with Added Notes) — descending Cmaj7 arpeggio with color tones, used to avoid the perfect 4th over Imaj7
- Minor 7th Scale Arpeggio (with Added Notes) — descending Am7 arpeggio with color tones, used to avoid the flat 6th over VIm7

**Traversal Rules:**

- **Guide-Root Selection Rule** (`PositionContinuity`): Select the 5th-string or 6th-string guide root to determine which arpeggio visualization applies for a given fretboard position. *"The inverted arpeggios can be visualized many ways, depending on the guide root we"* (Chapter 2 summary; p. 80).

- **Ascending Arpeggio Zone Definition Rule** (`PositionContinuity`): Ascending arpeggios establish and define the current fretboard zone; descending arpeggios characteristically span two zones. *"ascending arpeggios will define the fretboard zones, because the descending arpeggios will almost always use two zones"* (Chapter 7 summary; p. 275).

**Modification Rules:**

- **Open-String First-Finger Transposition Rule** (`SubstitutionExpand`): When transposing an arpeggio shape out of open position, replace each open string with the first finger to preserve the shape's interval relationships. *"all the open strings…finger number [1 can replace them when transposing]"* (Chapter 2 summary; p. 47).

- **Added-Note Color Tone Rule (Scale Arpeggios)** (`ColorToneRequire`): Descending Cmaj7 and Am7 scale arpeggios are augmented with color tones derived from transcribed Benson solos, yielding lines that fit Ionian/Lydian and Dorian/Aeolian contexts while avoiding the perfect 4th. *"Cmaj7 and Am7 descending chord arpeggios with added notes"*; *"we will create melodies for Cmaj7 that can fit over Ionian or Lydian chords, and melodies for Am7 that can fit over Dorian or Aeolian chords"* (Chapter 7 summary; p. 269).

- **Guitar Arpeggiation Constraint for Superimposition** (`_pending:arpeggiation-constraint`): Because stacked chords cannot be voiced simultaneously on guitar, superimposed harmonies must be realized by arpeggiation of each chord in sequence. *"it is difficult to play these superimposed chords correctly on the guitar…we must arpeggiate two chords"* (Chapter 7 summary; p. 275).

- **Three-Finger Playing Preference (No Fourth Finger)** (`_pending:finger-avoidance`): Arpeggio and scale fingerings preferentially use three fingers, avoiding the fourth finger, following Benson's characteristic left-hand approach. *"he does not use the pinky or 4th finger very often, playing mostly with three fingers"* (Chapter 1 summary; p. 2).

### Blues Scale Combination System

The Blues Scale Combination System governs how the minor blues scale and major blues scale are combined within a key to produce authentic blues improvisation. The two scales are not interchangeable: the minor blues scale (minor pentatonic plus the blue note/♭5) can be used across all chords of a major or minor blues form as a single summarizing scale, but when the two scales are mixed, the player must follow harmony chord-by-chord rather than collapsing all changes into one scale. The major blues scale — derived from the 3rd degree of the minor blues scale — applies over major chords including dominant 7ths, but sounds most bluesy only in combination with the minor blues scale. Overreliance on either scale alone is explicitly named a failure mode: Chapter 4 calls it *lazy* improvisation. The same-key combination rule is restricted to major tonalities; in minor contexts, the minor blues scale in C pairs with the Eb major blues scale instead (Chapter 4 summary).

**Members:**
- Minor Blues Scale — minor pentatonic plus the blue note (♭5); usable across all chords of a major or minor blues form
- Major Blues Scale — same pitches as the minor blues scale starting from the 3rd degree; applies over major chords including dominant 7ths
- Eb Major Blues Scale (Minor Tonality Pairing) — in minor tonality, the minor blues scale in C pairs with this scale rather than the C major blues scale

**Traversal Rules:**

- **Chord-by-Chord Scale Following Rule** (`FamilyCoherence`): When mixing minor and major blues scales, do not summarize all chord changes into one scale; assign each scale to its appropriate chord or chord type individually. *"we cannot summarize all the chords into one scale anymore, we must follow the harmony"* (Chapter 4 summary; p. 151).

- **Two-Zone Minimum for Horizontal Lines** (`PositionContinuity`): Horizontal blues-scale melodic ideas must connect at least two zones to function properly. *"we must use at least two zones to make it work"* (Chapter 4 summary; p. 146).

**Modification Rules:**

- **Minor Blues over Dominant 7th — Major 3rd Caveat** (`ColorToneRequire`): When using the minor blues scale over a dominant 7th chord, the major 3rd must be played quickly in the phrase to avoid harmonic clash. *"Make sure to play the major 3rd quickly in the phrase, otherwise the melody can sound wrong"* (Chapter 4 summary; p. 141).

- **Blues Scale Combination Restricted to Major Tonality** (`FamilyCoherence`): The same-key minor+major blues combination rule applies only to major tonalities; in minor tonality, the minor blues in C pairs with the Eb major blues scale instead. *"Blues scales played in the same key work only for major tonalities and not for minor"* (Chapter 4 summary; p. 140).

- **Single-Scale Overreliance Prohibition** (`_pending:multi-idea-combination`): Relying on any single blues scale for the entirety of an improvisation produces lazy, stagnant lines; the system requires combination of multiple melodic ideas. *"Be careful not to rely on this technique. It will make you sound like a lazy improviser."*; *"the secret of a great improviser is to combine all the melodic ideas at once"* (Chapter 4 summary; pp. 140, 131).

### Superimposition and Improvisational Vocabulary System

The Superimposition and Improvisational Vocabulary System governs how the player builds, stores, and deploys improvisational language through a language-learning loop: listen to great soloists, sing their lines, transcribe them, then extract the underlying harmonic concepts to generate new lines rather than simply reproducing copied licks (Chapter 7 summary). The vocabulary units — *licks* — are treated as fixed melodic-plus-physical units: pitch sequence, fingering, and picking are inseparable, and each lick must be sung and aurally internalized before physical application (Chapter 5 summary). Superimposition is defined as the melodic or harmonic joining of two or more chords; on guitar this requires arpeggiation because simultaneous chord-stacking is unavailable (Chapter 7 summary). Rhythmic displacement — offsetting intervallic patterns with rhythmic motifs — prevents mechanical predictability when applying arpeggio-based patterns. The system explicitly requires parallel study of Volumes 1 and 2 for the rhythmic vocabulary needed to vary lines with taste (Chapter 7 summary).

**Members:**
- Lick (Pre-Set Melodic-Physical Unit) — a fixed small melodic idea with inseparable fingering and picking; the basic vocabulary unit of improvisation, learned by singing before playing
- Minor Blues Substitution Trick — context-specific substitution vocabulary required because minor blues mixes minor and dominant chord types that a single pentatonic does not cleanly summarize
- Descending Scale Arpeggio with Added Notes — zone-by-zone descending Cmaj7/Am7 arpeggios with color tones; the primary superimposition device for I and VIm chords
- Intervallic Pattern with Rhythmic Displacement — an arpeggio-based melodic pattern made musical by applying rhythmic motifs and motif-overlap (starting each motif by repeating the last note of the previous)
- Superimposed Chord Pair — two chords joined melodically or harmonically; realized on guitar through sequential arpeggiation

**Traversal Rules:**

- **Language-Learning Transcription Loop** (`_pending:language-learning-loop`): Move from listening to singing to transcribing to concept-extraction to new-line generation, cycling through solos of great artists to build vocabulary that transcends imitation. *"understand, organize and transcend what we transcribe finding out the musical concepts behind them"*; *"Music is exactly like a language…listen and copy from the greatest artists we love"* (Chapter 7 summary; p. 300).

- **Context-Driven Lick/Fingering Selection** (`PositionContinuity`): When applying a lick, choose fingering and picking options based on the surrounding fretboard position — where you are coming from or going to. *"You can choose one of the examples depending on where you are coming from or going to on the guitar fretboard"* (Chapter 5 summary; p. 184).

- **Minor Pentatonic Family Preference Rule** (`FamilyCoherence`): Lick lines built purely from minor pentatonic (without major pentatonic mixture) are preferentially deployed over chords belonging to the minor chord family, though the rule is not absolute. *"our preference is to use the lines over minor chords or chord sequences belonging to the minor chord family"*; *"This is not a rule. Melodic lines using only the minor pentatonic scale will often work perfectly over major chords"* (Chapter 5 summary; p. 210).

**Modification Rules:**

- **Singing-First Internalization Rule** (`_pending:ear-body-before-theory`): Every lick or melodic line must be sung and aurally internalized before physical application; ear and body memory precede theoretical understanding. *"learn to sing every melodic line (lick) that you know. Internalize the sound."* (Chapter 5 summary; p. 210); *"we must learn to sing melodies of songs and solos of our favorite artists"* (Chapter 7 summary; p. 300).

- **Memorize-Before-Theory Substitution Rule** (`SubstitutionExpand`): Minor blues substitution tricks must be memorized as vocabulary before their underlying theory is understood; pragmatic fluency precedes theoretical comprehension. *"it is not necessary to understand the theory behind it, just memorize them"*; *"the minor blues form has two different types of chords (minor and dominant), so we must learn some substitution tricks"* (Chapter 5 summary; p. 195).

- **Rhythmic Displacement Anti-Predictability Rule** (`TextureCycle`): Every intervallic arpeggio pattern must be offset with rhythmic motifs (rhythmic displacement) and motif-overlap to prevent mechanical, predictable-sounding lines. *"Every intervallic pattern should be applied using them…what we call rhythmic displacement"*; *"DO NOT rely on these. They are exercises and can begin to sound very predictable and extremely boring."* (Chapter 7 summary; p. 270).

- **Fixed Fingering-and-Picking Lick Unit Rule** (`_pending:fingering-picking-lock`): A lick's notated fingering and picking are inseparable from the lick itself and must be followed exactly; they are chosen for hand coordination, not arbitrarily. *"follow the given fingering and picking possibilities exactly as notated for each one of the examples"* (Chapter 5 summary; p. 210); *"Play the exact suggested fingering…They were chosen specifically to better help you master this technique"* (Chapter 1 summary; p. 2).

- **Pattern Note-Order Invariant** (`_pending:melodic-pattern-invariant`): Within any melodic pattern, the sequence of pitches is fixed; only the rhythm applied to that sequence may vary. *"the order of the notes is always the same, the only thing that changes is the rhythm, otherwise we would be changing the melodic pattern"* (Chapter 3 summary; p. 120).

- **Avoid-Note Omission over Imaj7 and VIm7** (`OmissionAllow`): The perfect 4th (over Ionian/Imaj7) and flat 6th (over Aeolian/VIm7) are omitted as default choices; they may be retained in exceptional musical contexts. *"Be careful with the avoid notes of both scales (perfect 4th in the Ionian mode, and flat 6th in the Aeolian mode)"*; *"there are always exceptions and at times the avoid note works fine depending on the situation"* (Chapter 6 summary; p. 244); *"the avoid note (perfect 4th) can create some melodic problems, especially over the root chord and the relative minor chord (Cmaj7 and Am7)"* (Chapter 7 summary; p. 269).

## Provenance Notes

**What is drawn from what.** All four systems are derived directly from the book-level distillation and the seven per-chapter summaries provided. No content has been invented outside those sources.

- **Chapter 1** (*Picking Technique*): Contributes foundational rules to both the Five-Zone Fretboard Navigation System (string-change upstroke rule, note-count picking direction) and the Chord Arpeggio Construction System (three-finger preference, fixed fingering/digital memory). Chapter 1 does not yield an independent system of its own; its content is the physical substrate that all other systems inherit.

- **Chapter 2** (*Simple Chord Arpeggios*): Is the primary source chapter for the Chord Arpeggio Construction System, contributing the root-string groupings, guide-root concept, open-string transposition rule, and inverted arpeggio visualization. Chapter 2 does not yield an independent system because its content is entirely subsumed within the arpeggio system.

- **Chapter 3** (*Pentatonic Scale*): Is the primary source chapter for the Five-Zone Fretboard Navigation System, contributing the zone-skip rule, 1st/6th string zone-change rule, 4-1-4-1 fingering rule, two-notes-per-string constraint, and pattern note-order invariant (shared with the vocabulary system). Chapter 3 does not yield an independent system; the pentatonic scale is a member context within the navigation system.

- **Chapter 4** (*Blues — A First Step Toward Improvisation*): Is the primary source chapter for the Blues Scale Combination System, contributing all three members, both traversal rules, and all three modification rules. Chapter 4 does not yield an independent system beyond the blues scale combination system.

- **Chapter 5** (*Improving Your Blues Ideas*): Is the primary source chapter for the Superimposition and Improvisational Vocabulary System, contributing the lick unit concept, context-driven lick selection, minor pentatonic family preference, singing-first internalization, memorize-before-theory substitution, and fixed fingering-picking lock rules. Chapter 5 does not yield an independent system; its content is the vocabulary-building half of the superimposition system.

- **Chapter 6** (*Natural Major Scale Application*): Reinforces and formalizes the Five-Zone Fretboard Navigation System for natural major and minor scales, contributing guide-chord zone anchoring and horizontal-over-vertical preference. It also contributes the avoid-note omission rule shared with the vocabulary system. Chapter 6 does not yield an independent system; the Ionian/Aeolian mother-scale content is presented as the full-scale application of the navigation framework already established.

- **Chapter 7** (*The Secret of the Two Chords*): Is the primary source chapter for the advanced content of both the Chord Arpeggio Construction System (ascending zone definition, descending two-zone span, color-tone addition, arpeggiation constraint) and the Superimposition and Improvisational Vocabulary System (language-learning loop, rhythmic displacement, avoid-note premise). Chapter 7 does not yield a fifth independent system; superimposition is treated as the culminating application of the arpeggio and vocabulary systems already introduced.

**Cross-system sharing.** Several rules appear in multiple systems because the book itself presents them as universally applicable: the horizontal-over-vertical preference and zone-anchoring rules appear in both the navigation system and the blues system; the fixed fingering rule appears in both the arpeggio system and the vocabulary system; the avoid-note omission rule is shared between the vocabulary system and the scale system formalized in Chapter 6.
