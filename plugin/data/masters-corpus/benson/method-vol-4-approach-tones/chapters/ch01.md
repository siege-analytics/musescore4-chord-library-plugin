## Target Tone definition

> We de%ine Target Tones as the main notes of a melody. In other words, the starting notes,
>     or ending notes. For example, the “C” major chord can have 3 target tones: C , E and G,
>     and any other note we consider as passing notes also called approach notes. However, in
>     addition to the chord tones, we can also set the chord tension notes from a determined
>     scale as target tones, even if they do not belong to the chord itself.

**Significance (p. 1).** Foundational definition of the chapter's central concept; establishes that target tones include both chord tones and scale tension notes.

## Target Tone rule: avoid notes excluded

> It is important to understand that we can only use chord tones or chord tension notes as
>     target tones, but not avoid notes. The only exception would be when the avoid note
>     becomes part of the chord. Therefore, let’s divide the target tones into two groups:
>
>
>     a) Chord Target Tones
>
>     b) Scale Target Tones (tension notes)

**Significance (p. 1).** Explicitly prohibits avoid notes as target tones (with one exception) and introduces the two-category taxonomy that organizes the rest of the chapter.

## Approach Tone types overview

> After understanding what a Target Tone is, let’s learn about what we call: Approaching
>     Tones (passing notes), which are notes that circle the target tones. They are essential to
>     get into the jazz improvisational world. We divide them into three different groups:
>
>
>     1) Scale Tone Approach (Diatonic Approach)
>
>     2 Chromatic Approach (simple, double and triple)
>
>     3) Enclosure Approach

**Significance (p. 1).** Lays out the complete three-part taxonomy of approach tones that structures the entire chapter — the organizational spine of all subsequent instruction.

## Scale Tone Approach definition

> This type of approaching tone is when you reach the target note by using scale notes
>     immediately before or after it. When used over the major scale, it is called Diatonic
>     Approach. However, as we are going to use other scales applying the same ideas, let’s call
>     it simply “Scale Tone Approach” (ST). There are four types of ST: simple, double, triple and
>     quadruple Scale Tone Approach.

**Significance (p. 3).** Defines Scale Tone Approach, introduces the preferred terminology over 'Diatonic Approach', and names the four sub-types that govern all subsequent scale-tone exercises.

## Ascending vs. Descending ST direction

> Notes used immediately before the target tone are called Ascending Scale Tone Approach,
>     and notes used immediately after are called Descending Scale Tone Approach.

**Significance (p. 3).** Establishes the directional terminology used to label every scale-tone example throughout the chapter.

## Chromatic Approach definition and sub-types

> The chromatic approach is one of the oldest and most used concepts in the history of
>     western music. This type of approach connects one note to another, using half steps
>     between them. We have four chromatic approach possibilities, and each one of them can
>     be ascending or descending.
>
>     1) Simple Chromatic
>
>     2) Double Chromatic
>
>     3) Triple Chromatic
>
>     4) Mixed Chromatic

**Significance (p. 5).** Defines the chromatic approach by its half-step mechanics and enumerates all four sub-types, providing the classification framework for all chromatic exercises.

## Chromatic approach: chord-dependent possibilities

> It is important to understand that depending on the chord, the possibilities of chromatic
>     approaches can be different, because the distance from one note to another in each type
>     of chord is different.

**Significance (p. 5).** Warns that chromatic approach options vary by chord type, directing the student to analyze interval distances before applying chromatic ideas.

## Chromatic approach caution rule

> Although it is one of the most used approaches in every style of improvisation,
>     chromatics must be studied very carefully because the "borderline" between building a
>     beautiful melody or a non-musical melody is very thin.

**Significance (p. 5).** The method's explicit quality-control warning that chromatic overuse risks non-musical results.
