## Dorian over II/V cadence

> Dorian single lines are one of the most important subjects for those who want to learn
> about improvisation, because they are related to the cadence II / V, and that is the reason
> why we will not differenciate between dorian and mixolydian single lines. This type of
> concept was very well developed in the bebop era by the great Charlie Parker, Dizzy
> Gillespie, Bud Powell and many others.

**Significance (p. 144).** Establishes the method's core premise: treating dorian and mixolydian as a unified melodic vocabulary over II–V, which governs the entire chapter's approach.

## Avoid note C over G7

> if we have the
> cadence Dm7 | G7 , we could play “D” dorian single lines over both chords. However, when
> we have a single dominant 7th chord (G7 for example) we must be careful with the avoid
> note "C", because it can make your melody sound weird or even incorrect. The only
> exception is when the chord is a Gsus, so the “C” note will work with this chord as a
> starting note or even an ending note. In other words, we dePinitely can use “D” dorian
> ideas over G7, but without forgeting to emphasize the “B” note instead the “C” note.

**Significance (p. 144).** Key practical rule: the 4th (C) is an avoid note over a standalone G7 but works freely over G7sus, and the 3rd (B) must be emphasized as the substitute melodic target.

## Dorian over non-diatonic minor chords

> We can use dorian ideas over any minor chord as a “possibility of color”. However, when
> we have minor chords that do not belong to the harmonic Pield, there is no other option
> than using dorian ideas, otherwise the melody will sound wrong.

**Significance (p. 144).** Distinguishes dorian as optional color over diatonic minor chords versus mandatory usage over non-diatonic minor chords.

## Dorian as static scale; chord superimposition

> dorian scale is a static scale, which means that it does not have any avoid note. Therefore,
> we can play all the tetrads and triads from the harmonic Pield over a dorian chord. See
> below:
>
> • Tetrads: Cmaj7, Dm7, Em7, Fmaj7, G7, Am7, Bø
> • Triads: C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am, B˚

**Significance (p. 144).** Defining dorian as a 'static scale' with no avoid notes is foundational; the listed tetrads and triads form the method's broad superimposition vocabulary.

## Major 7th as leading tone in dorian

> Another important thing that we must know, is that we can add the major 7th to the
> dorian scale, in order to make it more melodic. The reason we can do this, is because the
> major 7th works as a leading tone to the root of the scale, thus creating the illusion of a
> dominant 7th chord leading to a minor chord. So pay attention to the following single lines,
> because we will use this concept frequently.

**Significance (p. 144).** Adding the major 7th is the method's characteristic bebop-dorian move; framing it as a functional leading tone unlocks the major-7th line examples throughout the chapter.

## Combining Cmaj7 & Fmaj7 lines for D dorian

> by combining Cmaj7 lines with Fmaj7 lines, we can build a
>   beautiful D dorian line. So, it means that we already have thousands of dorian lines just
>   adapting the major 7th lines to minor chords.

**Significance (p. 145).** Generative rule for building dorian vocabulary: recycle existing major-7th arpeggio lines from the I and IV of the parent key rather than learning entirely new material.

## Modifying dorian lines over G7

> it is very important to remember that all the lines ending on the “C” note have
> to be modi>ied when played over G7 because the “C” note is an avoid note for that chord.

**Significance (p. 146).** Practical editing rule that ties the conceptual avoid-note warning to concrete melodic modification.

## Scales as fretboard guides, not melodies

> It is important to understand that we use scales only to guide us through the guitar
>   fretboard. When building melodic ideas, we should always mix scales, superimposed
>   arppegios and chromatic tones, otherwise the improvisation will sound predictable.

**Significance (p. 147).** Anti-scalar rule: scales are fretboard maps, not melodic content; the combination with arpeggios and chromaticism is what produces idiomatic bebop lines.

## Five zones: key-dependent ordering

> we must understand that the Bive chord shapes related with the Bive zones will
> appear in a different order depending on the key. For example, the chord shape
> representing the 1st zone in the key of "C", represents the 4th zone in the key of F. The Birst
> zone of a tonality will always be related to the closest chord shape from the Birst fret.

**Significance (p. 150).** Explains the method's zone-numbering system and its key-dependent ordering — essential for applying zone diagrams across tonal centers.

## Zones as guiding melodies

> The scale/arpeggios should be understood as guiding melodies to help us, and not pre-set
> melodies that cannot be changed. The most important thing is to know what we can play
> melodically in each one of the Bive vertical guitar fretboard zones.

**Significance (p. 150).** Distinguishes conceptual roadmaps from fixed licks, reinforcing improvisational flexibility over pattern memorization.

## Horizontal zone connections

> In addition to helping us build more colorful single lines, the idea to connect scale/
> arpeggios in different keys will help us start playing and thinking horizontally and not
> only vertically. The =irst step to understand this is to study the connections between
> neighboring zones (those that are close to each other).

**Significance (p. 151).** Introduces the chapter's structural goal of horizontal fretboard thinking, framing zone connections as the bridge between vertical scale shapes and fluid single-line improvisation.

## Dorian lines over all C major scale chords

> Although this chapter speci?ically explores the dorian world, it is very important to
> understand that all the melodic lines in this chapter can be used over any chord derived
> form “C” major scale: Dm7 (Dorian), Em7 or Esus(b9) (Phrygian), Fmaj7 (Lydian), G7
> (Mixolydian), Am7 (Aeolian), Bø (Locrian).

**Significance (p. 180).** Universalizing rule: all dorian exercises simultaneously constitute modal vocabulary for every chord in the parent major key.

## Two-mode practice method for patterns

> It is important to understand that we must
> study this in two different ways:
> 1) Play the exercises very slowly in order to better visualize them on the fretboard. Keep
> playing the same exercise in the same key until you feel comfortable with it.
> 2) Use the arpeggio sequence musically and not as an exercise. Therefore, we must combine
> them with other melodic ideas we have learned before, creating new single lines.

**Significance (p. 198).** Two-mode practice prescription — slow visualization then musical integration — is the method's explicit pedagogy for converting pattern exercises into improvisation vocabulary.

## Odd/even rhythmic displacement rule

> Remember that a fantastic device is to use odd rhythmic cells when we have even
> notes, and use even rhythmic cells when we have odd notes. By doing this we take out the
> “exercise feel”, making these arpeggio patterns sound more musical.

**Significance (p. 198).** Concrete, actionable technique for disguising the mechanical regularity of arpeggio patterns and making them sound like spontaneous improvisation.
