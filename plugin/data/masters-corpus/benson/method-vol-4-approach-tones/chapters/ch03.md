## Harmonic minor scale usage

> The most important characteristic from the harmonic minor scale is that it produces it's
>       own dominant chord creating tension and resolution at the same time. But although we
>       can use this scale in a static way over its chord family, we will use its melodies over
>       harmonic situations where we have tension & resolution.

**Significance (p. 235).** Central thesis of the chapter — harmonic minor is an active, cadential scale — which governs every melodic decision that follows.

## Minor 7th as added color tone

> A very important and useful device for the harmonic minor lines is that we can also use
>       the minor 7th, which is a note that is not derived from this scale but sounds great. The
>       reason we can use both 7ths is because the harmonic minor came from the aeolian scale
>       and both have the same harmonic function.

**Significance (p. 235).** Concrete rule and harmonic justification for adding the b7 to harmonic minor lines, expanding the usable note pool without abandoning scale function.

## Resolution note rule

> always
>       pay attention to the resolution note because we must always respect the ending chord,
>       which in this case is Cm7, a non "C" harmonic minor scale chord. Actually, most of the
>       time, the ending chord of a cadence V7/I will not be derived from the harmonic minor
>       scale.

**Significance (p. 235).** Foundational rule of cadential melodic writing: the resolution note must fit the tonic chord, even when the line is drawn from harmonic minor.

## Applicable cadences list

> We can apply any harmonic minor line over these chord sequences:
>
>   1) ||      G7        | Cm7 ||                              4) || Fm7            G7 | Cm7 ||
>   2) ||     Fm7 | Cm7 ||                                     5) || Abmaj7 G7 | Cm7 ||
>   3) || Abmaj7 | Cm7 ||                                      6) ||       Dø       G7 | Cm7 ||

**Significance (p. 235).** Maps the full range of harmonic contexts where C harmonic minor lines are valid — a practical substitution guide.

## Harmonic minor over major resolution

> Now that we know that the harmonic minor single lines can also 1it perfectly over cadences
> ending on major chords depending on the resolution note, let's see some examples below.
> We must understand that when we have a complete “C” major cadence such as Dm7 / G7 /
> Cmaj7, we cannot use “C” harmonic minor ideas over the Dm7, it would not work.

**Significance (p. 236).** Critical boundary condition: harmonic minor lines work over the V7 and I of a major cadence only when the resolution note is adapted, and are forbidden over the II chord.

## Ionian (#5) relative relationship

> In order to better visualize the Harmonic Minor Scale, we will split the guitar fretboard
> into 5 vertical zones based on guide chord shapes just like we did before with Ionian and
> Aeolian. However, to understand what type of guide chord shapes we should use, we must
> remember that every minor chord/scale has a relative major chord/scale or vice-versa. So
> the relative from the Harmonic Minor chord/scale is what we call "Ionian (#5).

**Significance (p. 238).** Defines the conceptual pairing that underpins the entire fretboard zone system: Ionian (#5) as the relative major of harmonic minor.

## Five vertical zone guide chords

> Guide Chords ⇒              Harmonic Minor & Ionian (#5)              = Cm (maj7) & Ebmaj7 (#5)
>
> 1st Zone: Cm(maj7)              2nd Zone: Eb+(maj7)                       3rd Zone: Cm(maj7)

**Significance (p. 238).** Master reference anchoring all five fretboard zones to their guide chord shapes — the visual backbone of the harmonic minor navigation system.

## Scale arpeggios construction rule

> Basically we will use the same scale arpeggios we learned for major 7th and minor 7th
>   chords, but in the major scale arpeggio we will replace the perfect 5th with the #5th, and
>   in the minor scale arpeggio we will replace the minor 7th with the major 7th.

**Significance (p. 243).** Precise technical rule for deriving harmonic minor scale arpeggios from previously learned shapes — a concise transformation instruction connecting old knowledge to new material.

## Diminished chord as rootless V7

> On the VII degree of the harmonic minor scale we have a diminished chord (in the case of
> “C” harmonic minor we have "B" diminished). As we know, every diminished chord is
> symmetrical, so B˚ will provide us three more equal chords: D˚, F˚ and Ab˚. However, all of
> them are what we call fake diminished chords, because they are G7(b9) [V7] without the
> root note.

**Significance (p. 246).** Defines the diminished chord's harmonic function as a rootless G7(b9) and explains the symmetry that makes four diminished labels interchangeable over the same dominant.

## Diminished-to-G7 note mapping

> a) B˚ : B, D, F, Ab ------- (major 3rd, perf 5th, minor 7th and =lat 9th of "G")
> b) D˚ : D, F, Ab, B ------- (perf 5th, minor 7th, =lat 9th and major 3rd of "G")
> c) F˚ : F, Ab, B, D (enharmonic) --- (minor 7th, =lat 9th, major 3rd and perf 5th of "G")
> d) Ab˚ : Ab, B, D, F (enharmonic) - (=lat 9th, major 3rd, perf 5th and minor 7th of "G")

**Significance (p. 246).** Table explicitly mapping each note of all four enharmonic diminished chords to their function within G7, making the substitution logic fully transparent and practical.

## Chromatic illusion technique

> Another device that can help us build more colorful melodic lines is to simply play
> chromatics between the arpeggiated notes derived from a determined scale. By doing this
> we create the illusion of playing more than one scale at the same time. (Note: Most often
> we will only use simple ascending chromatic approaches over the diminished arpeggios.
> Descending chromatic approaches work differently and we will cover them next.)

**Significance (p. 260).** Defines the chromatic illusion technique and immediately qualifies its directional application — ascending only over diminished arpeggios — establishing a rule for the examples that follow.

## Dorian vs. aeolian: static vs. active

> The main diference between these two scales is the 6th degree, which de=ines whether a
>   scale is static or active. For example, when we have a single line that uses a =lat 6th, we can
>   conclude the line was built from an active scale, and should be played when we have
>   harmonic motion (cadences). Let's use the key of "Cm" to better understand the idea. If we
>   have the cadence G7, Cm7, we cannot use the "A" note (major 6th) to build single lines,
>   because this note is derived from the dorian scale and cannot be played over any cadence.

**Significance (p. 261).** Central theoretical rule distinguishing static from active scales by their 6th degree, and explicitly forbids the natural 6 over V7–Im cadences.

## Augmented chord harmonic origin

> In addition to the diminished chords, the harmonic minor scale can give us an
>   augmemented chord which is also symmetric, giving us three equal augmented chords.
>   In the case of “C” harmonic minor we have: G+, B+, D#+ (or Eb+). See below the
>   relationship between these chords with G7:
>
>   a) G+: G, B, D# ------- (Root , major 3rd , augmented 5th of "G")
>   b) B+: B, D#, G ------- (major 3rd, augmented 5th, Root of "G")
>   c) D#+: Eb, G, B (enharmonic) ------- (minor 6th, Root, major 3rd of "G")

**Significance (p. 281).** Defines the augmented chord as another symmetrical structure derivable from harmonic minor and maps all three rotations to their G7 functions.

## Descending augmented: horizontal picking

> In order to keep the alternate picking when going from the higher notes to the lower notes,
>   the descending augmented chord shapes will be played horizontally, and for this reason we
>   will have only one shape possibility per string.
>
>   Of course we could use the sweep technique to give us more possibilities, but as
>   mentioned before, the George Benson picking technique works much better when we play
>   horizontal ideas (mainly when we have a melodic line going from the high to low).

**Significance (p. 284).** Technique rule specific to the method's alternate-picking philosophy: descending augmented arpeggios must be played horizontally to maintain the characteristic Benson picking pattern.

## Augmented chords as melodic cells

> Note: Remember that these augmented chords are not to be played consecutively, they are
>   just small melodic cells to help us build melodies using the harmonic minor scale.

**Significance (p. 284).** Usage rule that prevents the student from treating augmented arpeggios as independent harmonic material and clarifies their role as embedded fragments within harmonic minor single lines.

## Passing chord tonic-note rule

> We can use the triads and tetrads that contain the "C" note (Dø, Fm7, Abmaj7) only as
>       passing chords in a melody going from G7 leading to Cm7.

**Significance (p. 308).** Refines the superimposition rule by designating specific degrees — those containing the tonic — as passing material only, preventing melodic stagnation on the resolution note during the dominant.

## Diminished shape selection rule

> are just suggestions, there is no rule about what shape is the best, because it will depend
> on what comes next in terms of melody. Therefore, the best thing to do is to memorize many
> single line ideas using the diminished chord as demonstrated on the following pages.

**Significance (p. 248).** Method's guiding principle for diminished fingering: context-dependent selection over rigid rules, directing the student toward melodic memorization.

## Chord-family superimposition rule

> minor scale to create melodies over G7 leading to Cm7. However, be careful when using
>   the root of the Cm (maj7), because it can sound weird, making the whole line sound
>   incorrect. So the best thing to do it is to forget the root for now.

**Significance (p. 308).** Core rule for chord-family superimposition: use the harmonic minor chord family over the cadence, but avoid landing on the tonic root to preserve melodic tension.

## Degree substitution options

> • Second dregree = Dø: can be replaced by D˚
> • Fourth dregree = Fm7: can be replaced by F˚, or Fø
> • Fifth dregree = G7: can be replaced by G+7
> • Sixth dregree = Abmaj7: can be replaced by Abm(maj7) or Ab˚(maj7)

**Significance (p. 335).** Degree-by-degree arpeggio replacement options that allow avoidance of the problematic C note while maintaining harmonic minor color.
