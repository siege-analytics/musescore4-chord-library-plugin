# Prescriptive lessons -- benson x method-vol-4-approach-tones

## maj7

Benson treats maj7 chords in this volume primarily as donor material rather than as targets in their own right. Existing Cmaj7 and Fmaj7 arpeggio vocabulary is directly recycled as Dorian minor-chord lines, and the Eb+(maj7) guide chord anchors the harmonic minor fretboard map as a navigational shape rather than a melodic destination. The foundational avoid-note rule -- that only chord tones and tension notes may serve as target tones -- is first stated in the context of maj7 and governs all chord qualities throughout the book.

- [ch.1 p.1] Benson's master rule for all chord types: only chord tones or tension notes may function as target tones -- avoid notes are structurally excluded unless the chord has absorbed them. "The only exception would be when the avoid note becomes part of the chord."
- [ch.2 p.145] Cmaj7 and Fmaj7 arpeggio lines can be directly reused as D Dorian vocabulary. "By combining Cmaj7 lines with Fmaj7 lines, we can build a beautiful D dorian line... we already have thousands of dorian lines just adapting the major 7th lines to minor chords." Reviewer: confirm the Cmaj7/Fmaj7 pairing is stated exactly as written.
- [ch.3 p.238] Eb+(maj7) serves as the second of two guide chord shapes anchoring the five-zone harmonic minor fretboard system alongside Cm(maj7). The Ionian (#5) scale is identified as the parallel major-key resource for this system.

## min7

Benson's treatment of min7 is the most densely legislated section of the book. Dorian is the default scale -- it is "static" (no avoid notes), allowing all seven diatonic tetrads and triads of the parent key to be superimposed freely -- but the chapter adds a leading-tone option (major 7th) that creates dominant-resolution tension over a minor chord, and draws a firm line between optional Dorian usage over diatonic minor chords and mandatory usage over non-diatonic ones. Chapter 3 adds three proscriptive rules: harmonic minor lines are forbidden over IIm7 in a major cadence, the natural 6th is forbidden over V7--Im cadences, and harmonic minor arpeggio shapes require degree substitution (b7 replaced by maj7) to produce the correct harmonic minor sound.

- [ch.2 p.144] Dorian is a static scale with no avoid notes, so every tetrad and triad in the parent major key is available for superimposition over a Dorian chord. Benson lists all seven.
- [ch.2 p.144] The major 7th may be added to the Dorian scale as a leading tone. "The major 7th works as a leading tone to the root of the scale, thus creating the illusion of a dominant 7th chord leading to a minor chord." This device is the mechanism that makes maj7 arpeggio recycling (see maj7 section) musically coherent.
- [ch.2 p.144] Dorian is optional color over diatonic minor chords but mandatory over non-diatonic ones. "When we have minor chords that do not belong to the harmonic field, there is no other option than using dorian ideas, otherwise the melody will sound wrong."
- [ch.2 p.180] All Dorian-based melodic lines generalize to every mode of the parent scale: Dm7 (Dorian), Em7/Esus(b9) (Phrygian), Fmaj7 (Lydian), G7 (Mixolydian), Am7 (Aeolian), Bm7b5 (Locrian). Reviewer: check whether Benson states this as a blanket rule or hedges it.
- [ch.3 p.235] The minor 7th (b7) is permitted as a color tone in harmonic minor lines even though it is not a scale degree, inherited from Aeolian. "Both have the same harmonic function."
- [ch.3 p.236] Harmonic minor lines are explicitly forbidden over the IIm7 chord in a full major cadence (Dm7 / G7 / Cmaj7). "We cannot use C harmonic minor ideas over the Dm7, it would not work." Reviewer: confirm this is a flat prohibition, not a preference.
- [ch.3 p.243] Harmonic minor arpeggio shapes are derived from familiar major and minor 7th shapes by degree substitution: perfect 5th replaced by #5 in major arpeggios, minor 7th replaced by major 7th in minor arpeggios.
- [ch.3 p.261] The natural 6th (A natural in C minor) is forbidden in any line over a V7--Im cadence. "This note is derived from the dorian scale and cannot be played over any cadence." Hard rule, not a preference.

## dom7

Benson imposes the heaviest rule density on dom7. Three distinct proscriptions govern what notes to avoid (the 4th/C over G7, the tonic root of the destination chord, the natural 6th in minor cadences), and two positive tools -- diminished superimposition and augmented cells -- are each bounded by their own constraints. The overarching principle, stated in Chapter 2, is that scales are navigation maps only; authentic lines come from mixing scales, superimposed arpeggios, and chromatics.

- [ch.1 p.5] Chromatic approach tones require restraint regardless of chord quality. "The borderline between building a beautiful melody or a non-musical melody is very thin." This warning sets the ceiling for all chromatic usage throughout the book.
- [ch.2 p.144] The 4th (C) is an avoid note over standalone G7 and must not be emphasized. Benson instructs emphasis on B (the 3rd) instead. The sole exception: when the chord is Gsus, C functions as a valid starting or ending note. Reviewer: confirm the sus4 exception is stated at p.144 vs. elsewhere.
- [ch.2 p.146] Any Dorian line that ends on C must be edited before use over G7, because C is the avoid note for that chord. Practical editing rule, not just a theoretical caution.
- [ch.2 p.147] Scales are fretboard maps, not melodies. "When building melodic ideas, we should always mix scales, superimposed arpeggios and chromatic tones, otherwise the improvisation will sound predictable." Anti-scalar principle governing the entire dominant (and broader) vocabulary.
- [ch.3 p.235] Harmonic minor is reserved for dominant-function cadential contexts (tension and resolution). Six valid cadential patterns are listed, including bare G7 | Cm7, Fm7 G7 | Cm7, and Dm7b5 G7 | Cm7.
- [ch.3 p.246] The VII diminished chord (Bdim) and its three enharmonic equivalents (Ddim, Fdim, Abdim) are all valid superimpositions over G7 because each is a rootless G7(b9). All four are interchangeable as melodic material over the dominant.
- [ch.3 p.284] Augmented chord shapes are melodic cells embedded in lines, not consecutive harmonic sounds. "These augmented chords are not to be played consecutively." Hard prohibition on stacking augmented arpeggios.
- [ch.3 p.308] Landing on the tonic root (C) when superimposing harmonic minor chord-family material over G7 must be avoided. "The best thing to do is to forget the root for now." Reviewer: confirm whether this is restricted to the tonic root specifically or to the full C triad.
- [ch.3 p.335] A degree-substitution table provides alternatives that avoid the tonic root: Dm7b5 can be replaced by Ddim; Fm7 can be replaced by Fdim or Fm7b5; G7 can be replaced by G+7; Abmaj7 can be replaced by Abm(maj7) or Abdim(maj7).

## dom7b9

The dom7b9 entries are closely related to the dom7 diminished-superimposition rules but carry additional procedural specifics. The key added constraints are that chromatic approaches over diminished arpeggios are ascending-only (at least as the primary case), and that shape selection among the four enharmonic diminished fingerings is context-driven rather than rule-driven.

- [ch.3 p.246] All four enharmonic diminished spellings over G7(b9) are interchangeable. Benson provides a table mapping each note of Bdim, Ddim, Fdim, and Abdim to its function within G7(b9). Reviewer: verify the table is present and complete.
- [ch.3 p.248] No fixed rule governs which of the four diminished shapes to choose; selection depends on what comes next melodically. "The best thing to do is to memorize many single line ideas using the diminished chord."
- [ch.3 p.260] Chromatic approach notes over diminished arpeggios are ascending only in the primary case. "Descending chromatic approaches work differently and we will cover them next." Reviewer: confirm whether descending chromatic over diminished is addressed later and, if so, where.

## sus4

Benson's treatment of sus4 is a single, precise exception carving out the 4th as a permissible structural note -- the only chord type where the avoid note of the adjacent dominant becomes a valid target tone.

- [ch.2 p.144] When the chord is Gsus (not G7), the C note is no longer an avoid note and may function as a starting or ending note. This is the explicit and sole exception to the rule forbidding C as a target tone over dominant-function G chords. Cross-ref: dom7/avoid-note-4th-over-standalone-dominant.

## min-maj7

Benson uses min-maj7 chords in two distinct roles in Chapter 3: as the primary guide chord shape that anchors the harmonic minor fretboard zones (Cm(maj7)), and as the tonic-resolution target that governs the selection of ending notes in cadential lines. Both roles are navigational and structural rather than melodic improvisation targets.

- [ch.3 p.238] Cm(maj7) is the first of two guide chord shapes (alongside Eb+(maj7)) that organize the five-zone harmonic minor fretboard system. Zones alternate between the two shapes: "1st Zone: Cm(maj7), 2nd Zone: Eb+(maj7), 3rd Zone: Cm(maj7)..."
- [ch.3 p.235] The ending note of any harmonic minor cadential line must fit the destination chord. Benson notes that the tonic chord in a V7--I resolution is usually not derived from the harmonic minor scale -- it is typically Cm7, not Cm(maj7) -- so the resolution note must be selected to fit the actual landing chord. Reviewer: confirm whether Benson specifies which notes of the landing chord are valid resolution targets.

## aug7

Benson's aug7 entries are both technical and technique-specific. The three augmented rotations available from C harmonic minor are mapped explicitly to their intervallic functions within G7, and a picking-technique prescription -- horizontal alternate picking -- is attached specifically to descending augmented lines, named as the George Benson technique.

- [ch.3 p.281] The three augmented rotations derivable from C harmonic minor are mapped to G7 functions: G+ (root, major 3rd, aug 5th of G), B+ (major 3rd, aug 5th, root of G), D#+ (minor 6th, root, major 3rd of G). All three are usable as melodic cells over the dominant.
- [ch.3 p.284] Descending augmented arpeggio shapes must be played horizontally on the guitar, one shape per string. "The George Benson picking technique works much better when we play horizontal ideas." This is both a musical and a technical prescription.

## dim7

The dim7 entries largely overlap with dom7b9 in substance -- all four enharmonic spellings are rootless G7(b9) shapes, interchangeable as melodic material -- but add the ascending-only chromatic approach constraint and the context-dependent shape-selection rule as the key procedural details.

- [ch.3 p.246] All four enharmonic spellings of the diminished chord over G7(b9) (Bdim, Ddim, Fdim, Abdim) are interchangeable because each is a rootless V7(b9). Melodic choice among them is determined by what follows in the line, not by a fixed preference.
- [ch.3 p.260] Simple ascending chromatic approaches are the primary device when decorating diminished arpeggios. Descending chromatic treatment differs and is addressed separately in the book. Reviewer: flag if the descending treatment appears to have been extracted elsewhere in the usage notes.

## min7b5

Benson treats min7b5 chords (in the context of C harmonic minor superimposition over G7) as problematic because they contain the tonic C note. The two lessons are paired: the first restricts min7b5 to passing-chord status, the second provides the substitution that removes the problem.

- [ch.3 p.308] Dm7b5, Fm7, and Abmaj7 all contain the tonic C note and may be used only as passing chords when moving from G7 toward Cm7 -- they cannot be used as arrival or emphasis points during the dominant. Reviewer: confirm the three chords are listed together as a group at this page reference.
- [ch.3 p.335] Dm7b5 can be replaced by Ddim to sidestep the tonic-note problem. This substitution is part of the larger degree-substitution table (see dom7/degree-substitution-to-avoid-tonic-note). Cross-ref: min7b5/passing-chord-containing-tonic-note.
