## Melodic Minor: no avoid notes

> The single lines derived from the melodic minor scale tend to be the favorite of jazz and
> fusion musicians. As this scale has no "avoid" notes, the possibilities are in<initely larger
> than other scales that contain "avoid" notes.

**Significance (p. 1).** This foundational rule distinguishes the melodic minor scale from other scales and explains why it is favored for improvisation, establishing the core premise of the chapter.

## Scale application examples by chord

> 1) Over a Gsus(b9) we would visualize the single lines as being derived from “F” melodic
>        minor because the Gsus(b9) chord is the IInd degree of the F melodic minor scale.
>
>
>     2) Over a C#7alt we would visualize the single lines as being derived from “D” melodic
>        minor because the C# altered is the VIIth degree of the D melodic minor scale.
>
>
>     3) Over a C+maj7 we would visualize the single lines as being derived from “A” melodic
>        minor or “C” Ionian (#5) because it is the IIIrd degree (relative) of A melodic minor.

**Significance (p. 2).** These three worked examples demonstrate exactly how to identify which melodic minor scale to use over any given chord by locating the chord's degree within the scale family.

## Visualizing melodic minor: 5 vertical zones

> Just like we did with the harmonic minor scale visualization, we will also use the 5 vertical
>     zones to visualize the melodic minor scale over the guitar fretboard. As this scale is also
>     derived from the Aeolian scale, we can assume that it has a relative chord as well, which
>     will be the Lydian(#5), bIII of its chord family.

**Significance (p. 4).** This introduces the book's fretboard organization system — 5 vertical zones — and establishes the Lydian(#5)/Melodic Minor pairing as the conceptual anchor for visualizing the scale.

## Melodic minor construction rule

> The process to visualize the melodic minor scale is quite easy, because we only have to @lat
>     the 3rd of the Ionian scale or change the perfect 5th of the Aeolian scale into the @lated 5th.
>     Summarizing, we can use the same 5 vertical zones exactly as we did before with Ionian
>     and Aeolian but this time having Cmaj7(#5) and Am(maj7) as guiding chords.

**Significance (p. 4).** This gives two construction shortcuts for deriving the melodic minor scale from familiar scales (Ionian and Aeolian), and specifies the guide chords used to anchor each zone.

## Guide chords for vertical zones

> Guide Chords → Lydian (#5) & Melodic Minor = Cmaj7(#5) & Am(maj7)
>
>      1st Zone: Cmaj7(#5)                  2nd Zone: Am(maj7)                       3rd Zone: Cmaj7(#5)

**Significance (p. 4).** This explicitly names the two alternating guide chords that anchor each of the five fretboard zones, giving the player a concrete chord shape to visualize when locating the scale.

## Time signatures in patterns: usage rule

> The patterns and scale visualizations in this book may have different time signatures in order to
> help with the organization and visualization. Notice that depending on where and how we play
> them on the fretboard, we will have more or less possibilities. Don’t let the time signatures
> confuse you. Learn the ideas that you like the most and apply them where you feel they sound
> the best. Give more attention to the single lines shown.

**Significance (p. 1).** This is a meta-rule for how to approach all exercises in the book, instructing the student to prioritize single lines over time-signature groupings and to apply patterns by ear.
