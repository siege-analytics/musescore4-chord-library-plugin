# Prescriptive lessons -- benson x method-vol-5-melodic-minor

## maj7

Benson treats maj7(#5) -- the Lydian(#5) / Ionian(#5) voicing built on the raised fifth -- as one half of a two-anchor fretboard navigation system rather than as a standalone chord type. The Cmaj7(#5) guide chord alternates with Am(maj7) across all five vertical neck zones, so the player's maj7 knowledge is always harnessed to a spatial framework rather than used in isolation. No standalone improvisation prescription exists for a plain Cmaj7; every maj7 reference in the book ties back to the guide-chord role.

- [ch.5 p.15 / ch.7 p.33] Cmaj7(#5) anchors zones 1, 3, 5 (odd zones) in the five-zone fretboard system, alternating with Am(maj7) in zones 2 and 4. The player is told to internalize both shapes as paired visual anchors -- not as isolated voicings -- so that the full melodic minor palette is always spatially accessible: "Guide Chords -> Lydian (#5) & Melodic Minor = Cmaj7(#5) & Am(maj7). 1st Zone: Cmaj7(#5). 2nd Zone: Am(maj7). 3rd Zone: Cmaj7(#5)."


## aug7

Benson maps the augmented major seventh chord (C+maj7) to degree III of the parent melodic minor scale, making A melodic minor the improvisation source for C+maj7. The Lydian(#5) / melodic minor pairing is named as the characteristic coupling, meaning the player has two equivalent ways to name the same scale resource: from the aug-maj7 root (C Ionian(#5)) or from the melodic minor parent (A melodic minor).

- [ch.5 p.15] For C+maj7, derive single lines from A melodic minor or equivalently from C Ionian(#5), because C+maj7 is the IIIrd degree of A melodic minor. The two framings are interchangeable -- the player should know both for fluency but only needs to locate one on the neck to access the full scale.


## dom7

The dominant seventh is the primary laboratory chord for the entire volume, represented mainly by D7(9) and its variants D7(#11) and D7(#9). Benson's dominant-seventh pedagogy has three distinct layers: (1) using melodic minor Giant Lines to generate flux and reflux over a static D7 vamp, (2) a hard rule that extended scale options always require at least two guide chords for proper visualization, and (3) a special-case warning for D7(#11) whose dual identity as melodic minor IV and dominant diminished constrains which scales can safely be used over it. The tensions-known / selectively-voiced rule also originates here before being generalized.

- [ch.3 / ch.5 p.15 / ch.7 p.27] For D7(9) as the primary vamp chord, apply melodic minor Giant Lines to create "flux and reflux" -- extended lines run over the static dominant to generate harmonic tension and release. Fretboard visualization of extended options over this chord requires a minimum of two guide chords: "we must use at least 2 guide chords in order to properly visualize what we are doing."
- [ch.7 p.27] PROSCRIPTIVE -- do not attempt to visualize extended melodic minor scale options over any dominant seventh with only one guide chord. A single anchor is explicitly ruled insufficient whenever the scale palette goes beyond basic options. Single-guide-chord visualization is permitted for basic applications only.
- [ch.7 p.28] PROSCRIPTIVE -- for D7(#11) specifically, its dual identity as both melodic minor IV chord and dominant diminished voicing means certain scales and chords cannot safely be applied: "we must know that in some cases we cannot use certain types of scales and chords, otherwise we might have a terrible result." Standard degree-mapping alone is insufficient for this chord; the dual-identity constraint must be checked first.
- [ch.7 p.28] For D7(9,#11,13) and E7(9,b13) -- the melodic minor IV and V dominant chords -- know all tensions completely even when voicing only a subset in performance: "The chords D7(9,#11,13), E7(9,b13) do not have to contain all the tensions presented. However, we have to know all of them in order to do not make a mistake when harmonizing or improvising." Theoretical knowledge is total; performance voicing is selective.


## dom7alt

Benson treats the altered dominant as degree VII of the parent melodic minor scale, yielding a simple half-step-up rule: to improvise over any altered dominant, locate the melodic minor scale whose root sits a half step above the chord root and use that scale as the sole improvisation source. The degree-mapping procedure is the same algorithm used for all chord types in the book, applied here to the VII degree.

- [ch.5 p.15] For C#7alt, visualize single lines from D melodic minor -- a half step above the altered root -- because C#7alt is the VIIth degree of D melodic minor. The rule generalizes: any Xalt chord maps to the melodic minor a half step up from X.


## sus4

Benson maps the sus(b9) chord -- a Phrygian-flavored suspended dominant -- to degree II of the parent melodic minor, yielding a whole-step-down rule for the improvisation source. The degree-mapping procedure is applied to Gsus(b9) as a worked example showing the student how to navigate a non-obvious chord type by finding its parent scale position first.

- [ch.5 p.15] For Gsus(b9), derive single lines from F melodic minor -- a whole step below the sus root -- because Gsus(b9) is the IInd degree of F melodic minor. The rule generalizes: any Xsus(b9) chord maps to the melodic minor whose root is a whole step below X.


## min-maj7

Benson treats the minor-major seventh chord as the tonic anchor of the melodic minor system rather than as a common functional target. Am(maj7) is one of the two master guide chord shapes anchoring the five-zone fretboard system, and the full tension set for all chords in the family -- including this one -- must be known even when only a subset is voiced in practice.

- [ch.5 p.15 / ch.7 pp.28, 33] Am(maj7) anchors zones 2 and 4 (even zones) in the five-zone fretboard system, alternating with Cmaj7(#5). All melodic minor tetrads across the neck are related back to this shape. Additionally: "we have to know all of them in order to do not make a mistake when harmonizing or improvising" -- the tension-knowledge rule applies to this chord as to all members of the family.


## min7

Benson uses the minor seventh chord context primarily as a teaching key for melodic minor pattern acquisition. The student learns all melodic minor tetrads and Giant Lines in the key of Cm first, using the minor seventh shape as a visual anchor for the Eb/E substitution that distinguishes melodic minor from natural minor. The min7 chord then carries through in later chapters as the reference chord family for an extensive set of notated exercises.

- [ch.7 p.30 / ch.11 pp.60-90 / ch.13 pp.96-145] For Dm7 (and the Cm7 reference key used to teach it), learn all melodic minor tetrads by first anchoring in the key of Cm: "we will learn this new scale arpeggios in the key of 'Cm' thus helping us to visualize the 'Eb' note replacing 'E' note." The minor seventh chord shape is not itself the improvisation target -- it is the spatial anchor through which the Eb/E substitution becomes visible, giving the player a reliable fretboard entry point for any minor seventh context requiring melodic minor lines.


## min7b5

Benson treats the half-diminished (min7b5) chord as an organic member of the melodic minor chord family rather than as a special-case or problem chord. F#o (the sixth degree of A melodic minor) draws its improvisation lines from the same A melodic minor scale family that governs all other diatonic chords in that collection, with no separate procedure required.

- [ch.7 pp.28, 33] For F#o (half-diminished / min7b5 built on the sixth degree of A melodic minor), use A melodic minor scale lines -- the same source that governs Am(maj7), Bsus(b9), Cmaj7(#5), D7(9,#11,13), E7(9,b13), and G#7alt. The chord is listed without asterisk or caveat in the family table, indicating it carries no dual-identity constraint. Apply the standard five-zone guide-chord visualization system.
