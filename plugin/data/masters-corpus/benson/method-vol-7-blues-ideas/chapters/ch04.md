## Inverted/reverse hammer technique

> With the Medium Tempos we will be using all the time a very special and unique technique that
> we have learned previously called inverted hammer or also called reverse hammer. Also, in this
> type of tempo, we will learn how to breathe between the melodies thus giving the solo a
> different vibe and context.

**Significance (p. 15).** Names the method's signature inverted/reverse hammer technique and introduces 'breathing between melodies' as a distinct skill.

## Rhythmic Positioning defined

> This is the most difficult tempo to master and improvise because we will be using what we call
> "Rhythmic Positioning", which combines all types of time feel and rhythmic cells we have been
> working on.

**Significance (p. 15).** Formally defines 'Rhythmic Positioning' as the method's term for combining time feels and rhythmic cells.

## Pre-set chorus combination method

> • 1, 1, 1 (means that we are playing the complete chorus 1)
>     • 2, 2, 2 (means that we are playing the complete chorus 2)
>     • 3, 3, 3 (means that we are playing the complete chorus 3)
>     • 1, 2, 3 (means that we are combining the first part of chorus 1
>       with the second part of chorus 2 and the resolution of chorus 3)

**Significance (p. 16).** Gives the explicit notation system for mixing pre-set choruses, the method's core combinatorial framework.

## Scale visualization rule

> Notice that some scales below make part of the same mode. However, they must be visualized
> differently according to each situation that might appear. For example, when we talk about "G"
> Major Phrygian, of course we are talking about "C" Harmonic Minor Scale. When we are
> talking about "F" Lydian Dominant Scale, we are talking about "C" Melodic Minor Scale. But
> as we mentioned before, depending on the situation you must visualize one of them or maybe
> both at the same time.

**Significance (p. 17).** Establishes the rule that enharmonically equivalent scales must be visualized differently depending on harmonic context.

## Jazz pulse: beats 1 and 3

> Although some people think that the secret of jazz is the pulse 2 and 4, actually it is the opposite.
>     The real Jazz feeling has its pillar in 1 and 3. But what about the 2 and the 4? Guess what, the
>     stronger we feel the 1 and 3, the easier we can feel the pulse in 2 and 4.

**Significance (p. 18).** States a foundational counterintuitive rule about jazz feel that underpins all rhythmic positioning work.

## Dance of the Elephant body movement

> "the dance of the elephant" which is to move your body from one side to the other just like an
>     elephant walks.

**Significance (p. 18).** Introduces the method's named physical technique for internalizing pulse through body movement.

## Free Slow Blues Vibe rule

> Although we use the same groove feeling as in the slow blues vibe, in the free slow blues vibe
> you can release yourself from the common rhythmic cell and play more "flying in the air".

**Significance (p. 19).** Defines the distinction between Slow Blues Vibe and Free Slow Blues Vibe as one of rhythmic liberation.

## Studying each blues section separately

> It is extremely important to understand that we
> should not study the whole form at once. If we really want to master it, we must study each part
> and each possibility separately to make them part of ourselves.

**Significance (p. 21).** States the explicit pedagogical rule against whole-form study.

## Lines vs. harmonic concept

> However, after learning some of them,
> we should practice improvising not only using the line ideas we have memorized, but the
> harmonic concept on it. The lines are extremely important, no doubt, but the idea is not to
> become a "parrot" but a wise improviser that creates new combinations derived from a single
> line.

**Significance (p. 21).** Articulates that memorized lines are vehicles and internalizing the harmonic concept is the actual goal.
