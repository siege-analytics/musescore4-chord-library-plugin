## Bmaj7 replacing F7

> Bmaj7 is the resolution chord from the cadence C#m7, F#7 that can replace F7 leading to Bb7. Melodically speaking, Bmaj7 chord
>      must be treated as a Lydian chord, however we can also use hybrid ideas over it. Notice that the Bmaj7 Lydian scale contains the
>      altered tensions from F7 (#9, b9, #11, b13). Also it can give a special flavor to F7 because of the note Bb (A#), which is the major 7th
>      from "B" and the perfect 4th from "F".

**Significance (p. 60).** Defines both the harmonic logic and the modal/hybrid treatment for the Bmaj7 substitution over F7.

## Bm7 Dorian substitution for F7

> In this case Bm7 should be considered Dorian because it is a minor chord not derived from the chord family of the tonality. Actually,
>      Bm7 Dorian is a combination between "F" Dominant Diminished and "F" Altered Scale that can lead us to Bb7 (next chord of the
>      blues form).

**Significance (p. 62).** Scale rule for non-diatonic minor chord substitution.

## B° as C7 substitute leading to Fmaj7

> In this case we are replacing the Subdominant Region chord Bb7 by a diminished chord working as C7 (Dominant Region chord)
> leading to Fmaj7 (key). But let's understand this substitution step by step: B° is the same chord as F° which is actually the same chord
> as E7 with a different bass note. Therefore, we would have E7 leading to Fmaj7. Notice that all the notes from E7 (except the flat 7th)
> are half step from the notes of Fmaj7, which means that we would have many leading notes working together to reach the tonality (F).
> But we still have to analyze the flat 7th of "E". First of all, it is important to understand that a leading note sometimes can be a whole
> step, not exactly a half step as usual. Actually it is a process of understanding where a note wants to go. So, the "D" note (b7th of "E")
> can work as a leading note to "E" (major 7th of "F").

**Significance (p. 63).** Step-by-step explanation of diminished substitution and leading-tone principle.

## Superimposed IIm7/V7/I — V degree rule

> Although we can apply any leading dominant 7th scale over the V degree of a IIm7, V7, I
> cadence, when we superimpose them things are quite different because the V7 degree cannot
> have tensions anymore thus becoming part of the IIm7 degree dorian.
>
> In other words, in general the superimposed IIm7, V7, I cadences lose the V degree and stay
> only with the IIm7 and the Imaj7. However, remember that we can always use the relative
> chords from minor and major chords.

**Significance (p. 69).** Key rule on V-degree behavior under superimposition.

## G#m7 chain substitution over D7

> Above we have a cadence G#m7, C#7 replacing D7. But in this case we consider it as a simple G#m7 Dorian replacing D7. This chord
>      makes part of a chain of consecutive minor 7th chords (Bbm7, Am7, G#m7). We should play hybrid or Dorian ideas over each one of the
>      chain of chords. Although we can play G#m7 Dorian ideas over D7, we can also play G#m hybrid ideas over D7 because it will sound
>      like a combination between D Altered and D Dominant Diminished Scale. Apart from that, G#m7 lines can give a peculiar flavor to D7
>      because the 4th from "G#m7" (C#) is the major 7th of "D".

**Significance (p. 70).** Defines the chain-of-minor-7ths substitution concept with both Dorian and hybrid scale options.

## Ebm7/Ab7 cadence replacing D7

> Above we have the cadence Ebm7, Ab7 replacing D7. In this case we can simply use Ab7 Lydian Dominant or Ab7 Dominant
>      Diminished ideas because Ab7 is the Tritone Substitution from D7. However, we can also consider this cadence as being a single Ebm7
>      Dorian or Ebm7 hybrid, which brings to D7 an interesting flavor, mainly because of the note "Db" (flat 7th from "Eb" or major 7th
>      from "D"). Attention: Notice that we can also use ideas derived from Gbmaj7 which is the relative from Ebm7.

**Significance (p. 72).** Three scale approaches for Ebm7/Ab7 substitution with rationale.
