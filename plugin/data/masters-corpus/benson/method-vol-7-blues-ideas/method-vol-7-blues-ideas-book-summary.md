# Book-level distillation — method-vol-7-blues-ideas

_Aggregated from per-chapter summaries under `summaries/`._

This book — organized around what the author calls "Giant Lines" — is a systematic method for jazz improvisation taught by a master guitarist (Benson, as framed by co-author Peter Farrell) and structured around the blues form as the universal proving ground. The pedagogical architecture moves from static situations to active ones, from single-chord contexts to multi-chord substitution chains, and from inside vocabulary to outside reharmonization. The front matter (Chapters 1–3) consists entirely of introductions, biographical material, and dedications — no techniques appear there. The instructional content begins in earnest with Chapter 4 and builds through Chapter 12 in a carefully sequenced progression: static dominant-chord improvisation first, then deeper harmonic layering within those same contexts, then substitution theory applied to multiple chord qualities, and finally active (substitution-rich) lines over the full blues form.

## The Master's Organizing Framework: Harmonic Regions and Scale Visualization

The conceptual spine of the method is the doctrine of Harmonic Regions, introduced in Chapter 7 and elaborated across Chapters 8–12. Rather than treating chords as isolated events, the method groups them into regions — Major Subdominant, Minor Subdominant, and their relative/tritone-linked equivalents — and derives substitution options from those regional relationships. A chord does not stand alone; it belongs to a region, and any chord within that region may substitute for another. Chapter 8 demonstrates this with the Subdominant Minor Region, showing how Gbmaj7 and Emaj7 can replace Bb7 by tracing their derivation through the parallel minor (Fm) of the home key (F) and its relative major (Ab). Chapter 11 formalizes the Minor Subdominant Region move: instead of playing Bb7 directly, derive a substitution from Bbm's harmonic area, selecting Gbmaj7 → Ebm7 → Ab7 as a cadential replacement. This regional thinking — not chord-by-chord analysis — is the master's characteristic macro-move.

Paired with regional theory is the principle of scale visualization: the same scale or mode must be mentally pictured differently depending on harmonic context, even when its pitch content is enharmonically identical. Chapter 4 establishes this as foundational, and it recurs throughout later chapters as the basis for assigning Dorian to minor-7th replacements, Lydian to non-diatonic major substitutions, Lydian Dominant or Dominant Diminished to altered dominant contexts, and hybrid sounds when notes coincide with multiple altered tensions simultaneously. The method insists on context-specific scale identity rather than pattern-based fingering.

## Substitution Mechanics: The Leading-Tone Principle and Interchangeable Chord Tricks

The micro-level substitution logic throughout Chapters 8–12 is built on the leading-tone principle: individual notes pull toward resolution, and the method exploits that pull to license non-diatonic chords. Chapter 8 demonstrates how diminished chords function as V7 substitutes by their leading-tone content (F° = E7, creating leading tones to F7). Chapter 10 generalizes this into a chain principle — consecutive minor-7th chords (e.g., Bbm7, Am7, G#m7) can replace a single dominant target — with substitution success determined by whether shared leading tones and unique upper-structure notes bridge convincingly to the target region. Chapter 12 introduces the consecutive minor-7th chain again (Gm7–Abm7–Am7) as a structural device, and formalizes "color notes" as pitches that function simultaneously at two different interval roles (e.g., a note that is both the minor 3rd of one chord and the major 6th of another), making them particularly potent for hybrid sounds.

The concept of "Interchangeable Chord Tricks," introduced in Chapter 7, frames the practical application: within a given harmonic region, certain chords are functionally interchangeable, and the choice between them can depend on practical constraints such as tempo. The book states explicitly that the Fmaj7 substitution over a dominant vamp works better at faster tempos — one of the few tempo-conditioned rules in the method.

## Layering Vocabulary: From Blues Scale to Superimposition

Chapter 6 establishes the method's approach to deepening within a single harmonic context rather than rushing to new chords. Over D7(9), the book teaches three escalating layers: Blues Scale Ideas as the foundation, integration of Mixolydian and Lydian Dominant with blues vocabulary simultaneously, and finally superimposition of major-7th arpeggios over the dominant chord. This layering logic — Blues Scale → Mixolydian/Lydian Dominant hybrid → maj7 superimposition — recurs implicitly throughout the book as the template for any dominant-chord context. Chapter 9 extends superimposition to IIm7–V7–I cadences, establishing the rule that superimposed cadences typically shed the V degree, retaining only IIm7 and Imaj7, with relative chords available as additional color options. The aesthetic preference stated is that hybrid ideas sound better than strict modal applications — a recurring prescriptive judgment that frames the entire vocabulary-building enterprise.

## Rhythm, Tempo, and Physical Practice

Chapter 4 establishes that jazz pulse is anchored on beats 1 and 3 (not 2 and 4) — a foundational and unconventional prescription — and introduces "the dance of the elephant" as a physical body-movement technique for internalizing this pulse. The method's rhythmic term is "Rhythmic Positioning," defined as the combining of time feels and rhythmic cells. The book prescribes beginning new-concept practice at 110 bpm (slow-medium), then scaling to faster and slower speeds; fast playing is explicitly framed as a consequence of mastery, not a goal. The chapter also introduces the inverted/reverse hammer technique for medium-tempo work and the "Slow Blues Vibe" and "Free Slow Blues Vibe" as distinct tempo-feel categories, each with its own technical demands. Chapter 12 adds the phrasing prescription of resting during turnarounds to separate blues choruses and create breathing space between melodic "stories" — treating silence as structural punctuation rather than dead air.

## Scope and Methodology

The book's explicit methodology involves studying each blues section in isolation before assembling the full form, using pre-set chorus combination notation (e.g., "1,1,1" for full chorus; "1,2,3" for mixed sections) to organize practice sequences. Memorized lines are framed not as performance material but as vehicles for internalizing harmonic concepts — the line is a means, not an end. The book works systematically through chord qualities in sequence: D7(9) in Chapters 4 and 6, D7(#9) in Chapter 8, Minor 7th and Major 7th contexts in Chapter 9, Dm7 static ideas (inside and outside) in Chapter 10, and Dm7 active (substitution-rich) lines in Chapters 11 and 12. This sequencing — static before active, inside before outside, single-quality before multi-quality — is the pedagogical architecture. The book does not teach scales in the abstract; it teaches how scales, arpeggios, and substitution chains operate within specific harmonic regions of a specific form, making the blues not merely a practice vehicle but the structural argument for everything the method claims.
