## Outline No.1 simple form

> Here is the pattern in one of its simplest forms: beginning on the 3rd of the ii chord (G minor) down to
> the 3rd of the V7 chord (C7) and continuing up the arpeggio. The line comes to rest on the 3rd of the
> tonic chord.

**Significance (p. 18).** Defines the foundational mechanic.

## Borrowed parallel minor

> Note the use of D flat, the flat
> nine of the C7 chord. This note is "borrowed" from the parallel key of F minor (four flats).

**Significance (p. 20).** Borrowing from parallel minor.

## Iteration as rhythmic device

> With the outline in quarter note values, an easy way to get eighth note motion is to repeat notes,
> sometimes called iteration.

**Significance (p. 21).** Names iteration technique.

## Universal applicability

> These outlines will work with many other progressions. Anytime the root movement is down in fifths any
> of theses outlines will work, regardless of the quality of the chord.

**Significance (p. 29).** Generalizes applicability.

## C.E.S.H. with outlines

> Jerry
> Coker and others refer to this type of line as a chromatic elaboration of static harmony, or C.E.S.H.
> This type of chromatic elaboration is found with all three outlines.

**Significance (p. 39).** Names C.E.S.H. across all outlines.

## Chromatic leading tone reversal

> The chromatic altered tone stops the descent of the line; since it is a leading tone to the ii chord it pulls
> upwards. Often this momentarily changes the direction of the line, encircling the root of the ii chord
> before descending through the diatonic tones and finally resolving to the third of the V chord.

**Significance (p. 39).** Explains C.E.S.H. effect.

## Chromatic vertical vs linear

> At first glance, it may seem inexplicable that a C sharp and an E natural would sound good over a C
> minor 7. Viewing harmony as strictly vertical, these notes would not sound good; but when viewing them
> in a linear context we see and hear how these notes point to the target notes and make the line more
> interesting by creating and releasing tension.

**Significance (p. 38).** Central justification for chromaticism.

## All twelve pitches with clarity

> All twelve chromatic pitches are used in this example,
> yet it remains harmonically clear; it is not random chromaticism. Target notes occur in rhythmically
> significant spots, non-harmonic chromatic notes resolve when and where we expect them.

**Significance (p. 43).** Governing principle of chromatic usage.

## Octave displacement motivations

> Often to add interest, the improviser will place a pitch in another register than the listener expects. This
> may be the result of invention or necessity if the player is in the extreme ranges of the horn.

**Significance (p. 30).** Artistic and practical motivations.

## Ascending scale breaks for target

> Notice that the ascending scale breaks to avoid the upcoming target note. In ex.57, the
> scale that starts on beat three from C, goes to G, skips the A natural because it is the target note for the
> F7 chord.

**Significance (p. 32).** Subtle compositional rule.

## Stripping embellishments

> Up to now, we have seen how with additions the simple
> outline becomes more interesting. The opposite is true: taking away the insertions, ornaments, and
> embellishments also reveals interesting lines by the realignment of the original notes of the outline.

**Significance (p. 45).** Reduction is as revealing as elaboration.
