# Book-level distillation — connecting-chords-with-linear-harmony

_Aggregated from per-chapter summaries under `summaries/`._

## The Book's Pedagogical Architecture

This method book teaches jazz improvisation through a single, organizing conviction: that the improviser's primary obligation is *harmonic specificity* — the ability to make chords audible through melodic choices, independent of what the rhythm section provides. Chapter 1 establishes this as the dividing line between mastery and dependency. Three stances toward harmony are identified — specificity, generalization, and avoidance — and the book argues that even deliberate harmonic vagueness, in the hands of an expert, is an informed choice made by someone who knows precisely what they are omitting. The methodology is therefore descriptive: principles are extracted from transcriptions of great jazz artists, not invented. The analytic lens is consistently *linear* rather than vertical, because music is heard and conceived in time, and anticipation and suspension only make sense across the barline, not in a frozen vertical snapshot.

## Linear Harmony and the Guide-Tone Engine

The theoretical core of the book is *linear harmony*, introduced in Chapter 2. Chords are not vertical stacks but horizontal lines, and melody is chosen in relation to the bass line rather than root-position voicings. Within this framework, the **third** and the **seventh** of each chord are the load-bearing tones — called *guide tones* or *target tones* — that carry harmonic identity. The third provides harmonic specificity; the seventh is a restless *pointer* that resolves downward to the next chord's third, driving melodic momentum across the ii–V–I progression. This resolution mechanic — seventh of one chord descending to third of the next — is the engine of voice leading throughout the book. The root is generally omitted (the bass covers it), opening eleven pitch choices while maintaining harmonic clarity.

## The Three Outlines as Structural Vocabulary

Chapters 4 through 6 introduce the three foundational *outlines*: Outline No. 1 descends from the third of ii through the V7 and resolves to the tonic, the primary vehicle for C.E.S.H. (Chromatic Elaboration of Static Harmony) and borrowed tones from the parallel minor. Outline No. 2 arpeggiates the ii–V with diatonic passing tones, governed by the *punch line rule*: the target note is withheld until the V7 chord itself. Outline No. 3 features compound melody, particularly through the leap from the third to the flat-nine on the dominant, and shows special affinity for C.E.S.H. Chapter 7 demonstrates that *outline fragments* — partial versions omitting some essential chord tones — retain harmonic coherence as long as target tones are strategically placed and resolved. Outlines are treated as enabling skeletons that inspire rather than constrain.

## Embellishment Devices and the Chromatic Vocabulary

Chapter 3 provides the systematic embellishment vocabulary layered onto the outlines. Chromaticism is directional: flatted notes continue downward, sharped notes continue upward. Upper neighbor tones are diatonic; lower neighbor tones are chromatic. *Octave displacement* — particularly the 3rd-to-9th leap after the guide tone — is the signature dramatic device. C.E.S.H. becomes one of the book's most frequently invoked moves. Chapter 16's answer key codifies five specific soloing devices: inverted direction with pick-up notes, arpeggio displacement, stop-and-go rhythms, sawtooth shapes, and neighbor-tone surrounding.

## Modal Application and Practice Methodology

Chapter 8 extends the framework to modal contexts by demonstrating how bebop outline vocabulary is layered over modal harmonic foundations through leading-tone approach. Cannonball Adderley's use of C.E.S.H. Outline No. 3 on modal blues is the exemplary case. Chapter 9 establishes that deliberate, quality repetition at slow tempos must precede any elaboration, and that notation cannot convey what makes lines swing — listening and imitation are irreplaceable. Chapter 10 extends this into a generative practice loop: internalize outlines aurally, write down vocal improvisations, then perform with embellishment, targeting non-stop error-free modulation through all twelve keys. Chapter 11 reframes the book's endpoint as lifelong self-directed refinement built on the learning triangle of sound, fingering, and written notation.

## The Analytical Appendices as Evidence

The appendices (Chapters 12–14) complete the book's argument by showing the theory alive in recorded performance. Lou Donaldson's Birdland solo and Tom Harrell's transcribed solo demonstrate that jazz masters build coherence from exactly these predetermined outline structures. The etudes provide student-facing models for constructing complete solos within harmonic frameworks. The classroom appendix (Chapter 15) distills the book's philosophy into a single phrase: *limiting as control* — mastering constraint builds the disciplined technique from which eventual deviation and exploration become possible.
