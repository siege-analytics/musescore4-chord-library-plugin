# Chapter 1 — Introduction

_Distillation; backed by `chapters/ch01.md`._

This chapter establishes the core pedagogical goal of jazz improvisation: training students to play rhythmically coherent and harmonically specific lines, rather than depending on the rhythm section to provide harmonic grounding. The author identifies three primary approaches musicians take to harmony—harmonic specificity, harmonic generalization, or ignoring harmonic implications—and defines harmonic specificity as careful attention to chord implications, proper resolution of thirds and sevenths, and adherence to alterations called for by chord symbols. A key insight is that experienced improvisers who play deliberately vague do so knowing the harmonic implications and thus knowing what tones to avoid, inverting the common pedagogy that treats vagueness as ignorance. The methodology is descriptive rather than prescriptive, extracting principles from transcribed solos by outstanding jazz artists rather than inventing examples. The chapter introduces foundational terminology—harmonic rhythm (the pace of chord changes), consonance and dissonance redefined functionally as notes at rest versus notes that want to resolve, and the distinction between linear and vertical analysis—emphasizing that music must be studied in the linear manner it is heard and conceived, since anticipation and suspension explain seemingly senseless vertical alignments when viewed in the larger harmonic scheme.
