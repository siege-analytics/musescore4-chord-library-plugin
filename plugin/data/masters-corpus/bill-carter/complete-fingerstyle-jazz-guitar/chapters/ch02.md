## Thumb for lower strings

> Use the thumb (p) to play the lower three open strings, E, A and D.

**Significance (p. 20).** This is the foundational rule assigning the right-hand thumb to bass strings, which governs all subsequent two-part fingerstyle texture in the method.

## Stem direction and finger assignment

> In simple fingerstyle guitar notation, notes
> with down-pointing stems indicate the use of thumb, free stroke.

**Significance (p. 20).** This defines the notational convention used throughout the entire book to distinguish thumb (bass) from finger (melody) parts.

## Two-part finger/thumb coordination

> For the next exercise, play the top part (stems up) played /-m, rest stroke, and the bottom part
> (stems down) with p, free stroke. When alternating strokes between fingers and thumb, don't let your
> right hand be pulled out of alignment.

**Significance (p. 20).** This passage introduces the core right-hand coordination challenge of fingerstyle jazz -- maintaining hand alignment while alternating between rest-stroke fingers and free-stroke thumb.

## Fingering choice: fixed vs. mixed

> RH: Use rest stroke with /, m, or a for notes with stems pointing up and free stroke with p for
> notes with stems pointing down. Your fingering may emphasize assigning fingers to strings, one
> per string. Using a fixed pair such as i-m or m-a for the notes with stems pointing up would work
> also. So would mixing and matching.

**Significance (p. 20).** This gives the student explicit permission and guidance to choose among multiple valid fingering strategies, framing the decision as a deliberate choice rather than a fixed rule.

## Left-hand position rule

> Remember to keep the left thumb under the neck and fingers arched over the fretboard. Fretted
> notes allow you to have more control over duration, tone and expression.

**Significance (p. 21).** This states the canonical left-hand position rule and connects it to musical outcomes (duration, tone, expression), making it both a technique instruction and a motivating principle.

## Blues form overview

> Blues has a form. The most common form is twelve measures long with a standard harmonic
> structure (chords). In the following blues exercise, chord symbols are above the melody to indicate
> when the harmony changes.

**Significance (p. 22).** This is the method's first definition of blues form, establishing the twelve-bar structure and the relationship between melody and chord symbols before the student plays their first full blues.

## E minor pentatonic blues scale

> Here is the complete blues scale in an ascending and descending pattern. The scale begins
> and ends on E eight lines or spaces above. In this exercise, the scale returns to where it started.
> This distance is an octave. Memorize this scale. It’s called the E minor pentatonic blues scale.

**Significance (p. 24).** This is the chapter's central scale definition -- naming, describing, and commanding memorization of the E minor pentatonic blues scale, which underlies all the blues exercises that follow.

## Creating rests: fretted note release

> After you play a fretted note, stop it
> from ringing further by releasing the
> pressure of the active finger.

**Significance (p. 25).** This is the fundamental technique rule for executing rests on fretted notes, the simplest case that the method builds on before introducing open-string damping.

## Damping open strings with fingers

> If a sounding open string needs to stop at a specific moment,
> as when it is followed by a rest, damp it by touching it very lightly with a free finger. For now, start
> with any free right-hand finger.

**Significance (p. 26).** This introduces the open-string damping technique, a critical skill for rhythmic precision in fingerstyle guitar that differs fundamentally from stopping a fretted note.

## Alternating finger damping rule

> Practice damping the open string with the next finger in the normal alternation pattern. If
> you’ve just used /to pluck an open note, use mto damp the open note on the beat of the rest. Just
> pretend you are going to pluck the note again, swing into it lightly and just touch it.

**Significance (p. 26).** This gives a specific, actionable rule for which finger to use when damping, integrating damping seamlessly into the existing i-m alternation technique.

## Thumb damping

> The right thumb (p) will need damping skills too. Begin with using it both to play and damp
> notes. As before, the open string before a rest is the one that will need damping.

**Significance (p. 27).** This extends the damping concept to the thumb, completing the picture of right-hand muting across all fingers and establishing that both bass and treble voices require active silence.

## Tie symbol defined

> Atie symbol joins notes of the same
> pitch. The note on the left continues sounding for
> the duration of the note on the right. Do not pluck
> the second note. The combination of two tied
> quarter notes, below, sustains for a total of two
> beats. Notes can be tied within a measure or
> between measures.

**Significance (p. 29).** This is the method's formal definition of the tie, a notation concept essential for reading two-part fingerstyle arrangements where sustained inner voices must be distinguished from repeated attacks.

## Two-part notation: beat accounting

> When music is divided into two parts—melody and accompaniment—the number of beats
> per measure in each part must add up to the number of beats indicated in the time signature.

**Significance (p. 28).** This states the governing rule of two-part notation that the student must internalize before writing or reading any solo jazz guitar arrangement.

## i-m across all strings rule

> Having notes on lower strings does not by itself mean that they are automatically
> played with the thumb. If music has only one part (melody or accompaniment) paired /-m fingers
> will often play across all strings. If there are big jumps across strings, the thumb will enter to play.

**Significance (p. 30).** This corrects a likely student assumption by articulating the rule that string assignment -- not string position -- determines whether thumb or fingers play, a foundational principle for single-line jazz melodies.
