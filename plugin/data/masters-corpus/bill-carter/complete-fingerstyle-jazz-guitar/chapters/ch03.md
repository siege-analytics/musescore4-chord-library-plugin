## Metronome use and tempo range

> To work on rhythmics you will need something to use as a reference beat. Without a constant
> reminder, there is a tendency to speed up when playing is easy, and slow down when playing is
> difficult. If you don’t have one, now is the time to get a metronome (or electronic drum machine).
> For starters, set the metronome within the range of a comfortable pace between 60-100 beats
> (metronome ticks) per minute. You want accuracy, not speed. Listen to it; don't let it be just a
> background annoyance as though it were a television set in the next room.

**Significance (p. 34).** This gives the method explicit rule for metronome use, including the recommended tempo range and the warning against passive listening.

## Basic vs. specific rhythm, rest rule

> As you play the specific rhythm indicated, your metronome will provide the basic underlying
> pulse. The basic pulse is not notated in the exercises. If it helps, tap your foot. If tapping your foot
> is an additional burden, don’t do it. For preliminaries, you could clap your hands or sing (“do-bah-
> do-bah” or anything you like) with the basic beat of the metronome.
>
> As you play, use a fingered note for these exercises so that you can practice being exact about
> holding, damping and releasing notes. For an upcoming rest, be sure to release the previous note
> on the rest and not before or after.

**Significance (p. 34).** This defines the distinction between the notated specific rhythm and the unnotated underlying pulse, and gives the critical rule that rests must be honored precisely.

## Dotted note definition

> A dot after a note extends the note for precisely half as long as the note itself. In 4/4
> time a half note normally receives two beats. If it were dotted, it would receive three beats, as in the
> example below.
>
> The half note tied to a quarter note has the same duration as the dotted half note.

**Significance (p. 36).** This is the formal definition of the dotted note, including its equivalence to a tie.

## Dotted note vs. tie distinction

> Although dotted notes and ties are both used as note extenders, there is a difference. A dot
> after a note always extends the note by half the original value of the note. A note with a tie attached
> to the proceeding note on the right extends the original note by the value of the second note. When
> music is notated, the choice between using dotted notes over tied notes is determined by the ease
> of readability.

**Significance (p. 36).** This rule clarifies when to use a dot versus a tie and explains that readability drives the notation choice.

## Counting eighth notes convention

> In 4/4 time, or any meter in which a
> quarter note receives one beat (X/4), an
> eighth note or eighth rest receives a half
> beat. By convention, an eighth note falling
>
> ona beat is counted with 1, 2, 3 or 4, and an
>
> eighth note falling in between or offthe beat
> is counted with the word and.

**Significance (p. 40).** This establishes the fundamental counting convention for eighth notes -- on-beat numbers and off-beat and -- which underpins all jazz rhythm reading.

## Counting aloud technique

> To begin, count 1 and 2 and3 and 4 and through the entire exercise, making the eighth note
> the lowest common denominator. When you feel comfortable with eighth notes, you can skip
> subdividing with ands and count with numbers only.
>
> Try counting the eighth-note subdivisions aloud, especially in trouble spots. If you listen to
> yourself as you count, you will most likely spot any inaccuracies. For instance, if you listen and
> find that at a certain point you are changing the speed of your counting to match what you think
> the music is asking you to play, stop and check. The pace or tempo of the underlying basic beat
> should always be the same, of course. Pencil in the counting if necessary.

**Significance (p. 40).** This is the key self-monitoring technique: counting aloud with eighth subdivisions as a diagnostic tool to catch tempo-bending errors.

## Swing/shuffle definition

> Traditional blues and jazz put a spin on the interpretation of eighth notes. The notes are written
> as straightforward eighths:
>
> eT] WT.
>
> & 2 & 3 & 4 &
>
> But an authentic idiomatic interpretation would require eighth notes on 1, 2, 3 and 4 to be
> slightly longer in duration, and notes on and to be proportionally shorter. In early jazz, an accent
> was placed on 1 and 3. Later the place of emphasis changed, landing on 2 and 4. For now, stay
> with 1 and 3. This long-short feeling is called shuffle, or swing. It is also the feeling underlying the
> songs of rhythm and blues. The eighth-note feel will not show up in notes with a greater duration
> than eighth notes.

**Significance (p. 42).** This is the core definition of swing/shuffle feel and the rule that swing applies only to eighth notes, not longer values.

## Swing vocalization mnemonic

> You might vocalize the rhythm this way:
>
> Doo-ba/Doo-ba/Doo-ba/Doo-ba
>
> 1 &2 83 & 4 &
>
> Or even:
> Hump-ty/Dump-ty/Hump-ty/Dump-ty
>
> This shuffle rhythm works best for slower-to-medium blues and jazz. When the tempo is very
> fast, play eighth notes more evenly.

**Significance (p. 42).** This gives practical vocalization shortcuts for internalizing swing feel, plus the rule that fast tempos require more even eighth notes.
