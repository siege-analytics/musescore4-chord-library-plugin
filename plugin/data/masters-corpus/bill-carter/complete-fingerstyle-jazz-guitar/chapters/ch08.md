## Arpeggio definition

> The arpeggio (‘little harp”), as an accompanying technique, is a guitar “natural.” If you began
> playing fingerstyle guitar to accompany a singer—perhaps yourself—then you already have used
> arpeggios for accompanying. These fingerpicking patterns using the individual notes of chords
> are referred to as “broken chords.

**Significance (p. 86).** Formally defines the arpeggio technique and its alternate name broken chords, grounding the terminology for Part 5.

## Arpeggio rhythmic relationship

> Arpeggiation accompaniment works well as a faster flow of notes (eighth or sixteenth notes)
> accompanying a slower melody made up of whole, half, and quarter notes. There are no fixed
> rules, however.

**Significance (p. 86).** Gives the practical guideline for matching arpeggio rhythm to melody rhythm while warning against absolutes.

## Right-hand arpeggio fingering

> The right-hand fingering for the arpeggiation above is typical. When the arpeggio lies across
> several strings—as it does most often—p plays the lower string(s), while /and m play the middle
> and higher strings. Use a free stroke as you progress with i, mand a, from low strings to high, and
> you will not inadvertently damp the jower strings.
>
> Since p, i, and m are occupied playing the arpeggios, use the a finger, rest stroke, for the
> melody when needed.

**Significance (p. 87).** Core right-hand technique rule for fingerstyle arpeggio accompaniment, specifying finger assignments and stroke types.

## Left-hand sustain technique

> Sustain the arpeggio note group by holding the left-hand fingers in place.

**Significance (p. 87).** Essential left-hand rule that produces the characteristic harp-like resonance of arpeggiated accompaniment.

## Detached vs. sustained arpeggiation

> Arpeggios can be played in a detached, separated manner, with no notes melding together. On
> guitar, this usually happens more often in single note melody lines, not as an accompanying device.
> Therefore, you must use your taste and experience to decide when and where to sustain the broken
> chord and when to purposely separate the notes.

**Significance (p. 87).** Defines the alternate arpeggiation style and frames the choice between sustain and separation as musical judgment.

## Arpeggio range vs. melody rule

> watch that your choice of arpeggiation doesn’t reach higher than the
> melody, which might obscure the melody and confuse the listener.

**Significance (p. 88).** Critical voice-leading rule: accompaniment must stay below the melody to preserve melodic clarity.

## Passing tones to maintain direction

> If you set up an arpeggiated upward sweep from the sixth string toward the first as the primary
> pattern, keep it going as best you can. If you run out of room because the melody is slow, insert
> some passing tones between the existing lower notes to stretch it out rather than change the
> direction of the pattern.

**Significance (p. 88).** Specific problem-solving technique for preserving arpeggio pattern consistency when melody occupies too much range.

## Prioritize overall effect

> You may find it impossible to sustain every note of the arpeggio because of fingering problems,
> such as having to play two consecutive notes on one string. Go for the overall effect, and don’t
> get crazy over the shorter duration of a single note.

**Significance (p. 89).** Pragmatic technical rule: prioritize musical effect over mechanical perfection when fingering conflicts with sustain.

## When to abandon arpeggiation

> If you notice that the melody of the tune you wish to arrange continually leaps up and down
> wildly, abandon arpeggiation for that particular tune and choose another accompaniment
> technique.

**Significance (p. 90).** Clear decision rule for when arpeggio technique is inappropriate, teaching students to match technique to melodic character.

## Harmonizing with a third below

## Free stroke for thirds

> Usually the way to pluck a strong melody line is to use rest strokes as much as possible. Since
> thirds are on adjacent strings, the free stroke must be used. To make the top melody note louder,
> just do it. lsolate the highest string and tug at it more forcefully than the lower string of the chord.

**Significance (p. 91).** Explains the key technical constraint of playing parallel thirds on adjacent strings and the technique for projecting the melody.
