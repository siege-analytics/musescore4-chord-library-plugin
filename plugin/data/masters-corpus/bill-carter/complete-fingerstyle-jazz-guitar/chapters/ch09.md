## Chord tone harmony match

> When you are harmonizing a melody note with a note a third below, the lower note may or may
>
> not be part of the chord harmony indicated by the chord symbol above it. Chord tones contained
> in the melody match the chord perfectly, obviously.

## Seventh harmony third below

> When the melody note is the seventh of the chord, as the F (5) in the G7 chord in the second
> measure, the third below it would be the fifth of the chord.

**Significance (p. 93).** Gives a specific rule for one of the most common non-root melody-note cases, showing how thirds below sevenths land on chord tones.

## Non-chord tone decision rule

> One could argue
> that the B, as an eighth note, goes by so quickly that it does not jar the ear. Even melody notes
> are rarely all chord tones. In the big picture you need only to make the total effect sound like the
> chord indicated. Non-chord tones make for the interesting spice in an otherwise dull chord-tone-
> only dish. It’s your ear and your decision.

**Significance (p. 93).** Core aesthetic rule governing when to leave non-chord harmony tones in place, prioritizing overall effect over note-by-note correctness.

## Adjusting non-chord tones

> If you want to make the harmony for D nearby anda chord
> tone, use the perfect fifth of the G7 chord, A, four steps below.

**Significance (p. 93).** Teaches the corrective technique of dropping the interval from a third to a fourth to land on a chord tone.

## Bringing back the bass

> A melody harmonized only in thirds can progress by itself for a section of a tune, but probably
> not for the entire song. Put the bass back in. Even a simple root/root and root/fifth bass can turn
> the melody with thirds into a full three-part arrangement of melody, harmony and bass.

**Significance (p. 93).** Structural rule for when to introduce bass into a thirds-harmonized melody, specifying the minimum viable bass pattern for three-voice arrangement.

## Thirds exercise procedure

> Try putting thirds below each melody note. Follow the chord symbols to check for the correct
> third, and/or look at the TABLE OF THIRDS on the next page. Adjust according to your ear. Add a
> simple bass.

**Significance (p. 93).** Step-by-step procedural instruction encoding the complete harmonization workflow: thirds, chord-symbol check, ear adjustment, bass addition.

## Thirds as daily practice

> | encourage you to make scales in thirds as part of your daily practice routine. You want
> not only to write arrangements using thirds, but to play them with facility, too. To keep yourself from
> getting into a rut, make up little variations, such as playing the top note twice and the bottom note
> once, or staggering the notes, or changing the rhythm.

**Significance (p. 94).** Prescribes the practice method for internalizing thirds fluency, including specific variation strategies beyond rote repetition.

## Tenths definition

> An octave
> plus a major or minor third ten scale steps below (or above) the melody note is a tenth. Without
> specifically identifying them as such, you've already used them to mark major or minor sounds in
> recent parts of Section Two.

**Significance (p. 98).** Formally defines the tenth interval as the dropped-octave equivalent of a third, connecting it to prior usage.

## Parallel tenths limitation

> You probably would not use parallel tenths throughout the arrangement. For one thing, the
> melody would then have the same rhythm as the bass rhythm, as shown in the four measures
> above.

**Significance (p. 98).** States the key limitation of parallel tenths -- rhythmic lockstep with the melody -- establishing why they must be used selectively.

## Tenths as independent bass

> The tenth does often function as a bass note, but only infrequently does it move parallel
> to the melodic rhythm. More often it has its own independent rhythm.

**Significance (p. 98).** Defines the primary musical role of tenths in fingerstyle jazz: a bass-register color operating rhythmically independent from the melody.
