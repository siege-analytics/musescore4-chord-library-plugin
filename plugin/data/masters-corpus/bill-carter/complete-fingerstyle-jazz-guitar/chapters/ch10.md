## Walking tenths accompaniment

> While tenths are not often used to parallel a melody, they can be used to accompany another musician who is playing melody. When working with major and minor tenths, use the same principles to adjust the harmony as discussed in Section Two/Part 4, Marking Major and Minor.

**Significance (p. 100).** Establishes the primary use-case for tenths as accompaniment rather than parallel melody, cross-referencing the harmonization rules.

## Table of tenths practice

> Make tenths an ongoing project, as you did with scales in thirds. They proceed in the sharp direction.

**Significance (p. 101).** Gives the practice directive and structural rule: tenths cycle through keys in the circle-of-fifths sharp direction.

## Fingering pattern consistency

> By now you probably are getting familiar with the fingering patterns of scale tenths in sharp keys,
> so continue with the scales in flat keys. Fingering logistics are the same.

**Significance (p. 102).** States the pedagogical goal of automaticity through consistent fingering, the core rationale for the scale-table drills.

## Sixths defined relative to thirds

> Harmonizing a melody with a note six scale steps below works the same way as thirds and
> tenths. A sixth below a given scale note is the same chord tone as a minor or major third above
> the melody note.

**Significance (p. 104).** Conceptual anchor unifying sixths with the existing thirds/tenths framework, explaining why the same harmonic logic applies.

## Sixth as chord tone analysis

> If the melody note is the third of the chord, as is the first note E (1) of the first measure, the note
> six scale steps below is the fifth of the C chord.

**Significance (p. 104).** Opens a measure-by-measure harmonic analysis teaching how each sixth interval relates to the underlying chord.

## Sixth below root

> When the melody note is the root of the chord, as is the second note of the first measure, C
> (2), the sixth below is the third of the Am chord.

**Significance (p. 104).** Teaches a specific interval relationship: melody root produces a sixth that lands on the third of the chord.

## Adjusting sixth for chord clarity

> If the melody is the fifth of the chord, as A (3) in the case of the first note of the second measure,
> the sixth below will be the minor seventh interval C, of the Dm7 chord. The chord symbol above
> itis a simple triad, Dm. The minor seventh interval is contained in the C scale, so it is not a clash.
> You can change the C to D if you wish to keep the sound to a simple Dm chord.

**Significance (p. 104).** Teaches the decision-point rule: when a scale-sixth creates an extension the chord symbol does not show, the player can accept or adjust.

## Ninth extension from sixth

> When the melody note is the seventh of a dominant seventh chord, as is the third note in the
> second measure, the sixth below, D (5), is the ninth of the G7 chord indicated above.

**Significance (p. 104).** Identifies a specific case where the scale-sixth produces a chord extension (the ninth), training the student to evaluate color tones.

## Sixths as melody or bass

> Sixths are very useful as they can serve, like thirds, as shadows to the melody or as bass notes.

**Significance (p. 105).** States a key functional rule: sixths are multipurpose intervals operating in two distinct textural roles.
