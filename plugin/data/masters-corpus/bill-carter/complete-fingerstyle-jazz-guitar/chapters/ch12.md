## Guitar as miniature orchestra

> Think of the guitar as a miniature orchestra where melody, rhythm, harmony and counterpoint
> are played at the same time. Lines are accompanied by other lines. Chords exist, but not so often
> as clumps of static harmony than as individual independent voices. Selection of notes and voice
> leading becomes very important, and the standard-brand barre chords we all learned early-on are
> likely to be replaced by smaller, partial chord fragments.

**Significance (p. 115).** Defines the core Section Three concept: guitar as polyphonic instrument with independent voices, not block chords.

## Specific-general practice method

> To get the most value from this Section, itis important to create specific examples from general
> exercises and to make general exercises from specific examples. For instance, if a concept is
> illustrated in the context of the cycle of fifths or harmonized scale only, you should make it your
> job to select a part of it and use it in a specific spot in a familiar tune. It could be part of an intro,
> ending, bridge, fill—anything, anywhere. Conversely, if you are attracted to a specific idea ina
> tune, take the time to put that idea through various general musical permutations, such as the
> cycle of fifths, or harmonized scale. This enables you to think of using it in other keys and other
> tunes. Make the specific general and the general specific.

**Significance (p. 115).** States the bidirectional practice methodology: extract from exercises into tunes, and generalize from tunes into exercises.

## Improvise upon chord structure

> In the practical world of jazz, we improvise upon the given chord structure of a tune. The chord
> structure itself is set in the context of a scale. The particular scale notes are chosen from 12 pos-
> sible notes in the tone row. Scales are typically arranged with one, two, or three half steps between
> notes.

**Significance (p. 115).** Frames the harmonic foundation: jazz improvisation operates over chord structures built from scales with specific interval patterns.

## Jazz seventh chords defined

> The sound of jazz harmony has come to include chords with at least four different notes.
> Counting alternate lines or spaces upward from the lowest note of scale step will produce a chord
> with these intervals: 1-3-5-7. The scale step on which a chord is built affects the numerical
> description of the intervals. If a chord is built from the first scale step, the scale spelling is 1-3-5-
> 7, but if the chord is built on the fifth step of the scale, it is spelled: 5-7-2-4.
>
> Since the top note is seven scale steps (or degrees) away from the bottom note, these chords
> are called seventh chords.

**Significance (p. 116).** Defines the minimum harmonic complexity of jazz: four-note chords with 1-3-5-7 stacking, and names them as seventh chords.

## Four chord types in major key

> Chords in a major scale can be grouped into four types:
>
> Two major sevenths land IV
> One dominant seventh Vv
>
> Three minor sevenths Il, land VI
> One half-diminished seventh

**Significance (p. 117).** Summarizes the complete taxonomy of diatonic seventh chords: two major sevenths, one dominant, three minor, one half-diminished.

## Open position voicings

> The configuration of the harmonized seventh scale pictured on page 116 is in close position.
> Unless you have long spidery fingers, you are probably more comfortable with these chords in
> either of the following standard alternate configurations below. In the first case the third of the
> chord has been moved an octave higher, and in the second case both the third and fifth have been
> moved an octave higher.

**Significance (p. 117).** Introduces the open-position voicing concept: redistributing chord tones across octaves for playability on guitar.

## Diatonic harmony defined

> Harmony moving by whole steps and half steps within the scale is called diatonic harmony.

**Significance (p. 117).** Defines diatonic harmony as stepwise motion within a scale, the foundation for the walking-tenths and walking-sixths examples.

## Cycle of fifths in jazz

> The most basic harmonic movement of jazz is the cycle of fifths. The cycle of fifths, set in the
> context of the major scale descends in units of five scale steps, a perfect fifth (or seven half steps).
> In the diagram below F is a perfect fifth below C. A perfect fifth below F is B, and so it goes until
> moving through the cycle returns to C. This cycle is often graphically drawn as a circle because the
> last leg of the key cycle ends at the starting position.

**Significance (p. 118).** Names the cycle of fifths as the most fundamental jazz harmonic motion and explains its circular, descending-fifth structure.

## Cycle chord movement patterns

> The cycle often includes consecutive V7-| (dominant sevenths to major chords) or just V7-V7
> chords. Chords will commonly move every two or four beats, but may be any duration.

**Significance (p. 119).** Specifies common cycle-of-fifths chord patterns (V7-I or V7-V7) and rhythmic flexibility in chord duration.

## Root movement in cycle

> The harmonic movement of the next example shows the root movement in the lowest note of
> each chord. Every chord quality is different, but they all pass through the cycle pattern. The melodic
> fragment essentially repeats. Playing through the cycle does not mean that root movement must be
> used. You can use the cycle with other chord voicings. That is up to you, the composer/arranger.

**Significance (p. 119).** Teaches that cycle-of-fifths root movement is one voicing choice among many, separating harmonic motion from voicing.
