## Underlying rhythmic pulse

> Almost all jazz is in 4/4 time. The basic pulse of the rhythm is the quarter note, which, in the early
> days of jazz, was played by the drummer four times per measure with the kick drum. Even though
> today’s jazz is more free and more complicated, the basic pulse is the quarter note.

**Significance (p. 148).** Defines the quarter note as the fundamental rhythmic pulse of jazz, tracing its origin from the kick drum in early jazz.

## Underlying melodic pulse

> When you listen to a jazz solo usually you are hearing a rhythmic flow of eighth notes. The basic
> underlying beat of the rhythm is a quarter note with written or improvised melodies being played
> along with it in a stream of eighth notes.

**Significance (p. 148).** Defines the eighth note as the melodic pulse layer that flows over the quarter-note rhythmic foundation.

## Ghosting notes by plucking

> the music.

**Significance (p. 150).** Defines ghosted notes as extremely de-emphasized rhythmic elements felt more than heard, produced by very light plucking.

## Ghosting notes by slurs

> The ghosted or unaccented notes in the previous example were articulated by using very light
> picking. Hammer-ons and pull-offs, by their very nature, are less aggressive in attack and volume,
>
> and are ideal for ghosted notes.

**Significance (p. 150).** Introduces hammer-ons and pull-offs as natural ghosting techniques due to their inherently softer attack.

## Slides and fall-offs

> Slides and fall-offs are produced by plucking a fingered note once and then sliding the finger
> (with pressure) up or down the neck. The slides may be short or long, over one or many frets. With
> written music, they are preceded by an upward or downward diagonal mark, either / or \.
>
> A long slide is called a gliss, or glissando.

**Significance (p. 150).** Defines slides and fall-offs as expressive devices and introduces the glissando terminology.
