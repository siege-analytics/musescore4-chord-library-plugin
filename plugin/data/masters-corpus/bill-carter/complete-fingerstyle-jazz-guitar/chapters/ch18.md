## Within-the-scale cycle (Mozart fakeroo)

> A major scale can within itself provide cyclical movement, although not with
> exact perfect fifth intervals throughout. This within-the-scale cycle is used here
> through the Dm7, after which a’ VI (A’6) of the C scale is inserted. This deceptive
> cadence opens up several possibilities, but since we want to move on to E>, we'll
> stay with the A’6 as the way to go.

**Significance (p. 168).** Describes how the major scale provides an approximate cycle of fifths internally and demonstrates the deceptive cadence as a modulation pivot.

## Trust the ear

> Ultimately, whatever sounds good to the ear will probably work. Just go there. Of course, if all
> your playing buddies grimace, you do have to remember that there are community standards of
> decency.

**Significance (p. 168).** States that ear-based judgment ultimately governs harmonic choices, balanced by ensemble taste.

## Fun Jazz philosophy

> Since being taught to death is a horrible way to die, I've decided to make Part 7 a group of tunes
> without analysis, explanation or instruction. Just a personal word or two. Do | hear cheering?
>
> | call these pieces Fun Jazz, as they were fun for me to create. | didn’t sit down and compose
> them. They are improvisations based on standard chord changes which you may recognize.

**Significance (p. 169).** Frames Part 7 as unanalyzed improvisations over standard changes, shifting from instruction to creative example.

## Imply more with less

> In all of the tunes here | like to imply more with less. Chords, for instance, are most often only
> two or three notes, and the melody is all-important.

**Significance (p. 169).** States the core fingerstyle principle of implying harmony with minimal chord voicings (two or three notes) while prioritizing melody.

## Good evening friends / three voices

> In the days of Vaudeville, musicians used to play a purposely silly ending called "good evening friends." I decided to make the four-note theme into a blues.
> There are three independent voices at once in this piece, if you count a whole note as a voice. It is. Play the piece with an underlying triplet feel.

**Significance (p. 174).** Describes treating three independent voices simultaneously and applying a triplet feel to the piece.

## Ballad elbow room

**Significance (p. 178).** Names ballads as the form giving maximum freedom, including double-time feel shifts.

## Sparse blues / Joe Pass device

> This blues is sparse in bass notes yet feels as though nothing is missing. One way this is accom-
> plished is by using an almost continual eighth note flow. Joe Pass often used this device.

**Significance (p. 180).** Credits Joe Pass for the continual eighth-note flow technique that compensates for sparse bass notes in blues.
