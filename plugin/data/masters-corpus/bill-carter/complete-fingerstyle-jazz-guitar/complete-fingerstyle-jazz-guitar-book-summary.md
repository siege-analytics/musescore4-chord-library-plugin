## Overall Arc and Pedagogical Structure

*Beginning and Creating and Professional Fingerstyle Jazz Guitar* by Carter unfolds across three distinct phases that map directly onto its three-part title. Chapters 1-4 establish foundational equipment, posture, hand mechanics, and rhythmic literacy, treating the guitar as a physical instrument with specific biomechanical requirements before touching musical content. Chapters 5-11 pivot to the craft of arrangement -- reducing full-band jazz to solo guitar through a disciplined set of harmonization techniques (thirds, tenths, sixths) and accompaniment patterns (oom-pah, walking bass, comping). Chapters 12-18 enter professional territory, reframing the instrument as a miniature orchestra and teaching the jazz musician's harmonic vocabulary: diatonic seventh chords, the cycle of fifths, expressive idioms, and the structural conventions (intros, turnarounds, modulations) that govern performance practice. The progression is deliberately cumulative: no concept is introduced before its prerequisites are established, and signature techniques -- the alternating bass, voice leading through the cycle, thirds/tenths/sixths harmonization -- recur across phases with increasing sophistication.

## Characteristic Terminology and Systems

Carter's technical vocabulary is precise and consistent throughout. Right-hand fingers are named p, i, m, a, c; left-hand fingers are 1, 2, 3, 4. The two primary picking systems are the alternating i-m technique (for single-string passages) and the one-finger-per-string assignment (for cross-string arpeggios), with a decision rule: choose based on whether notes stay on one string or move across several. The alternating bass is the rhythmic engine of the entire approach -- a steady quarter-note thumb pulse on lower strings that provides both harmony and rhythm while fingers handle melody above. Harmonization is systematized through three interval families: thirds (a third below the melody, adjusted major or minor against the chord symbol), tenths (an octave plus a third below, functioning as an independent bass voice), and sixths (a sixth below, following the same major/minor adjustment discipline, with the key note always on top to preserve major-key sound). Carter's harmonic framework centers on diatonic seventh chords (the seven chord types within a major key), the cycle of fifths as the fundamental organizing motion in jazz, and the II-V-I progression as the load-bearing harmonic unit, with a specific voicing recipe: minor seventh voiced as root-plus-seventh, dominant seventh as root-plus-third.

## Rules and Prescriptions

Carter establishes a set of explicit, non-negotiable rules alongside more flexible guidelines. On the physical side: the guitar must allow string spacing sufficient for both hands to operate without muffling; the left thumb stays under the neck rather than wrapping around it; the right-hand arch must be at least level with the strings; the thumb plays with free stroke, the fingers with rest stroke for melody emphasis; and nails must be maintained uniformly. On notation and rhythm: both voices in a two-part texture must account for every beat in the measure simultaneously -- tied notes and rests in one voice do not excuse rhythmic silence in the other. On arrangement: the best tunes for fingerstyle treatment have strong, singable melodies that stand without elaborate harmonic support; chord tones are classified as dispensable or load-bearing, and arrangements must choose playability over harmonic completeness when the two conflict; economy of means is non-negotiable -- a complete arrangement should deploy arranging devices sparingly and well, never layering every available technique at once. On comping: the cardinal density rule is to play more during the soloist's rests and less when they play busily. On harmonization with sixths: the key note must sit on top, never on the bottom, or the result will sound minor rather than major.

## Methodology: The Arrangement Workflow

Carter teaches a systematic arrangement methodology centered on reduction and voice management. The workflow proceeds as follows: select a tune with a strong, melody-first character; reduce the full-band harmony to its essential chord tones, identifying which notes carry harmonic function and which are expendable; place the melody as the top voice; harmonize below it using thirds (consulting the TABLE OF THIRDS to determine major or minor adjustment), then anchor with a root or root-fifth bass line to create a three-voice texture; substitute tenths for thirds where an independent bass voice with its own rhythm is preferable; apply sixths where melodic shadowing or bass doubling is needed; and refine the accompaniment texture by calibrating density -- walking bass during melodic rests, comping patterns during sustained melody notes -- so the arrangement breathes rather than maintaining constant textural thickness. Throughout, the ear is positioned as the final arbiter: non-chord tones from scale-derived intervals are acceptable as "interesting spice" when the overall harmonic effect is coherent.

## Harmony as Moving Voices

A philosophical commitment runs through the professional section (Chapters 12-18) that distinguishes Carter's approach from chord-box thinking: harmony is several independent moving voices, not a glob of dots on a chord diagram. The cycle of fifths is practiced not as a series of static shapes but through analyzed voice leading -- half-step motion in the tritone (the V7-I pull), common tones held between chords, alternating root positions -- so that smooth harmonic flow emerges from the interaction of lines rather than from chord substitution as such. The tritone substitution and related ii-V replacements are presented as physical voicing swaps rather than theoretical operations, with the ear confirming whether a substitution works in context. The diminished seventh functions as a "hamburger helper" passing chord. Fourths/tritones voicing offers a rootless alternative that delegates bass coverage to a bassist in ensemble settings. Carter's bidirectional practice methodology -- extracting specific examples from general exercises and generalizing specific examples through the cycle and harmonized scales -- trains pattern recognition across all twelve keys as an ongoing discipline rather than a one-time learning event.

## Expressive Language and Cultural Grounding

Chapter 15 establishes that jazz technique is ultimately idiomatic language: swing feel (written as straight eighths, played with shuffle), off-beat accenting, ghosted notes (produced through light plucking, hammer-ons, or pull-offs for rhythmic understatement), slides and glissandos (rooted in blues tradition), and rhythmic displacement through anticipation and delay. Carter consistently frames these not as notatable procedures but as vocabulary absorbed primarily through listening to live and recorded jazz. The blues foundation from Chapters 2-4 -- the E minor pentatonic blues scale, the twelve-bar form, the shuffle feel -- is never abandoned; it persists as the cultural and sonic root from which professional jazz technique grows. Carter's restraint principle culminates in Chapter 18: imply more with less, prefer two- or three-note voicings that defer to melody, and treat the arrangement as an improvisation in texture rather than an exhaustive demonstration of harmonic knowledge.
