# Prescriptive lessons -- bill-carter x complete-fingerstyle-jazz-guitar

## maj7
Carter treats maj7 as the stable resolution target of the II-V-I cycle, appearing diatonically only at I and IV. On guitar, playability drives an octave displacement of the third, and in solo contexts the chord is pared to two or three notes so the melody stays dominant. Voice-leading into and out of maj7 is the through-line: common tones are held while the tritone of the preceding V7 resolves inward by half step.

- [ch.12 p.117] Diatonic placement: exactly I and IV carry maj7 in any major key -- the taxonomy anchor for all harmonic-family reasoning.
- [ch.12 p.117] Open-position voicing: the third is displaced an octave higher from close position as Carter's "standard alternate configuration" for guitar playability.
- [ch.16 pp.155-157] Cycle voice-leading: when maj7 arrives as I after V7, track common tones and feel the tritone's inward half-step pull -- "several moving voices rather than a glob of dots on a chord box grid."
- [ch.18 p.169] Solo-arrangement minimal voicing: two or three notes maximum; "the melody is all-important" and chord density must not compete with it.

## dom7
Dom7 is Carter's primary engine of harmonic motion -- structurally indispensable at V and the only chord required at the end of intros, the pivot of tag endings, and the fastest modulation tool (as little as two beats). Its tritone is not decoration but a physical voice-leading mechanism: the half-step inward resolution is more central than root movement, especially in ensemble. Shell voicings (root + third) keep it lean; fourths/tritones stacks are reserved for when a bassist covers the roots.

- [ch.12 p.117] Harmonic-family membership: only one dom7 is diatonic -- the V -- making it the singular tension chord and II-V-I's load-bearing middle.
- [ch.13 p.129] II-V-I shell voicing: voiced as root + third only, communicating function clearly while preserving textural space above.
- [ch.16 p.156] Tritone resolution: the tritone (e.g., Bb and E in C7) resolves inward by half step to the root and third of the I chord -- the physical sensation Carter wants the player to feel.
- [ch.17 p.159] Half-step movement preference: in modern jazz, tritone voice motion supersedes root movement, especially with a bassist present covering the bass register.
- [ch.7] Tritone substitution: any dom7 may swap to the dom7 a tritone away -- same tritone interval, different root -- treated as a practical voicing choice rather than a theoretical operation.
- [ch.17 p.160] Intro structural requirement: the intro's final chord must be V7 or a V7 substitute -- non-negotiable gateway into the tune.
- [ch.17 p.164] Tag structural requirement: the final I chord is replaced by V7 or substitute to restart harmonic motion and allow another pass.
- [ch.17 p.165] Modulation device: borrowing the V7 of the target key for as little as two beats suffices to move to a new tonal center.
- [ch.13 p.130] Fourths/tritones voicing -- ensemble only: root and fifth omitted entirely; only valid when a bassist covers those registers.
- [ch.14 p.135] Comping density policy: fill the soloist's rests, thin out when they play busily -- though tagged here as dom7, Carter intends this as a universal comping rule.

## min7
Carter's min7 treatment is governed by two contexts: diatonic placement (II, III, VI in major) and the II-V-I shell-voicing recipe. The II min7 is the most functionally loaded, acting as the gateway chord of jazz's central harmonic unit. Bass alternation between root and seventh provides self-accompaniment motion; fourths/tritones stacking is again an ensemble-only option.

- [ch.12 p.117] Harmonic-family membership: three min7 chords per major key -- II, III, VI -- with II carrying the most structural weight as the II-V-I entry point.
- [ch.13 p.129] II-V-I shell voicing: min7 at II voiced as root + seventh only, paired with dom7's root + third -- the paired shell recipe for the entire II-V-I move.
- [ch.13 p.124] Root-seventh bass alternation: as a self-accompaniment device, alternate root and seventh in the bass; strict alternation matters less than maintaining the low-to-high register effect.
- [ch.13 p.130] Fourths/tritones voicing -- ensemble only: same constraint as dom7 -- omit root and fifth only when a bassist is present.

## min7b5
Carter gives min7b5 thin but precise treatment: one per major key (VII degree), diatonically located where its flattened fifth produces inherent instability. Its functional role is passing or substitutional within the cycle, exploiting that instability to connect more stable chords chromatically or diatonically.

- [ch.12 p.117] Harmonic-family membership: exactly one half-diminished seventh per major key, on VII -- the taxonomy fact establishing its rarity and instability.
- [ch.12 p.117] Passing/substitution function: the b5 makes it naturally suited for chromatic or diatonic passing motion between stable chords; Carter's cycle-of-fifths system is the operative context.

## dim7
Carter frames dim7 entirely as a connector, never as a destination. Its symmetric structure (all minor thirds) makes it interchangeable at multiple chromatic positions, and he exploits this specifically for ascending bass-line motion -- inserted between diatonic chords a step apart to produce smooth half-step movement.

- [ch.13 p.121] Chromatic passing chord: "the hamburger helper" -- fills the chromatic gap between adjacent diatonic harmonies.
- [ch.13 p.121] Ascending chromatic use: specifically deployed to generate ascending half-step bass motion between diatonic chords a step apart.

## aug7
Carter's aug7 coverage is narrow and mechanical: a single decision rule for reading altered-fifth chord symbols. There is no extended voice-leading treatment -- the augmented fifth is handled as a direct half-step adjustment to an existing dominant seventh voicing.

- [ch.7 p.77] Altered-fifth rule: C7#5 -> raise the fifth a half step; C7b5 -> lower it a half step. Direct mechanical adjustment to the dom7 shape, applied on demand from the chord symbol.

## maj
Carter's maj (triad / unspecified quality) lessons cluster around the harmonization toolkit that defines Chapters 8-11: thirds, sixths, and tenths as interval layers below the melody, each with distinct rules for quality adjustment, voice placement, and rhythmic independence. Restraint governs the whole: no single technique runs an entire arrangement, arpeggios stay below the melody ceiling, and the final measure of the section prescribes sparsity over completeness.

- [ch.8 p.91 / ch.9 p.93] Thirds harmonization: place a diatonic third below each melody note; consult the TABLE OF THIRDS and the chord symbol to confirm major or minor quality.
- [ch.9 p.93] Non-chord-tone thirds policy: when the diatonic third is not a chord tone, the ear decides -- non-chord tones are "interesting spice," not errors.
- [ch.7 p.83] Mandatory third when ambiguous: when the melody note doesn't clarify major vs. minor, the accompaniment must supply the third to define the harmony.
- [ch.9 p.93] Thirds require bass anchor: thirds alone cannot carry a full arrangement -- add even a simple root / root-fifth bass to create a three-voice texture.
- [ch.11 p.108] Sixths -- key note on top: in sixth-interval harmonization the key note must sit in the upper voice; inverting it makes the ear hear minor. (Proscriptive.)
- [ch.9 p.98] Tenths -- independent rhythm: tenths function as independent bass voices with their own rhythmic values; they only rarely move parallel to the melody rhythm.
- [ch.9 p.98] Tenths -- no parallel throughout: parallel tenths cannot sustain an entire arrangement because they lock bass rhythm to melody rhythm. (Proscriptive; cross-ref: tenths-independent-rhythm.)
- [ch.8 p.88] Arpeggio range ceiling: arpeggiation must not exceed the melody's highest pitch -- doing so obscures the melody and confuses the listener. (Proscriptive.)
- [ch.11 p.111] Compositional restraint: deploy arranging devices sparingly -- a complete arrangement "isn't cluttered with every arranging device, but is still totally whole, missing nothing."
