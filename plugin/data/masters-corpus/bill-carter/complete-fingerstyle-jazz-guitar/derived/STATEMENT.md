## Overview

Bill Carter's Mel Bay's Complete Fingerstyle Jazz Guitar is organized as a genuine progression through three named phases -- Beginning, Creating, and Professional -- and the book earns all three labels. The first four chapters treat the guitar as a physical and biomechanical object before touching musical content: hand posture, nail maintenance, stroke mechanics, and the decision rule for choosing between alternating i-m and one-finger-per-string picking. Chapters 5-11 pivot to the craft of arrangement, introducing a systematic harmonization vocabulary (thirds, sixths, tenths) and a set of accompaniment textures (oom-pah, walking bass, arpeggios, comping) that reduce a full-band jazz standard to a playable solo guitar piece. Chapters 12-18 enter professional territory, reframing the guitar as a miniature orchestra and teaching the harmonic language -- diatonic seventh chords, the cycle of fifths, II-V-I, tritone substitution -- that governs jazz performance practice. The progression is strictly cumulative: no device appears before its prerequisites, and the signature techniques (alternating bass, voice leading through the cycle, interval harmonization) return at each phase with increasing sophistication.

What distinguishes Carter's approach from chord-box pedagogy is a consistent philosophical commitment to voice independence. In the professional chapters he states it directly: harmony is several independent moving voices, not a glob of dots on a chord diagram. This principle already operates implicitly in the earlier arrangement chapters -- tenths move on their own rhythmic values, arpeggios must not breach the melody ceiling, the bass voice must account for every beat even when the melody is tied -- so by the time the cycle-of-fifths voice-leading work arrives in Chapter 16, the student has been prepared to hear harmonic motion as the interaction of lines. The result is a method that simultaneously addresses a working guitarist's practical constraints (position economy, string spacing, physical stamina) and a jazz musician's harmonic obligations.

Carter's restraint principle is the third organizing thread. It surfaces first as a rule about arrangement density (deploy devices sparingly; a complete arrangement should be whole, not cluttered), recurs as a comping guideline (play more in rests, less against busy melody), and culminates in Chapter 18 as an aesthetic philosophy: imply more with less, prefer two- or three-note voicings that defer to melody, treat the arrangement as an improvisation in texture rather than an exhaustive demonstration of harmonic knowledge. The blues foundation introduced in Chapters 2-4 -- the E minor pentatonic scale, the shuffle feel, the twelve-bar form -- is never discarded; Carter frames it throughout as the cultural and sonic root from which professional jazz vocabulary grows.

## System 1: Solo Fingerstyle Arrangement System

### What the System Governs

This is Carter's central practical system: the process of taking a jazz standard from a lead sheet and producing a coherent solo fingerstyle guitar arrangement. The system operates across four simultaneous voice/texture dimensions -- melody, harmonization interval, bass line, and accompaniment texture -- and specifies both how to move between options within each dimension (traversal) and how to shape or constrain individual elements once selected (modification). The workflow runs from tune selection (strong, singable melody is the primary criterion; tunes whose interest lies primarily in their chord changes are poor candidates) through melodic placement, interval harmonization, bass anchoring, and density calibration. Throughout, the ear serves as the final arbiter: non-chord tones that result from scale-derived harmonization are acceptable as interesting spice when the overall harmonic effect is coherent.

### Members

| ID | Name | Description |
|---|---|---|
| melody-voice | Melody Voice | The top voice, single-note, stems up, derived from a lead sheet. Must typically sit above the fourth-string D threshold to support two-part arranging. |
| thirds-harmonization | Thirds Harmonization | A diatonic third below the melody, adjusted major or minor by consulting the chord symbol and Carter's TABLE OF THIRDS. |
| sixths-harmonization | Sixths Harmonization | A diatonic sixth below the melody. The key note must remain on top; placing it below produces a minor rather than major sonority. |
| tenths-harmonization | Tenths Harmonization | An octave plus a third below the melody. Unlike thirds and sixths, which shadow the melody rhythmically, tenths function as an independent bass voice with their own rhythmic values. |
| root-bass | Root / Root-Fifth Bass | The foundational bass layer: chord root alone, or root alternating with perfect fifth. |
| walking-bass | Walking Bass | A quarter-note thumb line connecting chord roots through scale-step passing tones and chromatic approach notes. |
| arpeggio-accompaniment | Arpeggio / Broken-Chord Accompaniment | Arpeggio patterns assigning p to lower strings and i-m-a to upper strings, executed with free stroke. |
| comping-texture | Comping Texture | Sparse, responsive chordal punctuation, calibrated to complement the melody's rhythmic activity level. |

### Traversal Rules

TR-1: Melody Range Gate before harmonizing (Ch. 5, p. 62) -- Before selecting any harmonization member, verify that the melody sits predominantly above the fourth-string D. Carter states that tunes with a preponderance of notes below that threshold are too low for two-part arranging; if the melody sits too low, raise it an octave before proceeding.

TR-2: Density Inversion -- busy melody implies sparse bass (Ch. 6, p. 74) -- When moving between the melody voice and a bass texture choice, invert rhythmic density. A melody with rapid eighth-note motion calls for a held or slowly moving bass, while a melody with long sustained tones invites walking or rhythmically active bass activity.

TR-3: Walking bass during melodic rests; comping during sustained melody (Ch. 8, p. 85) -- The two primary accompaniment textures switch roles based on melody activity. Walking bass is appropriate when the melody is inactive; comping texture is appropriate when melody notes are sustained.

TR-4: Arpeggio must not exceed melody ceiling (Ch. 8, p. 88) -- When arpeggio accompaniment is in use, the arpeggio sweep must not reach higher than the melody voice.

TR-5: Tenths move on independent rhythm, not parallel to melody (Ch. 9, p. 98) -- When applying tenths harmonization, the tenth must be given its own rhythmic value rather than moving in strict rhythmic parallel with the melody.

### Modification Rules

MR-1: Thirds major/minor adjustment against chord symbol (Ch. 9, p. 93) -- Consult the chord symbol and the TABLE OF THIRDS to determine whether a major or minor third is correct.

MR-2: Sixths key-note-on-top convention (Ch. 11, p. 108) -- The key note must remain on top with the harmony note a sixth below.

MR-3: Sixths major/minor adjustment same as thirds (Ch. 11, p. 111) -- Apply the same major/minor adjustment discipline to sixths as to thirds.

MR-4: Thirds harmonization requires bass anchor for three-voice texture (Ch. 9, p. 93) -- A section harmonized only in thirds can stand alone briefly, but not for an entire song. A root or root-fifth bass must be added.

MR-5: Add major/minor third in bass when melody is harmonically ambiguous (Ch. 7, p. 83) -- The accompaniment must supply the third to resolve the ambiguity. This is a mandatory addition, not optional color.

MR-6: Compositional restraint -- deploy arranging devices sparingly (Ch. 11, p. 111) -- A complete arrangement should use the available arranging devices economically, not layer every technique simultaneously.

MR-7: Altered fifth -- raise or lower fifth per chord symbol (Ch. 7, p. 77) -- When a chord symbol carries #5 or b5, raise or lower the fifth by a half step accordingly.

MR-8: Abandon arpeggio when melody leaps wildly (Ch. 8, p. 90) -- If the melody moves in continual large leaps, arpeggio accompaniment is inappropriate.

## System 2: Cycle-of-Fifths Voice Leading System

### What the System Governs

This is Carter's professional-level harmonic system, introduced across Chapters 12-16 and applied throughout Chapters 17-18. It governs the selection and smooth connection of seventh chords through the cycle of fifths, treating each chord as a set of independent moving voices rather than a static shape.

### Members

| ID | Name | Description |
|---|---|---|
| major-seventh-chord | Major Seventh Chord (I, IV) | Two per major key; voiced as open-position fragments redistributing chord tones across octaves for playability. |
| dominant-seventh-chord | Dominant Seventh Chord (V7) | One per major key; contains the tritone that resolves by half step to the root and third of I. |
| minor-seventh-chord | Minor Seventh Chord (II, III, VI) | Three per major key; in II-V-I context voiced as root plus seventh. |
| half-diminished-chord | Half-Diminished Seventh Chord (VII) | One per major key; functions as a passing or substitution chord. |
| diminished-seventh-chord | Diminished Seventh Chord | The hamburger helper passing chord; inserted chromatically between adjacent diatonic chords. |
| fourths-tritones-voicing | Fourths / Tritones Rootless Voicing | Voicing pairs alternating perfect fourths and tritones, omitting root and fifth. |

### Traversal Rules

TR-1: Move down a fifth or up a fourth through the cycle (Ch. 16, p. 155)
TR-2: V7 tritone resolves by half step to I (Ch. 16, p. 156)
TR-3: Conceive harmony as moving voices, not chord shapes (Ch. 16, p. 157)
TR-4: Diminished seventh as chromatic connector (Ch. 13, p. 121)
TR-5: II-V-I as the foundational traversal unit (Ch. 13, p. 129)
TR-6: Two-beat harmonic rhythm default for cycle practice (Ch. 16, p. 155)

## System 3: Jazz Performance Structure System

### What the System Governs

This system governs the structural units that frame and connect choruses in a jazz performance: introductions, endings, vamps, turnarounds, tags, and modulations. Introduced primarily in Chapter 17, the system rests on a single organizing relationship -- I to V7 -- and on the cycle of fifths as the mechanism for moving between keys.

### Members

| ID | Name | Description |
|---|---|---|
| intro | Introduction | A two- or four-bar framework that must end on V7 to resolve into the tune's opening chord. |
| ending | Ending | A closing structure using V7-I or IV-I; produced from any intro by omitting the final V7. |
| vamp | Vamp | A repeating intro used when accompanying singers; repeated until the singer gives the cue to begin. |
| turnaround | Turnaround | The final two measures of each chorus; functionally equivalent to a two-measure intro. |
| tag | Tag / Coda | An extension of the final chorus; executed by replacing the usual final I chord with V7. |
| modulation | Modulation | A key change accomplished via V7 borrowing, back-cycling, or a full II-V7-I approach from the new key. |

### Traversal Rules

TR-1: Intro must end on V7 (Ch. 17, p. 160)
TR-2: Omit final V7 to convert intro into ending (Ch. 17, p. 163)
TR-3: Turnaround functions as two-measure intro (Ch. 17, p. 164)
TR-4: Tag replaces final I with V7 (Ch. 17, p. 164)
