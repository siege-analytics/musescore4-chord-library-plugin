## Bossa Nova eighth notes

> It's important to note that when it comes to jazz standards, many of them are played with a swing feel, meaning the eighth notes are swung. However, in the case of a Bossa Nova, the eighth notes are played straight.

**Significance (p. 41).** This gives a foundational rhythmic rule distinguishing Bossa Nova feel from standard jazz swing feel, directly affecting how the student plays the song.

> To be clear, this is a key center change because Db major is by no means diatonic to C minor. What makes this even more apparent is that it's a ii-V-I, which is textbook diatonic moving harmony.

## Melody as harmonic justification rule

> The truth is, any chord can follow any chord as long as there is a strong melody in place. This goes for improvisation as well. You could even play outside of the chord changes, but if your improvised melody is strong and you resolve it confidently, it's going to sound intentional and pleasing to the ear.

**Significance (p. 47).** This is a core compositional and improvisational rule stating that melodic logic supersedes harmonic convention, with direct implications for how students approach outside playing.

## Melody-based improv exercise

> 1. The first chorus, just play the melody completely straight. Essentially, there is no improvising happening yet. During this chorus, you are establishing exactly what the melody is.
>
> 2. The second chorus, embellish the melody. What does it mean to embellish? You are using the melody as a "guideline." You can veer off from the melody a bit, but not too far off. You want to be clearly hearing the melody throughout the chorus, while still taking liberties with it.
>
> 3. The third chorus, reference the melody. You can go ahead and improvise freely and don't necessarily have to stick so tightly to the melody. But you will need to reference it from time to time. Someone who just walked into your practice room should be able to eventually pick up what song you're playing because you referenced a part of the melody.

**Significance (p. 49).** This is the chapter's signature exercise, providing a structured three-stage method for building improvisation directly from the melody rather than from scales.

## Blue Bossa form length

> The next thing to pay attention to is that Blue Bossa is a 16 bar form. This isn't entirely uncommon, but not as common as a 12 bar, 24, or 32 bar form as you will see in many of the other jazz standards we will study.

**Significance (p. 45).** This defines the structural parameter of the tune and contextualizes 16-bar form within the broader taxonomy of jazz standard forms the student will encounter.
