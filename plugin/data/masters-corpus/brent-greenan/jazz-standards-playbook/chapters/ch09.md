## F#dim7 as VI7b9 substitute

> F#tdim/7 chord is a substitute for the dominant VI7 chord.
>
> Since we know that dominant 7 chords can have extensions added to
> them (9ths, Tlths, and 13ths) with different alterations (b9, #11, b13
> etc...), we can easily make the vi chord into a VI7b9 chord. Jazz musi-
> cians will often add different altered extensions to chords to add more
> tension and resolution to chord progressions.

**Significance (p. 141).** This passage establishes the core harmonic rule that a diminished 7th chord can function as a rootless altered dominant, which is foundational to understanding chromatic passing chords in jazz.

## Rootless altered dominant concept

> F#dim7: F#-A-C-Eb
> D7(b9): D-F#-A-C-Eb
>
> Do you see the similarities in these two chords? They are virtually the
> same except the root in the D7(b9). You can think of the F#dim7 as a
> rootless D7(b9).

**Significance (p. 142).** This chord-tone comparison gives the player a concrete, interval-level proof of why a diminished chord can substitute for an altered dominant, making the substitution rule practically verifiable.

## Diminished passing chord function

> Now, on a compositional level the F#dim7 is functioning as more than
> just a substitution. It’s also a creating a chromatic passing note, in this
> case the bass note, to connect the | chord with the ii chord.

**Significance (p. 142).** This defines the dual role of the diminished passing chord, harmonic substitution plus voice-leading, which is a key technique players must understand to use such chords purposefully.

## Bridge key centers moving in 3rds

> The second element is the pattern being used. Yes, it’s a series of
> ii-V-l’s which our ears recognize. But the key centers are moving in a
> particular order. They are moving in 3rds.
>
> Bb to Gb is a descending major 3" interval.
> Gb to D is a descending major 3" interval.
> D to Gb is an ascending major 3" interval.

**Significance (p. 144).** This identifies the structural logic of the bridge, descending-third key-center motion, which is the same device behind Coltrane Changes and must be internalized for navigating this class of tunes.

## Bridge as stacked ii-V-I progressions

> Luckily, when we break this bridge section down into key centers, it
> begins to feel easier to understand. Instead of looking at it as a mish-
> mash of seemingly unrelated chords, you can just look at it as a bunch
> of ii-V-l’s in different key centers.

**Significance (p. 143).** This reframes a complex multi-tonal passage as a manageable series of ii-V-Is, which is the practical cognitive strategy the player should apply when learning and improvising over the bridge.

## Whole-Half diminished scale formula

> the intervallic formula for this scale is: W-H-W-H-W-H-W. The scale is
> based on alternating whole steps and half steps.

**Significance (p. 146).** This defines the Whole-Half diminished scale and its construction rule, giving players the complete palette of available note choices over a diminished 7th chord.

## Diminished chord tone improvisation

> Remember that it is being substituted for the VI7b9 chord, so any of
> those chord tones will serve you well.

**Significance (p. 145).** This gives a direct, actionable rule for note selection over the passing diminished chord, anchoring improvisation decisions to the underlying harmonic function rather than the surface chord symbol.

## Connecting key centers by step

> ’s important to notice that | do my best to enter into each new key
> center by half steps or whole steps. I’m referring to the first and last
> notes of connecting bars. This isn’t a rule or mandatory by any means,
> but it does allow for a smooth flow in the lines.

**Significance (p. 147).** This articulates a voice-leading guideline for melodic improvisation across rapid key-center changes, teaching the player a specific technique for maintaining line continuity through the bridge.

## Resting across key center seams

> It's also perfectly fine to not connected the key centers together. |
> did this in bar 3 of this last lick. | ended on a half note and started a
> new line in the next bar. Nothing wrong with that. As long as your line
> doesn’t sound like it’s unnaturally starting and stopping, this can be a
> great solution.

**Significance (p. 148).** This presents an alternative phrasing strategy, using rhythmic space rather than stepwise connection at key-center boundaries, giving players a second, equally valid option for navigating the bridge.

## Theory vs. musical feel in practice

> Of course, | don’t want you to play this scale verba-
> tim when you are improvising. That’s why | think the best way to help
> you navigate these changes is to give you some musical ideas as a
> reference. In my experience, just playing the music rather than spend-
> ing too much time getting bogged down in theory is more helpful.

**Significance (p. 146).** This establishes the method's guiding pedagogical rule: learn the theory for context, but prioritize musical licks and ear-based navigation over mechanical scale application.
