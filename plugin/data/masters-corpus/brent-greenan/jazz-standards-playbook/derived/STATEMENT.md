# The Jazz Standards Playbook -- Statement of Outputs

## Overview

*The Jazz Standards Playbook* by Brent Greenan is a progressive case-study curriculum that uses ten jazz standards as sequential vehicles for teaching harmonic analysis and improvisation. The book is organized so that each standard introduces one or two load-bearing concepts -- diatonic series, guide tones, secondary dominants, modal harmony, substitution vocabulary -- that then reappear and accumulate across all subsequent chapters. No chapter is self-contained; the architecture is explicitly cumulative. Earlier standards supply the vocabulary needed to analyze later, more harmonically complex ones, and the student is expected to arrive at each new standard already having internalized its melody by ear before any harmonic or improvisational work begins (book-level distillation, Overall Arc).

The governing philosophy of the entire book is that ear precedes notation. Chapter 1 roots this historically in how bebop musicians actually learned -- by listening and reacting in real time -- and operationalizes it through the LIST process: **Listen**, **Internalize**, **Sing**, **Transfer**. This is not merely introductory framing; it controls the entire improvisation philosophy, which consistently redirects students away from mechanical scale application and toward melodic and harmonic listening. Chapters 2 through 5 build functional harmony fundamentals (ii-V-I, guide tones, chord tones, secondary dominants, target chords). Chapters 6 through 9 add a layered substitution vocabulary (VI7 dominants, modal harmony, tritone substitutions, diminished-as-altered-dominant). Chapters 10 and 11 synthesize everything into multi-key navigation and the Note Choice Map methodology. Chapter 12 is a motivational close with no new instruction (Chapter 12 summary).

The book's improvisation method is built around six enumerable tools -- guide tones, melody incorporation, chord tones, contrafacts, motifs, and transcribed licks -- whose meta-rule is that **improvisation is composition sped up** (Chapter 5 summary). Each tool was introduced progressively across chapters 2 through 8 and formally consolidated in Chapter 10. The book's most systematic pre-improvisation methodology, the Note Choice Map paired with the quarter-note solo exercise, appears in Chapter 11 as the culminating synthesis of all prior harmonic analysis techniques.

## Systems

### Functional Harmony Navigation System

The central system of the book. This system describes how jazz chord functions relate to and move toward one another in progressions built on functional harmony -- the kind of harmonic logic governing nine of the book's ten standards. Chord types are classified as members of a taxonomy that includes tonic, subdominant, dominant, secondary dominant, target chord, minor iv, backdoor dominant, hybrid ii-V-I, tritone substitution, diminished-as-rootless-altered-dominant, vi-minor-converted-to-VI-dominant, and passing diminished. The learner navigates the system primarily through ii-V-I resolution chains and guide-tone voice leading, and modifies it through a set of substitution and conversion operations that expand or recolor chord functions without abandoning the underlying harmonic logic.

**Members:**

- Tonic (I / Imaj7) -- home-base chord function; goal of ii-V-I resolution
- Subdominant (ii / IVmaj7) -- pre-dominant function leading toward V
- Dominant (V7) -- primary tension chord; always dominant quality; resolves to I
- Secondary Dominant (VII7, III7, VI7, II7) -- a dominant 7th chord functioning as V of a non-tonic diatonic chord
- Target Chord -- any chord being actively approached; gives harmonic direction to melodic choices
- Minor IV (iv / ivmin6 / ivmin7) -- borrowed subdominant from parallel minor
- Backdoor Dominant (bVII7) -- dominant substitute approaching I by whole step from below
- Hybrid ii-V-I -- minor ii and V borrowed from parallel minor resolving to major I
- Tritone Substitution (bIImaj7) -- chromatic substitute for V; also functions as passing chord between ii and I
- Diminished as Rootless Altered Dominant -- diminished seventh chord reinterpreted as rootless dominant 7(b9)
- vi Minor Converted to VI Dominant -- diatonic vi minor recast as a secondary dominant VI7
- Passing Diminished (biii dim) -- diminished chord used chromatically to connect adjacent diatonic chords

**Traversal Rules:**

- *ii-V-I Resolution Chain* [`FamilyCoherence`] -- Move through chord functions by chaining ii-V-I progressions; wherever a V appears, a ii may be prepended. (Chapter 2 summary, "there is no more important chord progression to know than the ii-V-I"; Chapter 10 summary, "Where there is a V chord, you can almost always add a ii in front of it.")

- *Guide-Tone Voice Leading (7th to 3rd)* [`VoiceMotion`] -- When moving from one chord to the next, resolve the 7th of the current chord to the 3rd of the following chord by smooth stepwise motion. (Chapter 2 summary, "the 7th of each chord resolves to the 3rd of the next chord"; "Voice leading is the smooth melodic movement of notes (or voices) from one chord to the next.")

- *Nearest Chord-Tone Arpeggio Motion* [`VoiceMotion`] -- When soloing across a bar line, move from the last note played to the nearest chord tone of the incoming chord. (Chapter 4 summary, "Whatever the last note played in a particular bar was, I move to the nearest chord tone of the next chord.")

- *Target-Chord Harmonic Direction* [`FamilyCoherence`] -- Identify where each chord is going (its target) before improvising, so melodic motion implies harmonic motion. (Chapter 4 summary, "To understand the function of a chord, you must look at both the chord that comes before it and after it"; Chapter 5 summary, "the most important thing to pay attention to when trying to understand the harmony of a song is where the chords are going.")

- *Half-Step or Whole-Step Key-Center Entry* [`PositionContinuity`] -- When navigating to a new key center, approach it by half step or whole step to maintain smooth melodic flow between sections. (Chapter 9 summary, "I do my best to enter into each new key center by half steps or whole steps... it does allow for a smooth flow in the lines.")

- *Dominant Chord as Key-Center Pivot* [`FamilyCoherence`] -- Use any V7 chord as a pivot point to establish and resolve into a new key center. (Chapter 10 summary, "The V chord is always your secret weapon for resolving to a new key center.")

**Modification Rules:**

- *vi Minor to VI Dominant Conversion* [`SubstitutionExpand`] -- Convert any diatonic vi minor chord to a VI dominant 7th chord to extend dominant chains or create secondary-dominant motion. (Chapter 6 summary, "the vi minor is converted into a VI dominant"; Chapter 2 summary, "it is entirely normal and acceptable to turn minor chords in the diatonic series into dominant 7th chords.")

- *Minor iv Substitution and Expansion* [`SubstitutionExpand`] -- Substitute the minor iv chord for the subdominant area, and optionally expand it into a ii-V of the minor tonic (Abmin7-Db7 or Db7 as concrete voicing alternatives). (Chapter 5 summary, "the minor iv as a quick jump into the minor tonic key... You can also turn that Abmin6 into a ii-V.")

- *bIImaj7 Tritone Substitution* [`SubstitutionExpand`] -- Replace the V7 chord with a bIImaj7 chord a tritone away; the chord may also function as a chromatic passing chord between ii and I. (Chapter 8 summary, "The easiest way to think of this chord [bIImaj7] is a substitute for the V chord... Sometimes this is called a tritone substitution.")

- *Diminished Seventh as Rootless Altered Dominant* [`SubstitutionExpand`] -- Reinterpret a diminished seventh chord as a rootless dominant 7(b9) one minor third below. (Chapter 9 summary, "F#dim7 chord is a substitute for the dominant VI7 chord"; "You can think of the F#dim7 as a rootless D7(b9).")

- *Backdoor Dominant (bVII7) Substitution* [`SubstitutionExpand`] -- Replace V7 with bVII7 approaching I by whole step; the ii chord of the backdoor dominant may be prepended to form the backdoor ii-V. (Chapter 11 summary, "A backdoor dominant is a dominant 7th chord that substitutes the V7 chord for a bVII7 chord approaching the I chord by a whole step"; "The ii chord is being added in front of the backdoor dominant.")

- *Hybrid ii-V-I Minor Borrowing* [`SubstitutionExpand`] -- Borrow the ii(b5) and V7(b9) from the parallel minor key while resolving to the major I chord. (Chapter 11 summary, "A hybrid ii-V-I is when a minor ii-V-i is mixed with a major ii-V-I. The ii and V are borrowed from the minor key, but they resolve to the major I.")

- *Avoid Major 3rd and Major 7th Over Minor IV* [`OmissionAllow`] -- When a chord functions as a minor iv or minor chord, omit major 3rd and major 7th scale degrees from melodic lines. (Chapter 4 summary, "Don't be playing major thirds or major 7ths! [over iv minor].")

- *Minimal Pitch Adjustment Across Changes* [`VoiceMotion`] -- When moving between chords, change only the pitches that differ -- specifically 3rds and 7ths -- and hold all common tones. (Chapter 8 summary, "I try to adjust the notes minimally, only to accommodate the changes necessary from one chord to another"; "The only changes made is a B natural to a Bb and an E natural to an Eb"; "If you're paying attention, you'll note those are the 3rds and 7ths.")

### Modal Pitch-Collection System

When a tune is based on modal rather than functional harmony -- most explicitly "So What" in Chapter 7 -- chord symbols are mapped to modes derived from a parent major (Ionian) scale rather than analyzed through ii-V-I resolution. Because modal harmony is non-functional, guide-tone voice leading does not apply; the improviser instead treats each mode as an inventory of available note choices (a pitch collection, not a mechanical exercise -- Chapter 6 summary) and connects arpeggios by nearest chord tone. Key centers shift by the interval separating their parent scales. The Altered Scale and Lydian Dominant extend the system for altered-dominant contexts encountered in Chapter 11's treatment of Stella by Starlight.

**Members:**

- Ionian (Major Scale) -- parent reference mode; formula 1-2-3-4-5-6-7
- Dorian -- minor mode with natural 6; formula 1-2-b3-4-5-6-b7; primary mode in the So What study
- Phrygian -- minor mode with b2
- Lydian -- major mode with #4
- Mixolydian -- dominant mode; major scale with b7; formula 1-2-3-4-5-6-b7
- Aeolian (Natural Minor) -- minor mode with b6 and b7
- Locrian -- half-diminished mode with b2 and b5
- Altered Scale -- used over altered dominant chords; introduced in Chapter 11 as an extended pitch collection
- Lydian Dominant -- Mixolydian with #4; introduced in Chapter 11 for dominant chords with #11

**Traversal Rules:**

- *Parent Scale Identification* [`FamilyCoherence`] -- Derive each mode's pitch content by identifying its parent major (Ionian) scale, then counting to the appropriate scale degree. (Chapter 7 summary, "Each mode is related to its parent major scale (Ionian)"; "In this case we are dealing with D Dorian. So what is the parent major scale, or Ionian, of D Dorian? C major.")

- *Half-Step Modal Center Shift* [`PositionContinuity`] -- Move between modal sections by the interval separating their parent keys; a half-step parent-key shift is the primary compositional device in modal repertoire such as "So What." (Chapter 7 summary, "Eb Dorian... parent key is Db major. This is just a half step up from the previous key center.")

- *Minor-Third Key-Center Movement* [`SymmetryMovement`] -- Recognize key centers moving in minor thirds as a recurring compositional technique across multiple standards. (Chapter 8 summary, "There are other standards you will come across that have key center changes a minor 3rd up from the parent tonic. This is a common compositional technique"; Chapter 9 summary, "The key centers are moving in a particular order. They are moving in 3rds.")

- *Nearest Chord-Tone Modal Arpeggio Connection* [`VoiceMotion`] -- In modal contexts where guide tones don't apply, connect modal arpeggios by moving to the nearest chord tone of the next chord. (Chapter 7 summary, "In this exercise, I have connected each arpeggio with the nearest chord tone of the proceeding chord.")

**Modification Rules:** None identified for this system.

### Improvisation Toolkit System

The six improvisation tools constitute the member taxonomy of this system. The LIST process (Chapter 1) governs the ordering in which any tool is applied -- ear and voice precede notation and physical technique -- and the 12-key transposition requirement (Chapter 6) applies universally to every lick, pattern, and vocabulary item. The Note Choice Map, introduced in Chapter 11, synthesizes Tools 1 and 3 by stripping rhythmic complexity entirely through the quarter-note solo exercise so the learner can focus exclusively on pitch navigation through chord changes. The meta-rule governing the whole toolkit is that improvisation is composition sped up (Chapter 5 summary), which makes contrafact composition a central deliberate-practice method rather than a performance preparation.

**Members:**

- Tool 1: Guide Tones -- improvise using only 3rds and 7ths to outline harmony
- Tool 2: Use the Melody -- play straight melody first chorus, embellish second, reference third
- Tool 3: Use the Chord Tones -- arpeggiate chord tones (R-3-5-7) as the primary pitch source for solos
- Tool 4: Write a Contrafact -- compose an original melody over the existing chord changes
- Tool 5: Use Motifs -- develop recurring rhythmic or melodic cells with variation throughout a solo
- Tool 6: Write or Transcribe Licks -- compose original licks or transcribe recorded solos and internalize them through all 12 keys

**Traversal Rules:**

- *LIST Process Ordering (Ear Before Notation)* [`_pending:ear-first-sequencing`] -- Always sequence learning as Listen then Internalize then Sing then Transfer to instrument; ear and voice precede notation and physical technique. (Chapter 1 summary, "Listen, Internalize, Sing, Transfer"; "learning music by ear was always the primary method"; "if you can sing it, you can play it.")

- *Melody-First Chorus Progression* [`_pending:chorus-progression-ordering`] -- Progress through a tune chorus by chorus: straight melody first, embellishment second, loose melody reference third. (Chapter 3 summary, "The first chorus, just play the melody completely straight... The second chorus, embellish the melody... The third chorus, reference the melody.")

- *12-Key Transposition Requirement* [`_pending:transposition-requirement`] -- Every lick, pattern, or vocabulary item must be practiced and internalized in all 12 keys before it is considered learned. (Chapter 6 summary, "I always suggest taking things through all 12 keys.")

- *Quarter-Note Solo Navigation (Note Choice Map)* [`_pending:rhythmic-reduction-exercise`] -- Strip rhythmic complexity by soloing exclusively in quarter notes to isolate and practice pitch navigation through chord changes. (Chapter 11 summary, "create quarter note solos. This means I stick to a quarter note rhythm in all of my note choices. I take out the rhythmic component from my improvisation and just focus on navigating the changes.")

**Modification Rules:**

- *Contrafact Simplicity and Space Rule* [`DensityCeiling`] -- A contrafact melody must be simple, outline the chord changes clearly, express a theme, and include deliberate space. (Chapter 5 summary, "Try to keep it simple. The exercise here is to create a strong melody that both outlines the chord changes appropriately while expressing a theme"; "There are re-occurring rhythmic and melodic themes throughout with variations... I leave space.")

- *Motif Variation and Recurrence* [`_pending:motif-development`] -- Motifs must be short rhythmic or melodic cells that are repeated or evoked with variation throughout the solo. (Chapter 8 summary, "In music, a motif is a short rhythmic or melodic passage that is repeated or evoked in various parts of a composition.")

- *Chromatic Passing Tone Allowance* [`NCTHarmonization`] -- Non-diatonic chromatic passing tones connecting two diatonic pitches by half step are permitted within otherwise diatonic melodic lines. (Chapter 8 summary, "A chromatic passing tone is a non-diatonic note that connects two diatonic notes in a passage, usually by half steps.")
