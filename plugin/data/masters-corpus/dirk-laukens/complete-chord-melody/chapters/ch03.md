## key selection rule

> Search for a key where you can play the melody on the top 3 strings, roughly anywhere from the
> 3rd to the 9th fret.

**Significance (p. 14).** This is the method's core rule-of-thumb for choosing an arrangement key, anchoring all subsequent voicing decisions.

## melody placement on strings

> We need to be able to play the melody on the top 2
> strings (maybe dipping into the 3rd string occasionally) and around the 3rd to 9th fret ideally.
>
> Let’s now change the TAB of the melody line so that it mainly sits on the top 2 strings.
>
> This will mean you will need to play more horizontally over the fretboard, rather than staying in a
> single position.

**Significance (p. 15).** Defines the method's characteristic horizontal-playing approach that makes space for chord voicings underneath the melody.

## chord placement rule

> You don’t need a chord on every melody note.
>
> Too many chords can make your arrangement sound cluttered and make it difficult to play.
>
> Notice on the above example, I mainly place chords on the long-held melody notes. It sounds great
> and is easy on the hands.

**Significance (p. 17).** This is a fundamental economy rule that governs the entire chord melody approach, preventing over-harmonization.

## adapting long melody notes

> Also, notice how I adapted the melody in the above example. The long melody notes ring out quickly
> on guitar, so rather than trying to hold them for their full length, I place a chord voicing to emphasize
> the change of chord instead.

**Significance (p. 17).** Teaches the guitar-specific technique of substituting a chord voicing for an unsustainable long melody note, a cornerstone of the method.

## fingerstyle right-hand technique

> To pluck the bass notes, you can use your thumb by itself or consider Lenny Breau’s approach of
> using a thumb pick. This can be helpful to play single lines easily like a plectrum player when you
> need it.

**Significance (p. 18).** Gives a specific right-hand option (thumb pick) that bridges fingerstyle and plectrum technique for bass-note articulation.

## plectrum technique for chords

> Quick tip: When using a plectrum, pluck the bass note first, followed by a quick strum of the rest
> of the chord (you can see me doing this in the example below) - this can be a way to negotiate
> chord voicings with tricky string skips.

**Significance (p. 20).** Provides a concrete right-hand technique for plectrum players to handle string-skipping voicings that would otherwise be unplayable.

## hybrid picking definition

> There is a way to combine a standard plectrum with fingerstyle, called hybrid picking.
>
> In this case, you hold a plectrum as usual but use your free fingers (the middle, ring, and pinky) to
> play chord voicings whilst using the plectrum to pluck the lowest note in the chord.

**Significance (p. 21).** Defines the hybrid picking technique and its specific application to chord melody before the author rules it out as his recommendation.

## three harmonic families

> in music, there are three primary colors when it comes to harmony.
> A chord must belong to one of the following classifications:
>
> e Major — e.g. CMaj7, C6, CMaj9
> e Minor — e.g. Cm7, Cm9, Cm7b5
> e Dominant — e.g. C7, C9, C13, C7(b9)

**Significance (p. 22).** Establishes the foundational harmonic taxonomy the entire chord vocabulary module is built upon.

## minor and dominant sub-families

> Minor:
>
> e ‘m7’ sub-family - e.g. Cm7, Cm9, Cm13
> e ‘m7b5’ sub-family - e.g. Cm7b5, Cm1ib5
>
> Dominant:
>
> e ‘7’ or ‘natural dominant’ sub-family - e.g. C7, C9, C13
> e ‘7Zalt’ or ‘altered dominant’ sub-family - e.g. C7(b9). C7(b13b9), C7(b5), C7alt

**Significance (p. 23).** Refines the three families into sub-families, introducing the m7b5 and altered dominant categories that are critical for correct harmonization.

## altered vs. natural dominant rule

> I’m pointing this out early on in this course because it can often sound wrong playing a natural
> dominant instead of an altered dominant or playing a m7 instead of a m7b5 chord depending on the
> harmonic situation.

**Significance (p. 23).** States the key rule motivating the sub-family distinction — using the wrong dominant or minor sub-family produces audible harmonic errors.

## chord collection definition

> A chord collection is a group of chords that can be played over a particular chord quality or
> progression.
>
> They are useful because the sequence of chords in the collection includes every diatonic interval as
> the top note of the chords.
>
> Which means: no matter which note is in the melody line, you can harmonize it by referring to the
> chord collection.

**Significance (p. 24).** Defines the method's central organizational concept — the chord collection — explaining why it solves the problem of harmonizing any melody note.

## barre fingering technique

> The fingerings of the chords are important if you can manage them. An example would be the
> 3rd finger barre of the GMaj7 voicing. Using barres in that way can free up your other fingers
> to play additional notes or passing tones.

**Significance (p. 26).** Teaches the specific left-hand technique of using barre fingerings within voicings to keep fingers free for melody ornaments.

## Joe Pass chord-scale approach

> If you play this example, notice how it sounds like a Joe Pass chord melody line. Joe Pass would often
> play a block chord then link to the next chord voicing with scale notes or passing tones in this way.

**Significance (p. 27).** Names and models the characteristic Joe Pass technique of connecting block chords with scalar passing tones, a defining move of the method.
