## ii-V-I covers all harmonic families

> e V - dominant
> e I - major

## Chords constructed by stacking thirds

> Chords are constructed most often by stacking thirds.

**Significance (p. 75).** This concise rule is the generative principle behind all chord construction in the method, explaining how triads, seventh chords, and extended jazz chords are all derived.

## Slash chord definition

> Slash chords are a way to indicate what note should be in the bass of a chord.
> For example: G6/E means to play a G6 chord with an E as the lowest note.

**Significance (p. 82).** This defines slash chord notation, which appears throughout the chord collections and is essential for understanding bass voice leading in chord melody arrangements.

## Same notes, different chord names

> A chord’s sound is as much defined by the chords that are around it in the progression, just as much
> as the notes of the chord themselves.

**Significance (p. 84).** This rule establishes context-dependence in chord naming — a foundational concept for arrangers who must choose the correct chord label based on harmonic function, not just pitch content.

## Melody on top 2-3 strings

> In order to fit our chord voicings underneath the melody notes, we need to change the fingering of
> the melody to sit on the top 2 (or 3) strings.
>
> Note: it’s ok to occasionally dip down the 3rd or even the 4th string if necessary - don’t get too rigid
> about only sticking to 2 strings!

**Significance (p. 98).** This is the central practical rule for chord melody arranging — keeping the melody on the top strings is what makes room for accompanying chord voicings on lower strings.

## Interval label relates to current chord

> The interval number we give a melody note must relate to the current chord in the
> progression — NOT the overall key of the progression. This is a common mistake beginner
> arrangers often make.
>
> The interval numbers are the chord tone numbers, and could be one of the following:
> 1, b9, 9, b3, 3, 11, #11, 5,b13, 13, b7, 7

**Significance (p. 107).** This rule — labeling melody intervals against the current chord, not the key — is the critical analytical step that allows the arranger to select the correct voicing from the chord dictionary.

## Golden rule: interval relates to chord

> The golden rule:
>
> | Always relate the interval to the chord at that point in the chord progression.

**Significance (p. 110).** This distills the method's core analytical principle into a single actionable rule that governs all voicing selection decisions in chord melody arranging.

## m7b5 and 7alt in minor ii-V-i

> A great way to learn these new kinds of chords is by studying minor ii V i progressions, as they
> include m7b5 for the ii chord and 7alt for the V chord.

**Significance (p. 61).** This explains how minor ii-V-i serves as the vehicle for learning the remaining chord sub-types (m7b5 and altered dominants) needed to complete the chord dictionary.

## 9, 11, 13 as second-octave intervals

> refer to the intervals as 9, 11, and 13 - as these are more useful interval names for these notes
> considering from a chordal point of view, these notes are derived from the 2nd octave of the scale.

**Significance (p. 78).** Explains the terminology convention students must understand to read chord symbols correctly — why upper extensions are numbered 9, 11, 13 rather than 2, 4, 6.

## Omittable notes in guitar voicings

> On the guitar, there is a difference between the theoretically correct chord and the playable chord.
>
> Often notes can be omitted from the chord shape to get a similar enough sound, but with a much
> easier shape.

**Significance (p. 79).** Defines the practical trade-off at the heart of jazz guitar voicing — dropping theoretically required notes to make chords playable.

## Common omitted intervals

> Intervals most commonly omitted include the 5th, the root (1st), the 11th, and the 9th. Note though
> that even the 3rd and the 7th can be omitted for more complex chords in certain situations as well.

**Significance (p. 79).** Specifies which chord tones are safe to drop — the operational rule that follows from the omittable-notes principle.

## Extensions vs. altered tensions

> An extension (sometimes called a ‘natural’ or ‘diatonic’ tension) is an interval of the chord which
> naturally occurs in the parent scale of the key of the progression. Extensions are included as part of
> the chord name. Examples are G9, G13, and GMaj9.
>
> Altered tensions are notes that are outside the parent scale of the key of the progression, and are
> (usually) added in parentheses after the chord name. Examples are GMaj7(#11), G7(b13#9).

**Significance (p. 80).** Defines the distinction between extensions and altered tensions — a prerequisite for correctly reading the complex chord symbols used throughout the method.
