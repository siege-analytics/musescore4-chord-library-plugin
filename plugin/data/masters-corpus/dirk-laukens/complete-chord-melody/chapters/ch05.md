## Melody note identification steps

> To know which voicing to pick we need to establish:
>
> 1. The string the melody note is on
> 2. The type of chord that is in the chord progression whilst that melody note is played
> 3. The interval of the melody note in relation to that chord.

**Significance (p. 114).** Gives the three-step lookup procedure that operationalizes the voicing-selection rule into a repeatable workflow.

## Playability over complexity

> “If it’s easy on the hands, the music will flow better - so most of the time making things as easy
> as possible is musically the best option anyway”.

**Significance (p. 124).** Frames ease of execution not as a compromise but as the musically superior choice, justifying simplification decisions throughout the arranging process.

## Adding tension to dominant chords

> The jazzy sound in arrangements usually comes from adding extensions and tensions to voicings -
> particularly to dominant chords.

**Significance (p. 127).** Identifies the specific harmonic lever — dominant-chord tension — that transforms a 'vanilla' arrangement into a convincingly jazz-flavoured one.

## Solo guitar illusion concept

> A good solo guitar arranger simply suggests these elements throughout the performance. Play a
> measure of walking bass, followed by a chord line, followed by unaccompanied single lines —
> alternating through these different devices gives the ‘illusion’ to the audience of a complete band.
>
> The listener’s mind will fill in the gaps.

**Significance (p. 138).** Defines the governing aesthetic principle of solo jazz guitar arranging: implication rather than completeness, which shapes every subsequent technique in the module.

## Fingerstyle vs pick for solo guitar

> many of the voicings you are about to learn are quite difficult to play with a pick if
> you intend to sound the notes simultaneously without tricky muting techniques. For this reason, I
> gravitate to using fingerstyle picking for solo guitar.
>
> But, you can still use these voicings with a pick. Some players strike the bass note first with their pick
> then follow up with a strum of the upper notes in the voicing

**Significance (p. 139).** Addresses the fundamental technique choice for solo guitar and provides a practical workaround for plectrum players.

## Bass notes role in solo guitar

> The key difference between a ‘trio’ chord melody arrangement (to be played in a band) and solo
> guitar is the greater prominence of bass notes on the 5th and 6th strings.
>
> You no longer have a bass player to play with, so you need to fill that role ourselves — otherwise, the
> arrangement will sound thin and empty.

**Significance (p. 140).** Identifies the single most important structural difference between trio and solo arranging, making bass-note placement the primary new skill to develop.

## Key selection for open strings

> An excellent way around this is to choose a key for your arrangement that allows the use of open
> strings - especially for the bass notes.
>
> Choosing a key like A, E, or D gives you essentially another set of free fingers. Without needing to fret
> the occasional bass note, you can make things much easier technically.

**Significance (p. 142).** Gives a concrete, actionable strategy — key choice — for solving the technical difficulty of simultaneous bass and chord voicings in solo guitar.

## Shell voicing definition

> These are called shell voicings — i.e. the ‘bare bones’ of what you need to define the sound of the
> chord: the root, 3rd, and 7th.

**Significance (p. 145).** Defines the term 'shell voicing' and its three essential components, which become the foundational building block for all solo guitar arrangements in this module.

## Why remove the fifth

> You can remove the 5th from the chord and still retain the essential character of the sound.

**Significance (p. 145).** States the harmonic principle that justifies shell voicings — the fifth is expendable — allowing players to reduce fingering complexity without sacrificing chord identity.

## Arpeggiation definition and effect

> Arpeggiation is when you pluck the notes of a chord one at a time, instead of a block of notes
> played simultaneously.
>
> This can give a more lyrical sound and is especially effective in arrangements of jazz ballads.

**Significance (p. 169).** Defines arpeggiation and specifies its primary musical context (ballads), giving the arranger a clear criterion for when to deploy the technique.

## Octaves technique definition

> Octaves are a case of doubling the melody one octave down.

**Significance (p. 174).** Concise definition of the octave technique as used in chord melody, establishing it as a specific melodic-doubling device rather than a chordal one.

## Octave muting technique

> When playing the octaves, mute the unwanted open string between the octave notes by gently
> touching the string with your first finger.

**Significance (p. 179).** Provides the essential left-hand muting technique required to cleanly execute octaves on guitar, without which the in-between string will ring sympathetically.

## Diads definition

> Diads are when you harmonize a line in a parallel interval, usually 3rds or 6ths.

**Significance (p. 180).** Defines diads as a specific parallel-interval harmonization technique and identifies the two most common intervals used, directly setting up the notated examples.

## Refining arrangements principle

> Refining an arrangement is the most important step of solo guitar arranging.
>
> Making small tweaks, cutting unessential notes, and looking for skillful ways to finger passages to get
> the arrangement to feel good under the hands and sound good is the aim.

**Significance (p. 160).** Establishes refinement — not initial harmonization — as the most critical stage of solo arranging, reframing the overall workflow hierarchy.

## Chord voicing selection rule

> Choose chord voicings that have the same interval of the melody note as the highest note in the
> chord shape.
>
> OR
>
> a voicing that can be slightly adapted to have the melody note as the highest note and still sound
> good.

**Significance (p. 114).** This is the central rule of the chord-dictionary method — the single criterion that drives every voicing decision in the arrangement process.

## Chord placement frequency

> generally you don’t need to place
> chords on every melody note for a chord melody.
>
> In fact, less is more — placing chords at key places ‘here and there’ is easier on the hands and is
> usually more than enough to imply the harmony beneath the melody line.

**Significance (p. 122).** Establishes the core density guideline — sparse harmonization is both more playable and more musically effective than wall-to-wall chords.

## Options for difficult voicings

> Cut out notes from the chord - e.g. we could cut that low B to make it easier to change to the
> next chord

**Significance (p. 125).** First in a ranked toolkit for resolving unplayable voicings — drop notes rather than abandon the chord.
