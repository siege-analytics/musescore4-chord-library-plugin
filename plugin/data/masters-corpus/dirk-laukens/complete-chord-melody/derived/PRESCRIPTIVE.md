# Prescriptive lessons — dirk-laukens × complete-chord-melody

## maj7
Laukens treats Maj7 as the defining color of the Major harmonic family, voiceable from a shell (root, 3rd, 7th) with the 5th as first omission candidate. Beyond voicing economy, the chapter on substitution opens Maj7 to replacement by vi, iii, or any chord sharing the current melody note as a common tone. Shell voicings over walking bass alternate between the downbeat and the "& of 1" to keep the feel from locking into rigidity.

- [ch.3 p.22] Treat Maj7 (alongside C6 and CMaj9) as a member of the Major harmonic family; build your chord collection from this family so any melody note above a major root can be harmonized.
- [ch.5 p.145] Use shell voicings — root, 3rd, 7th — as the minimum viable Maj7 voicing; drop the 5th entirely without compromising harmonic identity.
- [ch.4 p.79] Omit in this priority order: 5th, root, 11th, 9th — keeping voicings lean and playable while retaining essential character.
- [ch.6 p.224] Substitute Maj7 I with the vi minor chord of the same key (e.g., CMaj7 → Am7) when the melody note is compatible, to add color while preserving tonic function.
- [ch.6 p.225] Replace Maj7 with chord iii of its key (e.g., CMaj7 → Em7) as an alternative reharmonization; strong common tones maintain the major family sound.
- [ch.7 p.247] Any chord may substitute for Maj7 provided the substituting chord contains the current melody note as a common tone on top — always verify this condition before deploying.
- [ch.6 p.186] When adding shell voicings over a walking bass on a Maj7 chord, mix placement between the downbeat and the "& of 1" to create rhythmic variety and an authentic jazz feel.

## min7
Laukens anchors min7 as the ii chord in major ii-V-I progressions, voiced from the same root/3rd/7th shell with 5th omitted first. The substitution framework extends min7's range: it can stand in for any dominant whose root lies a 4th below (iim7-for-V), and Joe Pass's ii/V interchangeability rule makes min7 and dom7 freely swappable. Shell rhythmic placement over walking bass follows the same downbeat/"& of 1" alternation prescribed across all chord qualities.

- [ch.4 p.48] Assign min7 voicings to the ii chord in major-key ii-V-I progressions; this is the primary harmonic context that trains the ear to every other min7 application.
- [ch.3 p.23] Treat min7 (with Cm9 and Cm13 as the 'm7' sub-family) as the standard minor color; never substitute min7 where the harmonic situation calls for min7b5 — it will sound wrong.
- [ch.5 p.145] Voice min7 at minimum with root, 3rd, and 7th; the 5th is the first candidate for omission and can be dropped without losing the chord's essential character.
- [ch.6 p.230] Play a min7 built on the 5th of any dominant chord as a substitute for that dominant (e.g., Cm7 over F7); this borrows the iim7 from the dominant's key and produces a less direct sound.
- [ch.6 p.230] Following Joe Pass's practice, freely interchange ii (min7) and V (dom7) with one another in a progression — treat them as functionally interchangeable colors.
- [ch.6 p.186] When comping min7 shell voicings over a walking bass line, alternate placement between the downbeat and the "& of 1" rather than always landing on beat one.

## min7b5
Laukens reserves min7b5 strictly for the ii slot in minor ii-V-i progressions, treating it as a distinct 'm7b5' sub-family that must never be blurred with plain m7. The sub-family boundary is a hard rule: the wrong voicing sounds wrong, and there is no practical overlap. When min7b5 appears as the ii of a minor ii-V-i, it also functions as a lick-swap trigger — the entire minor ii-V-i written melody may be replaced with a pre-learned jazz lick at that point.

- [ch.4 p.61] Use min7b5 exclusively as the ii chord in a minor ii-V-i progression; study this progression as the primary vehicle for internalizing min7b5 voicings in context.
- [ch.3 p.23] Never substitute plain min7 for min7b5 when the progression demands it — keep min7b5 (and its extension Cm11b5) as a distinct sub-family, separate from the 'm7' family.
- [ch.7 p.250] When min7b5 appears as the ii of a minor ii-V-i (e.g., Bm7b5–E7b9–Am7), treat it as the trigger to replace the written melody at that point with a pre-learned minor ii-V-i jazz lick.
- [ch.3 p.22–23] Assign min7b5 to the minor harmonic family but classify it as its own 'm7b5' sub-family; include it in the chord collection for any minor tonic context where the flattened 5th is diatonic.

## dom7
Laukens places dom7 in the "natural dominant" sub-family, distinct from altered dominants, and mandates color tones (9ths, 13ths) as the primary source of jazz sound. The shell (root, 3rd, 7th) is the irreducible floor, but dominant voicings must be enriched with extensions wherever playable. Joe Pass's ii/V interchangeability, tritone substitution, back cycling, and chromatic approach chords all apply at every dominant.

- [ch.3 p.23; ch.4 p.48] Assign dom7 (natural dominant, including C9 and C13) to the Dominant harmonic family's 'natural dominant' sub-family; use this sub-family for V chords in major ii-V-I progressions.
- [ch.5 p.127] Add extensions and tensions (9ths, 13ths, etc.) to dom7 voicings whenever possible — these color tones are the primary source of the jazzy sound in chord-melody arrangements.
- [ch.5 p.145] Build dom7 shell voicings from root, 3rd, and 7th; these guide tones are the irreducible core, and the 5th can always be dropped first.
- [ch.4 p.87] Starting from a basic G7 shape, generate many distinct dom7 voicings by adding or adapting individual notes from that template rather than learning each voicing in isolation.
- [ch.6 p.220; p.216] Replace any dom7 with the dom7 a tritone away (e.g., F#7 for C7); the guide tones swap positions but remain common tones, ensuring smooth voice-leading.
- [ch.6 p.186] When placing dom7 shell voicings over a walking bass, alternate between the downbeat and the "& of 1" to introduce rhythmic variety and avoid stiff on-the-beat placement.
- [ch.6 p.197] To approach a dom7 chromatically, play the identical voicing one fret above or below the target position, then resolve; the same shape one fret away is sufficient for the chromatic approach effect.
- [ch.3 p.23] Do not use a natural dom7 where the harmonic context demands an altered dominant — the wrong sub-family will sound wrong; always determine natural vs. altered before choosing a voicing.
- [ch.6 p.230] Following Joe Pass, treat V (dom7) as interchangeable with ii (min7) in a progression; either chord can stand in for the other, giving a flexible reharmonization option at every dominant.
- [ch.7 p.243] Extend or reharmonize any dom7 by inserting preceding chords from the back cycle (circle of fourths); apply this to build tension before any dominant resolution, with caution against overuse in ensemble settings.

## dom7b9
Laukens places dom7b9 firmly in the altered dominant sub-family and treats the minor ii-V-i as its primary teaching context. The altered color is non-negotiable — swapping in a natural dominant will sound wrong — and the more extensions (b13, etc.) that can be layered on, the more fully the jazzy tension materializes. Dom7b9 at the V position also triggers lick-swap substitution of the entire surrounding minor ii-V-i melody.

- [ch.3 p.23] Classify dom7b9 (C7b9, C7b13b9) within the 'altered dominant' sub-family alongside C7b5 and C7alt, distinct from the natural dominant sub-family; always apply altered voicings when the context demands alteration.
- [ch.4 p.61] Use dom7b9 as the V chord in minor ii-V-i progressions (e.g., Bm7b5–E7b9–Am7); study minor ii-V-i progressions specifically to learn dom7b9 voicings in their natural harmonic context.
- [ch.3 p.23] Never substitute a natural dominant for dom7b9 when the progression demands altered color — the wrong sub-family will sound wrong; commit to the altered voicing whenever the situation specifies it.
- [ch.7 p.250] When dom7b9 appears as the V of a minor ii-V-i (e.g., E7b9 in Bm7b5–E7b9–Am7), use it as the trigger to substitute a pre-learned minor ii-V-i lick in place of the written melody at that point.
- [ch.5 p.127] As an altered dominant, dom7b9 already carries primary tension; further enrich it with additional alterations (b13, etc.) where the voicing allows — the jazzy sound depends on loading dominant chords with extensions.

## dom7alt
Laukens treats dom7alt as the maximum-alteration member of the altered dominant sub-family, interchangeable with dom7b9 at the V of a minor ii-V-i but never replaceable by a natural dominant. Like all dominants, it must carry its color tones into the arrangement to produce the characteristic jazz sound. The sub-family boundary is absolute.

- [ch.3 p.23] Treat dom7alt (C7alt) as a member of the altered dominant sub-family alongside C7b9 and C7b5, not the natural dominant sub-family; use it wherever the harmonic situation calls for maximum alteration on the V chord.
- [ch.3 p.23] Do not replace dom7alt with a natural dominant — the wrong sub-family will sound wrong in context; confirm that the progression requires alteration before selecting any altered dominant voicing.
- [ch.4 p.61] Deploy dom7alt as a valid V-chord voicing choice in minor ii-V-i progressions, interchangeable with dom7b9 within the altered dominant sub-family; use minor ii-V-i progressions as the primary study context for mastering dom7alt voicings.
- [ch.5 p.127] Like all dominant chord types, dom7alt must carry its extensions and tensions into chord-melody voicings — the jazzy sound of arrangements depends on loading fully altered dominants with these color tones.
