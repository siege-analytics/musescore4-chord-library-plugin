# Chapter 2 — Chord Melody Devices

_Distillation; backed by `chapters/ch02.md`._

Chapter 2 of Roberts' Chord Melody method lays out the core vocabulary and techniques for combining melody and harmony on the guitar. Roberts begins with two fundamental voicing types — close block voicings, where notes are packed tightly together, and open voicings, where they are spread apart — and shows how a close voicing can be converted to an open one simply by moving one or more tones up or down an octave. The chapter's governing principle is that voice leading is the single most important force linking chords: in four-part harmony one is really dealing with four independent melody lines, which may move in parallel (same direction, same number of steps), in contrary motion (opposite directions), or through counter lines, which Roberts typically places in the second voice from the top and activates most when the main melody is static. Building on these connective tools, the chapter surveys four practical chord-melody devices: block chords (a full chord placed under every melody note, with voicing or inversion changed with each new note so all lines appear to move together), sustained chord background (one chord held through several melody notes, dropping tones only as fingering demands while trusting the listener's harmonic memory to fill the gaps), common tones (a single note sustained across a sequence of chords), and short chord punctuations (brief harmonic accents that, when played with authority and timed well within the bar, can imply a full harmonic flow without constant restriking). A characteristic Roberts move threads through all four devices: the listener's ability to remember harmony is treated as a compositional resource, so sparse or dropped voicings remain acceptable so long as the harmonic outline is clearly established and melodically reinforced by emphasizing chord tones.
