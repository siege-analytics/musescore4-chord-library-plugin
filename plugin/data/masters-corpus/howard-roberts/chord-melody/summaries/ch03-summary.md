# Chapter 3 — Chord Melody Studies

_Distillation; backed by `chapters/ch03.md`._

Chapter 3 of this guitar method introduces chord melody playing as a craft of layered harmonic decisions, beginning with the principle that close voicings serve as deliberate textural contrast against the broader sound of open voicings. A central harmonic insight is that chord symbols matter less than the voice leading that produces them — functional Roman numeral identity can only be determined from the full phrase, not from isolated spellings — and this extends to substitution techniques where diminished chords replace dominant 7b9 sounds to inject chromaticism into otherwise diatonic lines. The master's characteristic technical instruction is to sustain each chord tone as long as physically possible, delaying finger movement until absolutely necessary, because this discipline directly generates the continuity that defines chord melody style. Two-note dyads are flagged as a largely unexplored resource for implied harmony, particularly in improvisation, while chromatic passing chords (including altered passing chords like m7+5 shapes) serve as connective tissue between diatonic progression points. Inner voices evolve developmentally — from passing tones to counter lines to fully melodic patterns — and the chapter closes by enumerating the complete structural vocabulary a chord melody player should command: vertical textures, contrary motion, parallel motion, counter lines, block chords, sustained chord backgrounds, and short punctuations.
