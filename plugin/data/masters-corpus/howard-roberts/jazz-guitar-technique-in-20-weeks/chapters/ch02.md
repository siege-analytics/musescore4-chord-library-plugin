## string gauge and accuracy

> Extremely narrow gauge strings do not create enough resistance or "return" to their center position quickly enough to permit the right hand to develope predictable accuracy, or to strengthen the left hand. So, use a medium-heavy set of strings; nothing smaller than an .012 first string, etc.

**Significance (p. 4).** Establishes the physical rationale for string gauge choice, directly linking equipment spec to right-hand accuracy and left-hand strength development.

## action height rationale

> The strings should be raised to a medium-high action. There are three reasons for this: (1) to eliminate buzzes, (2) to help. strengthen the left hand, and (3) to develop accuracy for the right hand.

**Significance (p. 4).** Provides the three-part pedagogical justification for string action height, tying setup directly to skill development goals.

## neck and fret setup

> The neck should be inspected to assure that it is straight. The frets should be dressed down, eliminating high and low spots to avoid buzzes.

**Significance (p. 4).** Specifies concrete pre-practice setup checks that ensure the instrument itself does not impede technique development.

## pick selection

> Your pick should be of medium size, and medium to heavy in thickness. |
> Avoid very large or odd shaped picks. Standard celluloid picks are
>
> well suited to this purpose.

**Significance (p. 4).** Gives prescriptive pick specifications that directly affect tone control and right-hand mechanics.

## required practice equipment

> Also essential to the studies in this book will be: (1) a metronome, (2) a reel-to-reel or cassette tape recorder, and (3) an alarm clock or timer.

**Significance (p. 4).** Enumerates the three non-instrument tools the method requires, establishing the feedback and pacing infrastructure the student must have before beginning.

## right hand as limiting factor

> The right hand seems to be the greatest limiting factor for most modern-day guitar improvisors. It appears that once a style of picking has become habitual, it is very difficult to change, and frequently, total retraining is required.

**Significance (p. 5).** Names the right hand as the primary technical bottleneck and warns that habitual bad technique demands complete retraining, motivating the cautions that follow.

## flexibility over fixed technique

> flexibility-the ability to adapt the right hand to a variety of moves-is the key to longevity.

**Significance (p. 5).** Defines the single governing principle of right-hand technique: adaptability over any fixed system.

## avoiding anchor systems

> The big thing to avoid is any kind of anchor system that inhibits freedom of movement. Observe anchors at the elbow, at the wrist, grasping the pick-guard with the little finger, etc.; all of which may be functional for a specific sound, but should be viewed with caution when considered as the basis of an overall right hand technique.

**Significance (p. 5).** Identifies specific physical anchors by name and location, explaining why each is problematic as a foundational technique rather than a contextual choice.

## tension stops speed

> Keep it
> loose! - like a guy strumming a ukelele in a pineapple field. Tf there
> is any rigidness in your picking leverage system, it can stop you like
> a brick wall when the tempos get fast.

**Significance (p. 5).** Delivers the core physical law that tension becomes a hard speed ceiling, using a memorable image to make the principle stick.

## left-right hand synchronization

> Under any circumstances, the left and right hands must be in perfect synchronization. No Flams! If the finger attacks first and the pick follows, there will be two sounds instead of one. If the pick attacks first and the finger follows, again, two sounds instead of one.

**Significance (p. 5).** States the flam problem precisely in both directions, defining what perfect synchronization means acoustically.

## slow practice to hear flam

> To do so, play tones very slowly at all points on the fingerboard. Only in this way can we hear the flam effect. If we play fast in warming up, it becomes more difficult to hear the differential of attack.

**Significance (p. 5).** Prescribes the diagnostic method—slow play across the whole fingerboard—as the only reliable way to detect synchronization errors before they become ingrained.

## left thumb position

> The thumb of the left hand should ride along the approximate center of the back of the neck.

**Significance (p. 5).** Establishes the baseline left-hand thumb position that enables the perpendicular finger angle taught in the sentence that follows.

## perpendicular fretting angle

> The fingers should operate straight up and down on the strings, like hammers in a piano, at a right angle to the fingerboard. Attacking the string at an angle will move the string from it's center position, both cutting down on accuracy and also stretching the string to sound out of tune.

**Significance (p. 5).** Explains both the correct finger angle and the mechanical consequence of violating it—intonation error and lost accuracy—giving students a concrete reason to comply.

## finger lift economy

> The fingers should be lifted only high enough off the string to avoid string noise when moving, but not so high as to become wasted motion.

**Significance (p. 5).** Defines the precise functional range of finger lift, balancing noise avoidance against efficiency—a key determinant of speed.

## left wrist posture

> The wrist should maintain a fairly flat posture. Avoid severe arching of the left wrist as this can produce undesirable strains.

**Significance (p. 5).** Gives the wrist position guideline and its health consequence, making injury prevention an explicit part of left-hand technique.
