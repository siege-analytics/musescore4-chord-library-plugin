## Key center definition

> When a given major or minor scale is stacked upon itself in thirds as shown in Example 1,
> it creates harmony characteristic of that scale.

**Significance (p. 6).** This is the foundational definition of how diatonic harmony is generated from a scale, establishing the theoretical basis for the entire chapter.

## Identifying dominant seventh

> Eb 7 is a V chord (Dominant 7 type) which only appears once in any given major key,
> always as the V chord, and therefore, only one possibility, the V of Ab.

**Significance (p. 7).** This teaches the key analytical shortcut that the dominant seventh chord uniquely identifies a single key, making it the most decisive chord for key-center determination.

## Using key center brackets

> In all studies to follow, the key centers have been bracketed above the
> chord progression. Observe these key centers with care and for each new one, move your
> hand to a convenient fingering pattern for that particular scale and do your improvising
> in that region of the guitar.

**Significance (p. 8).** This gives the student a direct, actionable physical instruction for how to use key-center analysis while improvising over the book's exercises.

## Five movable scale patterns

> There are five such patterns for any given scale. And each
> of these five are movable up or down the neck to the selected key or, they may be connected
> to increase the range.

**Significance (p. 8).** This establishes the five-pattern framework that organizes the entire fingerboard system taught in the chapter.

## Playing changes by ear

> In addition to being able to logically calculate key centers fron an analysis of the progres-
> sion, the ability to play over changes by ear, can be viewed as the ideal end result.

**Significance (p. 8).** This sets the ear as the ultimate goal beyond intellectual analysis, orienting the student's long-term practice direction.

## Finger-per-fret mechanics

> There are five tone patterns for the diatonic major scale. The mechanics of the fingerings
> center around the use of a finger per fret, although in most cases it becomes necessary to
> reach out of position one fret either above or below the basic position with the first or
> fourth finger.

**Significance (p. 9).** This defines the physical left-hand rule governing all five patterns, giving students the core technique underlying every fingering diagram in the chapter.

## Patterns cover full fingerboard

> When these patterns are laid end to end, they cover the entire finger-
> board for any one key.

**Significance (p. 9).** This tells the student that mastering all five patterns yields complete neck coverage, establishing why all five must be learned.

## Modifying patterns for minor

> the relative harmonic minor scales can be quickly
> learned by simply sharping the fifth scale step of the diatonic scales, which is the same as
> __sharping the seventh of the relative minor scales.

**Significance (p. 9).** This provides the single-note modification rule that derives harmonic minor from the already-learned diatonic patterns, dramatically reducing new memorization.

## Arpeggios within scale pattern

> arpeggios will be easier to handle if they are played within a scale pattern that cor
> responds to the key center from which the chord is derived, e.g. the progression Dm7,
> Gm7, Cm7, F7 is a Ill, VI, Il, V Progression in the key of Bb. Therefore, one would
> select one of the five patterns for that key and play the arpeggios in that pettern.

**Significance (p. 9).** This gives a concrete worked example showing how to unify arpeggio playing within the key-center/pattern framework rather than treating each chord independently.

## Tonics circled for targeting

> Each pattern shown here starts with the lowest available in the range of the pattern going
> to the highest, (not from tonic to tonic). The tonics are circled for clear visual targeting.

**Significance (p. 9).** This explains the unconventional range layout of the diagrams and directs the student's attention to the circled tonics as orientation anchors within each pattern.

## Visual imprint priority

> The most important thing here is to develop = strong visual imprint of the pattern itself,
> coupled with the tone sequence it produces (the sound) with the view that when improvising,
> ae fingerings may be open to change at any time.

**Significance (p. 10).** This establishes the hierarchy of learning goals — visual/auditory pattern internalization over mechanical fingering adherence — which governs how the student should practice.
