## common tones defined

> A common tone is a note that is common to two or more chords, a very simple technique
> but not to be overlooked, i.e. when playing non-stop eighth notes, or triplets, etc., it
> is not necessary to keep the line moving at all times. The line can be flattened out ---
> a nice relief from excessive vertical movement.

**Significance (p. 11).** Defines the common-tone technique and explicitly instructs the student to use sustained notes as a relief device rather than constant melodic motion.

## scales create smooth lines

> Create smooth rolling lines.

**Significance (p. 11).** States the core functional purpose of scale usage in line construction before presenting each scale type.

**Significance (p. 12).** Distinguishes harmonic minor from natural minor by specifying its unique half-step intervals, essential for correct scale construction.

## melodic minor ascending/descending rule

> The Relative Melodic Minor Scale (with } steps between the 2nd/3rd and 7th/8th scale steps
> ascending and reverting back to the natural minor descending. NOTE: The ascending and

**Significance (p. 12).** Presents the traditional ascending/descending rule but immediately qualifies it with a practical override based on bar harmony, teaching harmonic context over rote rule-following.

## diminished scale usage

> The Diminished Scale (symetrical, consecutive whole step and half steps) most commonly
> used over dominant 'th chords or diminished chords.

**Significance (p. 12).** Defines the diminished scale's symmetrical construction and specifies the chord contexts where it is applied, giving the student both structure and usage guidance.

## whole tone scale usage

> The Whole Tone Scale (symetrical, consecutive whole steps) most commonly used over
> dominant 7th chords.

**Significance (p. 12).** Defines the whole-tone scale's symmetrical structure and pins its idiomatic use to dominant 7th chords.

## major pentatonic scale definition

> The Major Pentatonic Scale (same as diatonic major with the active tones 3rd and 7th
> left out). When harmonized creates inversions of a Major 6/9 chord, with little gravi-
> tational pull toward any key center "depending on the usage".

**Significance (p. 13).** Defines the pentatonic scale by subtraction from the major scale and explains its ambiguous tonal gravity, which is the key reason it is useful across multiple harmonic contexts.

## arpeggios defined and contrasted

> Arpeggios are specifically chords, broken up into single notes. They are effective in out-
> lining the bar harmony and offer a nice contrast to scale movement as they create smooth
> rolling lines, at sharper angles than scales.

**Significance (p. 13).** Defines arpeggios functionally and positions them relative to scales — same smooth quality but at steeper melodic angles — establishing the contrast the student should hear and exploit.

## interval skips dramatic effect

> Interval skips create sharp, dramatic lines. An interval is the distance between any two
> notes, Large interval skips can be a valuable tool for creating interesting lines and can
> have a dramatic effect in breaking up the monotomy of excessive scale movement.

**Significance (p. 13).** Identifies interval skips as the primary antidote to scale monotony and names the dramatic quality they produce, motivating the student to practice them.

## scale intervals avoid parallel

> Any
> sequence of interval skips, 3rds, 7ths, S9ths, etc., can easily be applied to a key center
> by playing scale intervals that take into account the half steps of the scale, thus, resulting
> in combinations of major and minor 3rds or major and minor 7ths (not, parallel 3rds, 7ths,
> etc.)

**Significance (p. 13).** Instructs the student to derive interval skips diatonically rather than in parallel, which is the critical technique for keeping skips in key.

## breaking up scale-like playing

> A most common complaint of beginning improvisors is that their playing tends to become
> too scale-like. One solution to this could be to stop playing things that "sound" like
> scales, for example, as an exercise; don't play more than 3 or 4 scale tones in the same
> direction, e.g. this type of scale movement creates interval skips, small and large, thus
> breaking up the scale effect.

**Significance (p. 13).** Names the most common beginner improvisation flaw and gives a concrete, actionable rule — no more than 3 or 4 consecutive scale tones in one direction — to overcome it.
