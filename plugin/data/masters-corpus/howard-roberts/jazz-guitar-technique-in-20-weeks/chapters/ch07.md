## blame attribution left/right hand

> A most common mistake is to blame the left hand when the right hand is at
> fault, or vice versa. Watch out for this.

**Significance (p. 17).** Directs students to diagnose technique problems on the correct hand before attempting a fix, preventing wasted practice effort.

## memorizing chord changes

> Get the progression off the paper and into your head as soon as possible.
> Memorizing the chord changes allows you to focus all of your attention on what
> you are playing rather than reading the music.

**Significance (p. 17).** States the explicit pedagogical reason for memorization: freeing attention from notation so it can be directed at execution quality.

## program adherence requirement

> The objectives put forth in this book are attainable only if the reader follows
> each step of the program without deviation from the schedule (that means no
>
> missed days, hours shortened, time frames disobeyed, key centers missed,
> introducing hammer-on's, pull-off's, before stated, playin tted eighth six-
> teenth notes instead of eighth notes, etc., etc.) If you do it, you get It.
> you don't, you don't.

**Significance (p. 17).** Sets the non-negotiable contract for the method: any deviation invalidates the expected results, making exact compliance the precondition for progress.

## growth plateau cycle explained

> In the Growth Process, we all go through periods in which we are activating new
> nerve junctures, both in the brain and throughout other parts of the body.
>
> This type of growth work draws a tremendous amount of systemic energies. The
> overall physical feeling is like pulling a train uphill, and the psychological
> spin-off is the feeling that we are getting nowhere. This will persist for some
> period of time. We then tend to go into a natural state of rest, "a plateau."
> During this rest period, we tend to feel that we have the world by the tail and
> great progress is taking place. Actually the reverse is true. The progress
>
> was made during the uphill climb, not during the rest period.

**Significance (p. 17).** Corrects the common misreading of felt progress vs. actual neurological progress, explaining why the difficult period is when real learning happens.

## environmental tuning interference

> your guitar may sound out of tume, which
> is certainly possible. However, keep in mind that it may actually be in tune with
> itself, but out of tune with an air conditioner, refrigerator motor, or traffic noises
> from outside. When your music sounds bad to you, the natural reflex is to stop
> playing. So before you blame yourself for a problem, make sure that you have
> accounted for elements in your environment that may be superimposing a
> subliminal orchestration over what you are playing. Learn to ferrot out the
>
> real problems.

**Significance (p. 18).** Teaches students to audit the practice environment before self-blaming, preventing the reflexive stop-playing response to ambient pitch interference.

## bio-cycles and practice regularity

> be aware of your bio-cycles. Every person has natural high and low points
> running in approximately monthly cycles. During low periods, you may feel very
> down and disqouraged, but this must not interrupt the regularity of your prac-
> tice routine,-as far as the project lessons in this book are concerned. You must
> continue to practice with controlled discipline, no matter how you feel about
> things from day to day.

**Significance (p. 18).** Distinguishes emotional state from practice obligation, establishing that bio-cycle lows are an expected variable that cannot justify skipping sessions.

## 21-day permanent imprinting threshold

> once you reach a given level of speed, and maintain playing
>
> at that level or faster, for approximately 21 days, on a daily basis, -- the
>
> ability acquired during that period of time will be permanently imprinted and
> assimilated by your nervous system. You could fay off for six months, come
> back and practice hard for about 2-3 weeks and it will come right back.

**Significance (p. 18).** Provides the specific duration target (21 days at tempo) that locks a skill into the nervous system, giving students a concrete milestone to aim for.

## group practice as accelerator

> An Accelerator -- form a group of other guitar players. Exchange solos and
> comping. Trade off in sequence. The group dynamics is superior to private
> study for a program of this sort.

**Significance (p. 18).** Identifies ensemble exchange as the single fastest accelerator for this program, elevating it above solo practice as the preferred mode.

## awkward tempos between feel zones

> when tunes are played with a specific feel,
> i.e. steady eighths or dotted eighths, sixteenths (shuffle) or triplets (12/8),
> etc., they tend to have slow, medium and fast tempos that are just right for
> that particular rhythm feel and any other tempos inbetween feel awkward and
> difficult to play on. This applies even to the most experienced players. You
> should expect that, during the process of building up your speed, you will
> encounter long periods when the tempo feels awful and, from time to time,
> points where the tempo is just right. Don't blame yourself -- just be aware
> of the real problem.

**Significance (p. 18).** Names the phenomenon of feel-specific tempo comfort zones so students recognize awkward mid-range tempos as a structural property of rhythm feels rather than personal failure.

## eighth notes only rule

> Play only eighth notes, continuous and uninterrupted. No rests. No phrasing.
> No hammer-on's. No pull-offts. No other ornamentation. "Nothing" but steady
> eighth notes.

**Significance (p. 19).** Establishes the foundational constraint of the six-week project: all variables are eliminated except note value, isolating picking mechanics as the sole focus.

## alternate picking requirement

> Use Alternate Picking only. No two strokes in the same direction, i.e. strive
> to make up strokes sound as strong as down strokes.

**Significance (p. 19).** Mandates alternate picking and sets the qualitative standard (upstroke = downstroke strength) that defines correct execution of the technique.

## metronome as tracker not target

> Do not set your metronome to a desired speed and try to rise to meet it. Rather,
> use the metronome only to track your progress. Keep a daily record of your
> tempos in the boxes provided. (NOTE: Your tempo may vary slower or faster
> from day to day. This is to be expected.)

**Significance (p. 19).** Reverses the conventional metronome usage from goal-setter to progress-recorder, removing the performance anxiety of chasing a fixed target.

## key center analysis flexibility

> The key centers bracketed in the chord progressions represent only one analysis
> of the progression. Other views may be applied as well. For example, you will
> encounter key center brackets in which a given chord could be viewed as a III
> or V1 chord of one key, but indicated as a I! chord of another. In these situations,
> one's individual preference can prevail. It's just a matter of where you want the
> change of tonality to occur. Also, there are almost unlimited scale and chord
> substitution possibilities. Feel free to use any harmonic devices at your disposal.
> If they're right, they will sound right and if they're wrong, you will know it
> immediately.

**Significance (p. 19).** Grants harmonic latitude within the structured program, teaching that functional ear-testing ('if they're right, they will sound right') is the final arbiter of substitution choices.

## tempo objectives vs. accuracy

> The week end tempo objectives shown at the top of each project lesson are
> scheduled to increase by two metronome points daily. It may be good to remember
> that these are only objectives. Care should be taken not to sacrifice accuracy
> and precision in order to meet the tempo goals.. These will always come In time.
> "The tempo objective plan is Symmetrical, but the learning curve is not.

**Significance (p. 19).** Explicitly subordinates speed goals to accuracy and warns that the symmetric tempo schedule does not match the asymmetric reality of skill acquisition.

## legato and steady time

> Play Legato. Hold each note as long as possible.
>
> |. Great attention should be given to holding steady time. Do not rush or drag
> the tempo.

**Significance (p. 19).** Pairs note sustain (legato) with metronomic steadiness as the two non-negotiable quality standards for executing the project lessons.
