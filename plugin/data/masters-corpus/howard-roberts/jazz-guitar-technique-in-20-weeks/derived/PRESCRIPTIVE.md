## Any Chord Quality

These lessons form the universal substrate of Roberts' method — equipment, mechanics, tempo management, practice psychology, and line-construction principles that apply regardless of the chord quality under the improviser's line. Mastery here precedes harmonic specificity; no scale selection or chord-specific device will compensate for a poorly synchronized right hand or an inconsistent practice schedule.

### Equipment & Setup

The physical rig is pedagogy: resistance builds strength, and feedback loops (metronome, recorder, timer) externalize the three things a student cannot reliably self-assess in real time.

- **String gauge and action:** Use medium-heavy strings (.012 minimum first string) and medium-high action. Narrow-gauge strings cannot return to center fast enough for predictable right-hand accuracy. *(Ch. 1, p. 3)*
- **Pick selection:** Use a medium-sized, medium-to-heavy celluloid pick. Avoid very large or oddly shaped picks. *(Ch. 1, p. 4) [proscriptive]*
- **Three mandatory non-guitar tools:** A metronome, a tape recorder, and a timer are required. They externalize the method's three feedback loops — time, self-monitoring, and session discipline. *(Ch. 1, p. 4)*
- **Note-clarity standard:** After setup, verify that every note rings loud and clear across the entire fingerboard. *(Ch. 1, p. 3)*

### Foundational Axiom

- **Speed is a byproduct of accuracy:** Every practice decision — tempo targets, equipment choices, session structure — is downstream of a prior commitment to precision. *(Ch. 1, p. 3)*

### Right-Hand Mechanics

The right hand is Roberts' primary concern: it is the most common limiter, the hardest habit to break, and the hand most damaged by fixed anchors and tension.

- **Right hand as primary limiter:** Once a poor picking style becomes habitual it is extremely difficult to change — complete retraining is frequently required. *(Ch. 2, p. 5)*
- **Flexibility as governing principle:** The ability to adapt the right hand to a variety of moves is the key to longevity. *(Ch. 2, p. 5)*
- **Avoid fixed anchor systems:** Elbow anchors, wrist anchors, and little-finger grip on the pick guard create a hard speed ceiling. *(Ch. 2, p. 5) [proscriptive]*
- **Keep the arm loose:** Any rigidness in the picking leverage system stops speed cold at fast tempos. *(Ch. 2, p. 5) [proscriptive]*

### Left-Hand Mechanics

Left-hand mechanics serve synchronization and intonation, not strength alone.

- **Thumb position:** The left-hand thumb rides along the approximate center of the back of the neck. *(Ch. 2, p. 5)*
- **Perpendicular fretting:** Fingers operate straight up and down on strings, like piano hammers, at a right angle to the fingerboard. *(Ch. 2, p. 5)*
- **Minimal finger lift:** Lift fingers only high enough to avoid noise when moving — not higher. *(Ch. 2, p. 5)*
- **Flat wrist posture:** Keep the left wrist fairly flat. Severe arching produces undesirable strains and risks injury. *(Ch. 2, p. 5) [proscriptive]*

### Left-Right Synchronization

Synchronization is binary: either both hands attack simultaneously and produce one sound, or they do not.

- **Simultaneous attack — no flams:** Left and right hands must attack every note at exactly the same moment. *(Ch. 2, p. 5)*
- **Slow-play diagnosis:** To detect flams, play notes very slowly at all positions across the full fingerboard. *(Ch. 2, p. 5)*
- **Session warm-up:** Begin every practice session with a 5-minute slow warm-up covering low, high, and medium register. High-neck notes travel much farther than low notes; this differential causes synchronization failures if not addressed. *(Ch. 6, p. 16)*
- **Systematic picking-direction isolation:** When diagnosing flams: play very slowly listening for uneven attacks; then use all upstrokes; then use alternate strokes; then reverse the beat assignment. *(Ch. 6, p. 16)*

### Diagnostic Protocol

- **Correct blame attribution:** Before attempting any technical fix, identify which hand is at fault. *(Ch. 6, p. 17; Ch. 7, p. 17) [proscriptive]*

### Fingerboard Navigation

Key-center thinking reduces harmonic complexity from chord-by-chord decisions to pattern navigation across five movable shapes.

- **Think in key centers, not individual chords:** The dominant seventh chord is the most decisive harmonic landmark — it appears only once per major key. *(Ch. 3, pp. 6–7)*
- **Move to the pattern immediately:** When a new key center arrives, move your hand to a convenient fingering pattern for that scale. *(Ch. 3, p. 8)*
- **Five movable patterns cover the neck:** Learn all five movable finger-per-fret scale patterns for any key. Tonics circled in each pattern for visual targeting. *(Ch. 3, pp. 8–9)*
- **Three and only three moves between patterns:** Position skips, stretching to the adjacent pattern, or sliding on the half step. *(Ch. 3, p. 10)*
- **Visual-auditory imprint is the durable goal:** Develop a strong visual imprint of each pattern coupled with its sound. *(Ch. 3, p. 10)*
- **Ear-based playing is the end, analysis is the means:** The ultimate goal of key-center study is to play changes by ear. *(Ch. 3, p. 8)*
- **Arpeggios belong inside the key-center pattern:** Play arpeggios within the scale pattern corresponding to the key center, not as isolated chord shapes. *(Ch. 3, p. 9)*

### Scale Selection (Universal)

- **Derive harmonic minor from diatonic patterns:** Raise the fifth scale step of the diatonic major (= raising the seventh of the relative minor). *(Ch. 3, p. 9)*
- **Treat melodic minor ascending/descending rules as a technicality:** The formality may be disregarded in favor of the existing bar harmony. *(Ch. 4, p. 12)*

### Line Construction

Roberts' line-construction doctrine resolves to two principles: don't play scales like scales, and use common tones deliberately.

- **Common tones as deliberate relief:** Sustaining a common tone flattens the line and provides relief from excessive vertical movement. *(Ch. 4, p. 11)*
- **Arpeggios as a distinct color:** Arpeggios outline bar harmony and contrast scale movement by moving at steeper melodic angles. *(Ch. 4, p. 13)*
- **Interval skips break scalar monotony:** Large interval skips create sharp, dramatic lines. *(Ch. 4, p. 13)*
- **Derive all interval skips diatonically:** Skip sequences must respect the scale's half steps, producing mixed major/minor intervals — not parallel ones. *(Ch. 4, p. 13) [proscriptive]*
- **No more than 3–4 consecutive scale tones in one direction:** Exceeding this produces lines that sound purely scale-like. *(Ch. 4, p. 13) [proscriptive]*

### Picking Mechanics

- **Alternate picking only, with dynamic equality:** Upstrokes must sound as strong as downstrokes. *(Ch. 7, p. 19; Ch. 8, p. 19)*
- **Eighth notes only, Weeks 1–13:** Continuous, uninterrupted eighth notes — no rests, no phrasing, no hammer-ons, no pull-offs. *(Ch. 7, p. 19) [proscriptive]*
- **Play legato, hold steady time:** Hold each note as long as possible. Never rush or drag. *(Ch. 7, p. 19)*

### Legato Technique

- **Hammer-ons, pull-offs, and glissandi are a late-program tool:** Introduced only in Weeks 15–17. Multiple pitches per single pick stroke produce dramatic apparent-speed gains. *(Ch. 5, p. 15)*

### Harmonic Analysis

- **Key-center brackets are one valid analysis, not the only one:** Substitutions and alternative key interpretations are freely available. If it's right, it sounds right. *(Ch. 7, p. 19; Ch. 8, p. 19)*

### Memorization

- **Get changes off the page:** Memorize chord changes as quickly as possible. Reading splits cognitive attention away from real-time musical decision-making. *(Ch. 6, p. 17; Ch. 7, p. 17)*

### Improvisation Construction

- **Melody sketch over changes:** Sketch a simple structural melody over the changes using half/quarter notes. *(Ch. 6, p. 16)*
- **Question and answer as primary form:** Q&A is the foundational structural pattern for building a solo line. *(Ch. 6, p. 16)*
- **Sing before playing:** Vocalize the intended line before executing it on the instrument. *(Ch. 6, p. 16)*
- **Break repeated licks in the flow, not by stopping:** Recognize a repeated lick in real time and play something different on the next pass. *(Ch. 6, p. 16) [proscriptive]*
- **Technique serves spontaneous invention:** Model solos are reference material only — not blueprints. *(Ch. 9, p. 27)*

### Tempo Management

- **Use the metronome as a recorder, not a target:** Do not set the metronome to a desired speed and try to rise to meet it. *(Ch. 7, p. 19; Ch. 8, p. 19) [proscriptive]*
- **Never sacrifice accuracy for tempo goals:** The tempo objective plan is symmetrical; the learning curve is not. *(Ch. 7, p. 19; Ch. 8, p. 19) [proscriptive]*
- **21-day permanent imprinting threshold:** ~21 consecutive daily sessions at tempo permanently imprints the ability. *(Ch. 7, p. 18)*
- **Awkward tempos are structural, not personal:** Every rhythmic feel has natural slow/medium/fast zones; tempos between zones feel wrong even to experienced players. *(Ch. 7, p. 18)*

### Practice Environment

- **Audit the room before blaming technique:** Air conditioners, refrigerator motors, and traffic can superimpose subliminal pitch references. *(Ch. 7, p. 18)*

### Practice Psychology

- **The growth plateau cycle:** The feeling of getting nowhere signals active neurological development. The feeling of great progress signals a rest plateau. *(Ch. 6, p. 17; Ch. 7, p. 17)*
- **Bio-cycles are real — practice through them:** Monthly emotional lows must not interrupt the regularity of practice. *(Ch. 7, p. 18)*

### Practice Mode

- **Noodling counts:** Informal playing (e.g., watching television) is a valid learning mode. *(Ch. 6, p. 16)*
- **Group practice is the fastest accelerator:** Trading solos and comping in sequence is superior to private study. *(Ch. 7, p. 18)*

### Program Structure & Discipline

- **Three sequential phases:** (1) Weeks 1–6 duple time; (2) Weeks 8–13 triplet time; (3) Weeks 15–17 tempo solidification with legato. *(Ch. 5, pp. 14–15)*
- **50-minute sessions, six days per week, ♩ = 126 target.** *(Ch. 5, p. 14)*
- **Exact compliance is non-negotiable:** No missed days, no shortened sessions, no out-of-sequence technique introductions. *(Ch. 6, p. 17; Ch. 7, p. 17) [proscriptive]*
- **Never skip a day:** Irregular practice is hazardous to progress, not merely suboptimal. *(Ch. 8, p. 19) [proscriptive]*

### Creative Development

- **Transpose into new keys:** Transposition forces new fingering patterns and stimulates fresh melodic approaches. *(Ch. 5, p. 14)*

## Dominant 7th

The dominant seventh receives two distinct scale tools in Roberts' system: the whole-tone scale for floating gravity-free quality, and the diminished scale (shared with the diminished chord) for tension-rich symmetry.

- **Whole-tone scale over dominant 7th chords:** Symmetrical consecutive whole steps; removes gravitational pull toward any specific tonic. *(Ch. 4, p. 12)*
- **Diminished scale over dominant 7th (shared with diminished chord):** Alternating whole/half steps, symmetrical. *(Ch. 4, p. 12)*

## Major 6/9

The major pentatonic occupies a unique structural position: removing the 4th and 7th degrees makes it harmonically lightweight and contextually portable. Its relationship to the major 6/9 chord is definitional — it is the horizontal spelling of that chord.

- **Major pentatonic creates major 6/9 harmonization:** Diatonic major with 4th and 7th omitted creates inversions of a major 6/9 chord when harmonized. Little gravitational pull; usable across multiple harmonic contexts. *(Ch. 4, p. 13)*
