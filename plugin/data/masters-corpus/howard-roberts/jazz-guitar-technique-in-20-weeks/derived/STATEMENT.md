# Jazz Guitar Technique in 20 Weeks — Statement of Outputs

## Overview

Howard Roberts' *Jazz Guitar Technique in 20 Weeks* is a rigidly disciplined, systems-driven method for building jazz improvisation technique on guitar. Its animating axiom — announced in Chapter 1 and restated through every subsequent chapter — is that **speed is a byproduct of accuracy**, never a goal in itself. Every structural choice in the book flows downstream from this premise: equipment standards, session scheduling, metronome doctrine, and the 20-week tempo target are all consequent on a prior, non-negotiable commitment to precision. The book refuses to let the student chase velocity; it instead builds the physical and cognitive conditions under which velocity becomes inevitable.

The method's arc moves through four domains in sequence. Chapters 1–2 address physical setup: the instrument, the tools, the hands, and the synchronization law that governs both. Chapters 3–4 build harmonic and melodic vocabulary: key-center analysis, the five movable fingerboard patterns, six scale types, arpeggios, interval skips, and common tones. Chapters 5–6 establish the structural program and the psychological framework required to survive it. Chapters 7–9 deliver the disciplinary fine print — the eighth-notes-only rule, the alternate-picking requirement, the 21-day imprinting threshold, the growth plateau cycle, and the diagnostic protocols for identifying and correcting failure. The book's closing move is a declaration of creative purpose: all technical training exists solely to enable **spontaneous invention** of the student's own musical ideas.

Three feedback devices — metronome, tape recorder, and timer — externalize the method's core monitoring loops and are treated as non-negotiable equipment alongside strings and picks. The metronome is never set to a desired speed as a target; it records where the student actually is. The tape recorder surfaces what the student cannot hear in real time. The timer enforces session structure. Together they constitute a self-monitoring system that removes subjective self-assessment from the practice loop and replaces it with objective evidence.

The book's deepest pedagogical claim is psychological: the subjective feeling of getting nowhere during practice is the reliable signal of active neurological development, while the feeling of great progress signals a rest plateau. This inversion of ordinary intuition is not motivational rhetoric — it is load-bearing doctrine that explains why the program demands exact compliance, forbids skipped days, and refuses to let the student deviate from the sequence. The 20-week structure is not a suggestion; it is a contract whose terms must be met in full for the promised results to obtain.

## Systems

### Physical Mechanics System

The Physical Mechanics System governs the body-and-instrument foundation that must be in place before any musical content is addressed. It has four members: Equipment Setup, Right-Hand Technique, Left-Hand Technique, and Left-Right Hand Synchronization.

**Equipment Setup** (Ch. 1) specifies medium-heavy strings (.012 minimum first string), medium-high action, and medium-to-heavy celluloid picks — not chosen for tone, but to provide the resistance that develops left-hand strength and right-hand control. Three non-guitar tools are equally mandatory: a metronome, a tape recorder, and a timer. The **note clarity standard** — every note must ring loud and clear across the entire fingerboard — functions as the diagnostic criterion for whether the setup is correct.

**Right-Hand Technique** (Ch. 2) is governed by a single principle: flexibility. Rigid anchor systems — elbow anchors, wrist anchors, pick-guard rests — are explicitly forbidden because they become hard speed ceilings as tempos increase. The required picking method is strict alternate picking, and the mechanical goal is dynamic equality between upstrokes and downstrokes, not merely directional alternation (Ch. 7–8).

**Left-Hand Technique** (Ch. 2) requires perpendicular finger angle (described as hammer-like, analogous to piano key strikes), thumb centered on the back of the neck, minimal finger lift to balance noise control against efficiency, and a flat wrist posture that reduces strain and enables consistency across the full fingerboard.

**Left-Right Hand Synchronization** (Ch. 2) is the system's non-negotiable law: both hands must attack each note simultaneously. Any flam — whether the left finger arrives first or the pick arrives first — produces two audible sounds and constitutes a failure state. Diagnosis requires playing very slowly across the full fingerboard to make flams audible, and correction requires addressing the correct hand (see Blame Attribution rule below).

The system operates under five traversal rules. The **Accuracy Precedes Speed** rule (Ch. 8) states that tempo goals must never be pursued at the expense of precision; speed is attainable only as a consequence of accuracy already in place. The **Flexibility Over Fixed Anchor** rule (Ch. 2) prohibits any right-hand anchoring system that restricts movement. The **No-Flam Synchronization Law** (Ch. 2) makes perfect simultaneous hand attack non-negotiable and prescribes slow-play diagnosis as the remediation protocol. The **Alternate Picking Stroke Equality** rule (Ch. 7) requires that upstrokes equal downstrokes in volume and clarity. The **Correct-Hand Blame Attribution** rule (Ch. 6) mandates that before any fix is attempted, the practitioner correctly identify which hand is at fault — a discipline Roberts singles out as frequently violated.

One phase-gated modification rule governs the system: **Legato Techniques Unlocked at Week 15** (Ch. 5, Ch. 7). Hammer-ons, pull-offs, and glissandi are forbidden during Weeks 1–13, because they obscure picking mechanics. They are introduced in Weeks 15–17 precisely because they allow multiple pitches per pick stroke, at which point that economy of motion becomes a speed multiplier rather than a diagnostic distortion.

### Fingerboard Harmony System

The Fingerboard Harmony System organizes the entire guitar neck through key-center analysis and five movable scale patterns. It treats chord progressions as belonging to key centers rather than as sequences of independent chords, and it equips the student with a complete melodic toolkit to improvise within those centers. The system has three members: Key Center Analysis, Five Movable Scale Patterns, and Melodic Toolkit.

**Key Center Analysis** (Ch. 3) establishes that diatonic harmony is generated by stacking a major or minor scale in thirds, and that most chord progressions belong to a single identifiable key. The student learns to identify that key analytically — the dominant seventh chord is named as a useful shortcut — but the goal is ultimately to hear the key center rather than calculate it. Arpeggios and scales are deployed within the key-center frame rather than changed chord-by-chord.

**Five Movable Scale Patterns** (Ch. 3) cover the full fingerboard for any key using a finger-per-fret left-hand mechanic. Tonics are circled within each pattern as visual targets. Patterns are connected by exactly three moves: position skips, stretching to an adjacent pattern, or sliding on the half step between patterns. Harmonic minor patterns are derived from already-learned diatonic patterns by raising the fifth scale step — a modification rule that reduces new learning to a single alteration (Ch. 3).

**Melodic Toolkit** (Ch. 4) comprises six scale types (diatonic major, harmonic minor, melodic minor, diminished, whole-tone, and major pentatonic), arpeggios as chord-outlining lines that move at steeper angles than scalar runs, interval skips derived diatonically, and common tones as a relief device from constant motion.

Six traversal rules govern how the student moves through the system. The **Key-Center-First Navigation** rule (Ch. 3) requires selecting one of the five patterns for the current key center and improvising within that region before moving on. The **Three-Move Pattern Connection** rule (Ch. 3) restricts movement between patterns to position skips, stretching, or half-step slides — the three and only three sanctioned transitions. The **Diatonic Interval Skip Derivation** rule (Ch. 4) requires that interval skips be taken from the scale's actual half steps, producing mixed major and minor intervals rather than parallel ones, thereby preserving tonality. The **Consecutive Scale Tone Ceiling** rule (Ch. 4) prohibits more than 3–4 scale tones in the same direction before the line must change direction or introduce a skip — the primary structural antidote to sounding scale-bound. The **Common Tone Relief** rule (Ch. 4) validates sustained common tones as a legitimate device for flattening melodic contour and providing rest from vertical movement. The **Ear as Final Harmonic Arbiter** rule (Ch. 7) grants latitude for chord substitution and alternative key-center interpretations, with the ear as the final arbiter: if the analysis sounds right, it is right.

One creative modification rule completes the system: **Transposition as Creative Stimulus** (Ch. 5). Repeating a project lesson in a transposed key forces new fingering patterns and consistently generates fresh melodic approaches, making transposition a practical tool for breaking habitual thinking.

### Practice Discipline System

The Practice Discipline System governs the structural, psychological, and scheduling requirements for executing the 20-week program. It is the system that holds the other two together under load. Its three members are Session Structure, Psychological Framework, and Improvisation Ethos.

**Session Structure** (Ch. 5) specifies 50-minute daily sessions, six consecutive days per week, one day off. The program divides into three phases: duple time (Weeks 1–6, eighth notes and subdivisions), triple time (Weeks 8–13, triplet feels), and tempo solidification with legato techniques (Weeks 15–17). The terminal tempo target is ♪ = 126. The appendix's numbered reference lines (1-A, 1-B, etc.) correspond to each project lesson and provide notated musical content grounding the program's abstract structure.

**Psychological Framework** (Ch. 6–7) is organized around the growth plateau cycle: the counterintuitive principle that subjective struggle is the reliable signal of active neurological development, while felt fluency signals a rest period. Students are explicitly warned not to quit when progress feels stalled, because that feeling is the learning. The 21-day imprinting threshold (Ch. 7) specifies that maintaining a given tempo for approximately 21 consecutive days permanently locks that ability into the nervous system as a motor reflex — not a temporary skill, but a permanent one.

**Improvisation Ethos** (Ch. 9) closes the system's conceptual arc: all technique training exists to remove obstacles between the student's musical imagination and its immediate realization. Model solos are reference material and source material, not prescriptions. The ethos is stated explicitly as "every man for himself" in creative improvisation.

Six traversal rules govern the system. **Absolute Schedule Adherence** (Ch. 6, Ch. 8) demands exact compliance: no missed days, no shortened sessions, no out-of-sequence introduction of techniques. **21-Day Permanent Imprinting Threshold** (Ch. 7) specifies the minimum continuous repetition required to convert a tempo into a motor reflex. **Metronome as Progress Tracker, Not Target** (Ch. 7, Ch. 8) prohibits setting the metronome to a desired speed and attempting to reach it; instead, the metronome is set to where the student can play accurately, and that reading is recorded as evidence of progress. **Growth Plateau Cycle Invert Subjective Signal** (Ch. 6) requires the student to interpret the feeling of getting nowhere as a positive signal and continue. **Technique Serves Spontaneous Invention** (Ch. 9) frames all technical work as instrumental to creative freedom, never as an end in itself. **Memorize Changes to Free Attention** (Ch. 6) makes chord memorization non-negotiable: as long as changes are read from the page, cognitive attention is consumed by notation rather than available for real-time musical decision-making.

Two modification rules extend the system. **Group Practice as Phase Accelerator** (Ch. 7) identifies ensemble work — trading solos and comping with other guitarists — as superior to solo practice for this program, and recommends it as an accelerant when available. **Line Construction Entry-Point Tools** (Ch. 6) provides three structured approaches for when improvisation stalls: melody sketch over changes (pre-planning a structural scaffold), question-and-answer form (the foundational call-and-response improvisational pattern), and sing before playing (embedding melodic ideas through the ear before the hand acts).

## Method / Practice Discipline

The 20-week program is structured as a sequence of timed, recorded, metronomically tracked 50-minute sessions, six days per week, progressing through three phases toward a terminal tempo of ♪ = 126. The method enforces its constraints not through willpower but through externalized feedback devices: the metronome records tempo honestly, the tape recorder surfaces what the player cannot hear in real time, and the timer ends the session at the scheduled moment regardless of subjective satisfaction.

The session's technical constraints during Weeks 1–13 are severe: eighth notes only, alternate picking only, no hammer-ons, no pull-offs, no ornamentation of any kind. These restrictions are not punitive; they isolate picking mechanics so that synchronization failures are unambiguous and diagnosable. The eighth-notes-only rule removes rhythmic variables; the alternate-picking requirement removes technical escape routes. What remains is the raw signal of whether both hands are synchronized and whether the pick is producing equally strong upstrokes and downstrokes.

The method's central diagnostic protocol is slow play: when synchronization is in question, the remedy is not more repetition at speed but extreme deceleration across the full fingerboard until flams become audible. Before any fix is attempted, the practitioner must identify the correct hand. This blame-attribution discipline prevents the common error of drilling the wrong hand.

Program adherence is framed not as motivational advice but as a structural requirement. Skipping a day is described as hazardous to progress (Ch. 8), and deviation from the sequence — introducing legato techniques before Week 15, for instance — undermines the phase logic the program depends on. The 21-day imprinting threshold makes clear why: the nervous system requires approximately 21 consecutive days of a given tempo to lock it in as a permanent motor reflex. A missed day potentially resets that counter.

The growth plateau cycle is the method's primary psychological tool. Because subjective struggle reliably signals neurological growth while felt fluency signals a rest plateau, the student is instructed to interpret difficulty as confirmation that the program is working. This inversion of ordinary intuition is what makes the iron contract of exact compliance sustainable: the student is not asked to push through difficulty despite not progressing — they are told that difficulty is progress.

Chord changes must be memorized and removed from the page as quickly as possible. As long as the chart is being read, cognitive attention is consumed by notation rather than available for musical decision-making. Memorization is not a convenience; it is the prerequisite for real improvisation.

## Characteristic Moves

The following moves constitute the method's core generative vocabulary — the specific techniques Roberts prescribes for building improvised lines within the system's constraints.

**Key-center bracketing.** Rather than analyzing each chord in a progression independently, the player identifies the governing key, selects one of five movable scale patterns for that key, and improvises within that region. When the key center changes, the hand moves to the appropriate pattern for the new key (Ch. 3).

**Three pattern-connection moves.** Movement between the five scale patterns is restricted to exactly three techniques: position skips (jumping to a non-adjacent pattern), stretching (reaching from one pattern to its neighbor without shifting), and sliding on a half step between patterns (Ch. 3). No other transitions are sanctioned.

**Consecutive scale tone ceiling.** No more than 3–4 consecutive scale tones may move in the same direction before the line must change direction or introduce a skip. This rule is the primary structural device for preventing scalar monotony (Ch. 4).

**Diatonically derived interval skips.** When the line introduces a skip, that skip must be derived from the scale's actual half steps — producing a mix of major and minor intervals rather than parallel ones. This preserves tonality while breaking the scalar surface (Ch. 4).

**Arpeggio lines.** Arpeggios are deployed within the current scale pattern as chord-outlining lines that move at steeper angles than scalar runs, creating contour contrast without leaving the key-center frame (Ch. 4).

**Common tone suspension.** When the line needs relief from constant directional motion, a common tone — a pitch shared by two or more chords in the progression — is sustained or repeated. This is treated as a legitimate compositional device, not a placeholder (Ch. 4).

**Question-and-answer form.** The foundational structure for solo construction: a melodic phrase poses a question; the answering phrase resolves it. This two-part call-and-response pattern is named as the entry-point form for building a solo line when improvisation stalls (Ch. 6).

**Melody sketch over changes.** Before improvising freely, the player pre-plans a structural melodic scaffold across the chord changes — a skeleton that provides direction without locking in specific notes (Ch. 6).

**Sing before playing.** A melodic idea is sung or internally heard before the hand acts on it, embedding the idea through the ear-to-hand pathway rather than discovering it through fingerboard exploration (Ch. 6).

**Legato acceleration (Weeks 15–17 only).** Hammer-ons, pull-offs, and glissandi are introduced in the final program phase as speed multipliers: multiple pitches produced per pick stroke, increasing apparent velocity without increasing picking effort (Ch. 5, Ch. 7). These techniques are forbidden in earlier phases.

**Transposition as creative reset.** Repeating any project lesson in a transposed key forces new fingering patterns and consistently generates fresh melodic approaches, breaking habitual lick patterns (Ch. 5).

**Noodling as low-pressure integration.** Informal, unstructured playing — called noodling — is validated as a legitimate practice mode for integrating vocabulary at low cognitive load, distinct from the structured project-lesson sessions (Ch. 6).
