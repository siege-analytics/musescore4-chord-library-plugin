# Prescriptive lessons -- jerry-coker x elements-of-the-jazz-language

## min7

Coker treats the minor seventh chord as the foundational II-chord entry point for nearly every device in the book: change-running, the 7-3 resolution (as source), the dorian bebop scale, and the bebop lick all land here first before extending to other chord qualities. The dorian bebop scale is the most characteristically opinionated prescription -- Coker documents that the added major 3rd directly contradicts the chord's minor quality yet appears in 99% of transcribed solos, making it non-negotiable. Beyond the single-chord devices, the min7 participates in CESH, harmonic generalization (tonic major and pentatonic), and the Back Door progression as the IV-7 chord, giving it the broadest multi-device coverage of any quality in the book.

- [ch.2 p.8-9] Change-running over II-7 is the foundational device: arpeggiate the chord to tune the ear, reinforce form, and generate pickup notes. The phrase need not begin on the root, may omit chord members, and may change direction freely.
- [ch.4 p.20 / ch.5 p.22] The b7 of the II-7 is the operative pitch in the 7-3 resolution, resolving down a half-step to the 3rd of the following V7. The 3rd of the II-7 is also drawn in because both the 3rd and b7 are the chord-defining tones.
- [ch.6 p.33 / ch.7 p.45] The dorian bebop scale (dorian + added major 3rd between scale degrees 3 and 4) is prescribed for min7 chords. The added note contradicts the chord's minor quality yet is documented in 99% of transcribed solos; even a 3-4 note fragment containing it implies the device.
- [ch.8 p.40 / ch.8 p.44] The bebop lick is defined as primarily a min7 and dom7 device. Learn it in isolation against tracks heavy in minor seventh and dominant seventh chords, then weave it into longer lines.
- [ch.12 p.74] CESH extends to II-7 chords beyond its canonical minor-chord-of-long-duration setting. Improvisers apply CESH on II-7, V7 (minor CESH built on the 5th of the V7), or the combined II-V.
- [ch.9 p.45] The tonic major scale covers D-7, G7, and C major together with no collision tones -- the simplest and safest harmonic generalization. Playing a C major scale or pattern over a D-7 G7 C progression produces no wrong notes.
- [ch.9 p.45] A single C pentatonic covers A-7, D-7, and G-7 (plus seven other chord types) with no collision tones. Pentatonic generalization is the safest available strategy for multi-chord spans.
- [ch.5 p.24] Practice 7-3 resolutions with exercise tracks and tunes that contain a large number of II-V cells. On stereo play-alongs, turn off the piano channel to test effectiveness of the resolutions without chordal support.
- [ch.6 p.32] Track AA substitutes the half-diminished chord for the minor seventh throughout, with root sequence identical to Track Y (C, F, Bb, Eb, Ab...). This prepares the minor II-V context where the II chord is half-diminished rather than plain minor seventh.
- [ch.17 p.119] When a Back Door substitution is chosen, the IV-7 chord carries specific melodic content: play CMAR or GBNF starting on the 9th of the BD substitute chord, or a bebop lick starting on the 4th of that chord.
- [ch.17 p.117] When II-V occupies a single measure (2 beats each chord), use one scale for the entire measure -- the dorian and mixolydian modes share identical pitch content, so no scale switch is needed mid-bar.

## dom7

Coker's teaching on the dominant seventh is the densest in the book: the chord receives the mixolydian bebop scale (with the anachronistic major 7 addition), the 3-b9 device exclusively, the tritone substitution, the back door and #II dim7 substitutes, and multiple harmonic generalizations. The pivot-note principle -- that the 3rd of V7 simultaneously closes the 7-3 resolution and opens the 3-b9 -- is the book's clearest example of device chaining and is presented as a structural feature of the language, not a coincidence. Root avoidance is the lone proscriptive teaching: the root of any dom7 chord is identified as the weakest tone and should not be over-used.

- [ch.4 p.20] The 3rd of V7 is the resolution target of the 7-3 device. The device is framed as voice-leading/melodic, not harmonic suspension: two notes imply two chords with maximum efficiency.
- [ch.5 p.26] The 3-b9 device is exclusive to the dominant seventh chord: melodic motion from the 3rd to the flatted 9th of the same chord, bidirectional, by leap or scalar fill. Chain it with 7-3 by using the 3rd of V7 as the shared pivot note.
- [ch.5 p.26] The 3rd of V7 is the pivot note linking 7-3 resolution and 3-b9: the 7-3 resolution ends on the 3rd; that same 3rd begins the 3-b9 motion. The overlap creates compound gestures larger than either device alone.
- [ch.6 p.33 / ch.7 p.45-46] The mixolydian bebop scale (mixolydian + chromatic note between scale degrees b7 and 1) is prescribed for dom7 chords. The added major 7 contradicts the chord's b7 quality yet appears in 99% of transcribed solos. Coltrane uses this pattern 21 times in an 8-chorus solo on a 16-bar tune.
- [ch.8 p.40 / ch.8 p.44] The bebop lick is primarily a dom7 and min7 device. Learn in isolation against tracks with a preponderance of minor seventh and dominant seventh chords, then integrate into longer lines.
- [ch.8 p.44] Applying the bebop lick over a dom7 while starting it on the root of the tritone-substituted chord is a gateway to altered dominant sounds before full structural understanding of the altered dominant is in place.
- [ch.12 p.74] CESH applies over V7 by constructing the minor CESH on the fifth of the dominant (e.g., G minor CESH over C7). This extends CESH beyond minor chords of long duration into the II-V motion.
- [ch.9 p.45] The V7 chord is covered by tonic major generalization (G7 uses G mixolydian = C major re-circulated from G). The C pentatonic also covers C7 and G7sus with no collision tones.
- [ch.15 p.82] Dominant seventh chords are the primary target for tritone substitution: substitute a dom7 chord whose root is a tritone away (e.g., F#7 for C7). The substitution sounds consonant because the listener's ear hears familiar precedent rather than conflict with the original V7.
- [ch.15 p.82] The IV-7 to bVII7 back door progression substitutes for V7. Prescribed melodic content on the bVII7: play CMAR or GBNF starting on the 9th of the BD substitute, or a bebop lick starting on the 4th.
- [ch.15 p.82] The #II dim7 substitutes for V7 because the ear hears the familiar precedent (its common role preceding I or III-7) rather than the conflict with V7. Otherwise anachronistic tones like the 4th and major 7th are rendered consonant by this precedent effect.
- [ch.6 p.31] Four practice contexts are prescribed for the 3-b9 device: (1) isolated dom7 chords, (2) dom7 chords moving in the cycle of fifths, (3) II-7 to V7 cells descending in whole steps, (4) II-7 to V7 alt. descending in whole steps.
- [ch.15 p.73] The Cry Me A River lick starts on the 13th of a dom7 chord. The lick fits five chord types without alteration; the 13th-of-dom7 entry is one of its highest-frequency uses.
- [ch.17 p.117] When a II-V compresses to two beats per chord in one measure, the dom7 is subsumed into a single shared scale: the notes of Bb dorian (for Bb-7) are identical to Eb mixolydian (for Eb7), so no scale switch occurs mid-bar.
- [ch.17 p.116] The root of any dom7 chord is explicitly identified as the weakest tone. Scales in the book are sometimes rooted off the chord root to discourage over-use of the root, which sounds simplistic and redundant.

## dom7alt

Coker treats the altered dominant as structurally near-identical to the tritone substitute, not as a separate chord category requiring its own scale vocabulary. The book's main pedagogical path to altered dominant fluency runs through the bebop lick (use it over the tritone sub root), the CMAR/GBNF licks (start on the +9), and the pentatonic (C pentatonic covers Gb7+5b9 cleanly). Track AA provides the systematic ear-training context by replacing every unaltered dominant in the cycle with the altered dominant.

- [ch.15 p.82] The altered dominant is defined as a dom7 chord containing +5, b9, +9, and +11. It is structurally near-identical to the tritone substitute; the two are interchangeable in Coker's system and share the abbreviation TT/AD in Appendix B annotations.
- [ch.8 p.44] The bebop lick is the gateway to altered dominant sounds: apply it over a dom7 by starting it on the root of the tritone-substituted chord. This implies the altered dominant without requiring full theoretical command of the chord.
- [ch.15 p.73] The Cry Me A River lick starts on the +9 of the altered dominant. Both CMAR and GBNF fit the altered dominant without alteration; practitioners must locate and memorize the +9 starting tone in each key.
- [ch.6 p.32] Track AA replaces every minor seventh chord with a half-diminished and every unaltered dominant with an altered dominant, using the same root sequence as Track Y (cycle of fifths). This trains the ear to hear and use altered dominants systematically across all keys.
- [ch.6 p.31] The 3-b9 device practice sequence includes "II-7 to V7 alt. descending in whole steps" as one of the four prescribed contexts, drilling the II-V motion with the altered dominant as the target chord.
- [ch.9 p.45] A C pentatonic covers Gb7 (+5, b9) -- the tritone substitute of C7 -- with no collision tones. This is the most harmonically distant of the ten chord types covered by a single pentatonic and demonstrates the device's particular value for handling altered dominants.
- [ch.17 p.117] The annotated "Urbane Blues" progression embeds two built-in tritone/altered dominant substitutions (measures 4 and 10), both flagged TT/AD, confirming structural equivalence of the altered dominant and its tritone substitute in Coker's annotation system.

## maj

Coker's treatment of the tonic major chord is comparatively lean: change-running establishes it as a landing point, the major bebop scale is prescribed (uniquely, without the anachronism present in the dorian and mixolydian variants), and the tonic major scale and C pentatonic both generalize cleanly over it. The CMAR lick (starting on the 4th) provides the primary stock-lick entry point.

- [ch.2 p.8-9] Change-running over the tonic major chord is the most fundamental improvisational device. The phrase may omit chord members, need not start on the root, and may ascend, descend, or do both.
- [ch.6 p.33 / ch.7 p.45] The major bebop scale adds a chromatic note between scale degrees 5 and 6. Unlike the dorian and mixolydian variants, this addition does not contradict the chord quality. A 3-4 note fragment containing the chromatic note is sufficient to imply bebop scale use.
- [ch.9 p.45] The tonic major scale covers II-7, V7, and I in a single scale with no collision tones. This is Coker's first and most accessible harmonic generalization strategy: play a C major scale or pattern over D-7 G7 C major and produce no wrong notes.
- [ch.9 p.45] The C pentatonic covers C major and F major (plus eight other chord types) without collision tones. The five-note structure minimizes harmonic conflict risk across multiple chord qualities simultaneously.
- [ch.15 p.73] The Cry Me A River lick starts on the 4th of a major chord. The lick accommodates five chord types without alteration; the 4th-of-major entry is one of five equally valid deployment points.

## min

Coker's treatment of the minor chord (as a tonic minor or standalone minor chord of long duration, distinct from the min7 II-chord role) centers almost entirely on CESH. The four CESH varieties are enumerated and ranked; harmonic minor generalization over minor II-V-I is justified by three practical arguments rather than theoretical purity.

- [ch.11 p.62 / ch.12 p.74] CESH's canonical setting is the minor chord of long duration. Of the four varieties (minor/root-in-motion, minor/fifth-in-motion, major/root-in-motion, major/fifth-in-motion), the minor/root-in-motion form is "the most common one by far" and is the primary study focus. Anchor initial learning to familiar standard-repertoire melodies that use this device.
- [ch.11 p.62] Four CESH varieties exist: minor and major, each with either root or fifth in motion. Real solos frequently present incomplete or heavily embellished CESH, making recognition a distinct skill from execution.
- [ch.9 p.45-46] The harmonic minor scale may generalize over minor II-V-I despite producing collision tones. Coker justifies this on three grounds: (1) minor II-V-I chords change roughly twice as fast as major II-V-I, limiting exposure to collision tones; (2) the aggregate effect on the listener is a minor key; (3) players instinctively avoid collision tones because their ears warn them in advance.

## min7b5

Coker's treatment of the half-diminished chord is sparse and instrument-agnostic: it appears in the book primarily as a Track AA substitution device and as the advanced diminished-scale generalization target for the minor II-V. Both lessons position the half-diminished as the gateway to minor II-V-I work.

- [ch.6 p.32] Track AA substitutes the half-diminished chord for the minor seventh throughout (root sequence identical to Track Y). This prepares the ear for the minor II chord in minor II-V-I progressions, where the II chord is half-diminished, not plain minor seventh.
- [ch.9 p.46] The diminished scale can cover both the half-diminished II chord and the V chord in a minor key under a single scale, excluding the I chord. Collision tones are present; this is an advanced extension that requires instinctive avoidance of collision tones and is recommended only for more capable improvisers.

## dim7

The #II dim7 receives a single, focused lesson as a V7 substitute. Coker's explanation relies entirely on the listener's precedent-memory mechanism -- the same perceptual logic underpinning the tritone substitution and back door progression.

- [ch.15 p.82] The #II dim7 substitutes for V7 because the listener's ear hears its familiar role as a chord preceding I or III-7, not the conflict with the original V7. Notes that would otherwise sound anachronistic (the 4th, the major 7th) are rendered consonant by this precedent effect. The #II dim7 shares this perceptual logic with the tritone substitution and the back door progression.

## pentatonic

Coker presents pentatonic generalization as a chord-quality-agnostic strategy, not a device tied to a single chord type. The single C-pentatonic-covers-ten-chords fact is the book's most compact statement of harmonic generalization's efficiency.

- [ch.9 p.45] A single C pentatonic scale (C, D, E, G, A) covers ten chord types -- C major, F major, Bb major (+4), A-7, D-7, G-7, C7, G7sus4, D7sus4, and Gb7 (+5, b9) -- with no collision tones. This is framed as the safest harmonic generalization available: it minimizes collision risk while opening up the search for notes common to several successive chords.
