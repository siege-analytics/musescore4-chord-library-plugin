# Prescriptive lessons -- jerry-coker x patterns-for-jazz

## maj

Coker grounds all major-triad work in a single derivation: extract scale degrees 1-3-5 from the major scale of the chord root. The 1-2-3-5 ascending bottom-structure pattern extends this to nearly any chord with a major third, and Coker asserts that this one pattern plus its minor counterpart covers virtually all changes in a standard tune.

- [ch.1 p.1] Derive the major triad by extracting scale degrees 1-3-5 from the major scale of the chord root. Students must be able to recite, write, and play major triads in every key before beginning any pattern work.
- [ch.6 p.25] The 1-2-3-5 ascending pattern applies to any chord with a major third. Together with the 1-2-b3-5 minor counterpart, these two patterns are sufficient to cover virtually all chord changes in a standard tune.

## maj6

The major sixth chord shares its parent major scale with the triad, M7, and M9, so no separate scale is required. Coker treats the chord-symbol choice as a notational convenience only -- patterns notated under a triad symbol apply equally to CM6.

- [ch.1 p.1 / ch.4 p.16] Derive CM6 by extracting 1-3-5-6 from the major scale. Because M6 shares the major scale with the triad, M7, and M9, a triad-symbol pattern is merely convenient shorthand -- "it would be impossible to determine exactly which chord is being used when the scale is common to all the chords mentioned."

## maj7

Coker treats the M7 as one member of a four-quality major family (triad, M6, M7, M9) all sharing a single major scale parent, so no separate scale fingering is needed for any of them. The two-fragment combination 5-8-6-4 and 2-5-3-1 achieves complete coverage of all four qualities in one pattern. In the IIm7-V7-IM7 context, the IM7 is the resolution target and is covered by recycling mastered two-measure fragments rather than introducing new material. The augmented scale is also prescribed as a secondary (and primary for M7+5) option. Within turnarounds, the IM7 holds only about two beats before quality permutation begins.

- [ch.1 p.2 / ch.4 p.16 / ch.16 p.82] Derive the M7 by extracting scale degrees 1-3-5-7 from the major scale. It shares the parent major scale with the triad, M6, and M9, so one fingering covers all four. Coker adds the heuristic that "in most instances, the M7 chord is likely to function as IM7 rather than IVM7," reducing decision burden in ambiguous key contexts.
- [ch.16 p.83 / ch.17 p.90] The IM7 is the resolution target of the IIm7-V7-IM7 group. All three chords share one parent key signature. Rather than new material, Coker extends mastered one-measure IIm7-V7 fragments into four-measure patterns that land on the IM7.
- [ch.7 p.27] The two-fragment combination 5-8-6-4 and 2-5-3-1, drawn from the major scale, simultaneously implies the triad, M6, M7, and M9 -- Coker's prescribed method for complete major-family coverage without writing separate patterns for each extension.
- [ch.28 p.134] The augmented scale is the primary source for the M7+5 chord, and a secondary option for the plain M7. The augmented scale on C (C-D#-E-G-Ab-B) contains two augmented triads and has only four distinct transpositions.
- [ch.25 p.118 / ch.23 p.115] In a turnaround, the tonic M7 holds for only about two beats. The root stays stationary while chord quality permutes, shifting function and key center through quality variation alone.

## maj9

The major ninth chord is a member of the four-quality major family sharing one parent major scale. Coker's enharmonic equivalence rule collapses the ninth to the second scale degree, so no new scale learning is required.

- [ch.1 p.2 / ch.1 p.3] Derive the M9 by extracting 1-3-5-7-9 from the major scale. The ninth is the same pitch as scale degree 2 -- "the ninth, eleventh and thirteenth are also the second, fourth and sixth tones respectively." Because M9 shares the major-scale parent with the triad, M6, and M7, no separate scale is needed for improvisation.

## dom7

Coker's dominant prescription rests on Mixolydian derivation: extract 1-3-5-7 from the Mixolydian mode of the chord root, and understand which parent key that Mixolydian belongs to before applying any formula. Dominant function is defined structurally -- major third, minor seventh -- and survives any alteration to the fifth, ninth, eleventh, or thirteenth. Within the IIm7-V7 progression, the shared parent key of both chords enables a single scalar pattern to run across both; when assigned specific fragments, the V7 takes 3-4-5-1 (or 1-2-6-5 in the two-measure version). For fully altered dominant sounds, the diminished scale positioned one half-step above the chord root simultaneously yields flat-9, +9, +11, and 13. One diminished scale also covers four dominant seventh roots related by minor third or tritone. Lydian Augmented Scale provides two additional alignment positions for altered-dominant contexts. Polychords are built either from substitutions or from the ninth, eleventh, or thirteenth of the chord; non-keyboard players achieve the bitonal effect through rhythmic alternation rather than simultaneous voicing. Turnarounds must conclude on a V7 or its flat-II7 substitute.

- [ch.9 p.50 / ch.9 p.51] Derive the dom7 by extracting 1-3-5-7 from the Mixolydian mode of the chord root. The student must relate each Mixolydian mode to its parent key before applying formulas or playing patterns.
- [ch.25 p.119 / ch.25 p.121] Dominant function is invariant as long as the third is major and the seventh is minor. The fifth may be perfect, lowered, or raised; the ninth perfect, augmented, or minor; the eleventh perfect or augmented; the thirteenth major or (rarely) minor -- all without disturbing function.
- [ch.17 p.88 / ch.20 p.97] Within a one-measure IIm7-V7, the V7 is assigned fragment 3-4-5-1 extracted from its Mixolydian scale. In the two-measure version, fragment 1-2-6-5 is used, with attention to the unusual interval between degrees 2 and 6.
- [ch.17 p.94 / ch.17 p.95] The shared parent key of IIm7 and V7 allows a single scalar pattern to run through both chords without alteration. Coker assigns ascending direction to the IIm7 measure and descending direction to the V7 measure.
- [ch.27 p.130 / ch.27 p.131] A diminished scale rooted one half-step above the dominant seventh chord root simultaneously supplies flat-9, +9, +11, and 13 -- "all four color tones are common to one another and can be used together to good effect."
- [ch.27 p.131] One diminished scale covers four dominant seventh roots: a C-sharp diminished scale serves C7, Eb7, F#7, and A7. This efficiency follows directly from the four-root symmetry of the diminished scale and the diminished-fifth substitution rule.
- [ch.33 p.146] Lydian Augmented Scale aligned with its root on the chord's seventh yields an augmented eleventh (flat-5) over a dominant seventh flat-5 chord. Students must learn all twelve Lydian Augmented scales through repeated full-range and intervallic (thirds) practice.
- [ch.33 p.145 / ch.33 p.146] Lydian Augmented Scale aligned with its root on the chord's third simultaneously yields raised 5th, raised 9th, lowered 9th, and augmented 11th over an altered dominant -- four alterations from one scale placement.
- [ch.25 p.118] Turnarounds must end on a V7 or a flat-II7 / flat-IIM7 dominant substitute to give phrase-endings "a sense of direction, namely to return gracefully to the beginning of a repeated section."
- [ch.25 p.121] Polychords over dom7 are built either by combining the given chord with a substitution chord, or by constructing the upper triad from the ninth, eleventh, or thirteenth of the given chord. The added triad must not compromise dominant function.
- [ch.25 p.121] Non-keyboard players achieve polychord effects through rhythmic alternation between the two chord layers rather than simultaneous voicing, beginning with Pattern No. 180.

## dom9

The dominant ninth shares its Mixolydian parent with the dom7 of the same root -- no new scale is required.

- [ch.9 p.51] Use the same Mixolydian scale for dom7 and dom9 chords on the same root. The ninth equals scale degree 2, already present in the mode, so no separate fingering is needed.

## dom7b9

The flat-9 is one of two available altered-ninth options on a dominant chord. Dominant function is retained regardless of ninth quality as long as the third-and-seventh core is intact. The flat-9 is also one of the four color tones automatically produced by the half-step-above diminished scale rule.

- [ch.25 p.119 / ch.27 p.130] The flat-9 (minor ninth) is a permitted alteration on any dominant seventh chord; dominant function is retained as long as the third is major and the seventh is minor. It is also one of the four color tones automatically produced by a diminished scale rooted one half-step above the chord.

## dom7alt

For fully altered dominant improvisation Coker prescribes two sources: the diminished scale one half-step above the root (yielding flat-9, +9, +11, and 13 simultaneously), and the Lydian Augmented Scale with its root on the chord's third (yielding raised 5th, raised 9th, lowered 9th, and augmented 11th simultaneously). These are the book's two primary symmetrical-scale prescriptions for the altered-dominant context.

- [ch.27 p.131] The diminished scale positioned one half-step above the dominant seventh root is the primary prescription for fully altered dominant improvisation. It yields flat-9, +9, +11, and 13 together -- four color tones that "can be used together to good effect."
- [ch.33 p.145 / ch.33 p.146] The Lydian Augmented Scale with its root on the chord's third supplies raised 5th, raised 9th, lowered 9th, and augmented 11th simultaneously. This is one of only two prescribed root-alignment positions for the Lydian Augmented Scale.

## min6

- [ch.11 p.62 / ch.12 p.67] Derive the m6 by extracting 1-3-5-6 from the Dorian mode. All four minor chord types -- minor triad, m6, m7, m9 -- "contain the same triad" and "use the same basic scale, the Dorian Mode," making the chord-symbol distinction a notation choice rather than a scale choice.

## min7

Coker unifies all four minor chord qualities under the m7 symbol and a single Dorian mode parent, treating the chord symbol as a notational convenience only. Within the IIm7-V7-IM7 group, the IIm7 is the most common minor seventh function, and its Dorian scale is shared with the V7 chord, enabling a single scalar line across both. The IIm7 is assigned fragment 3-4-5-7 in the two-fragment pattern prescription. Arpeggio exercises on m7 require directional reversal on each repetition, with two beats per chord.

- [ch.12 p.67 / ch.15 p.79] Derive the m7 by extracting 1-3-5-7 from the Dorian mode. All four minor qualities share the Dorian parent, so "the use of the minor seventh chord symbol is merely for convenience." Digital patterns are notated under the m7 symbol for the entire minor family.
- [ch.16 p.82 / ch.16 p.83 / ch.17 p.95] The m7 functions most commonly as IIm7. Its Dorian scale shares a parent key with the V7 Mixolydian scale, so a single scalar pattern can run through both chords. The heuristic is: "the m7 chord functions more commonly as a IIm7 than as a IIIm7 or VIm7."
- [ch.17 p.88 / ch.20 p.97] In the two-fragment IIm7-V7 pattern, the IIm7 is assigned fragment 3-4-5-7 from its Dorian scale. In the two-measure extension, this becomes 3-4-5-7 9-10-9-8. Harmonic coherence is justified by the shared parent key of both chords.
- [ch.14 p.71] Arpeggio patterns on m7 are practiced by reversing direction on successive repetitions -- descending, then ascending, etc. Each chord lasts two beats. This directional reversal is the defining technical drill for internalizing the Dorian sound.

## min9

The minor ninth shares the Dorian mode with the minor triad, m6, and m7. The ninth collapses to scale degree 2 by the enharmonic equivalence rule. No separate scale or pattern set is required.

- [ch.12 p.67 / ch.15 p.79] Derive the m9 by extracting 1-3-5-7-9 from the Dorian mode. The ninth equals scale degree 2. Because m9 shares the Dorian parent with the other three minor qualities, the same digital patterns cover all four without additional learning.

## dim

The diminished triad and diminished seventh chord share the same diminished scale parent. Coker treats the triad symbol in pattern notation as a convenience only -- the underlying scale is identical for both chord types.

- [ch.21 p.110 / ch.22 p.116] Derive the diminished triad by extracting 1-3-5 from the diminished scale on the chord root. The chord-symbol choice between diminished triad and diminished seventh is purely notational -- "it would be impossible to determine exactly which chord is being used when the scale is common to both."

## dim7

Coker prescribes the diminished scale as the source for both the diminished triad and seventh chord, exploiting the scale's four-root symmetry to reduce twelve roots to three distinct pitch collections. Transposition across all four roots within each collection is non-optional. The diminished-fifth substitution rule extends to four dominant seventh substitute relationships from a single tritone pair. Chromatic passing tones may be inserted between whole-step pairs in the scale to create chromatic density while preserving the diminished character -- placement, not pitch content, is what determines the sound.

- [ch.21 p.110 / ch.27 p.130] Derive the dim7 by extracting 1-3-5-7 from the diminished scale. The scale is symmetrical (alternating whole and half steps) and has only three distinct pitch collections; mastering three scales gives complete coverage of all twelve roots.
- [ch.22 p.116 / ch.21 p.109] Practice diminished scale patterns starting from every root in the reference chart (fig. 32). Each diminished scale serves four roots -- e.g., C diminished also yields Eb, Gb, and A diminished -- and transposing through all four roots within a collection is non-optional.
- [ch.27 p.131] Dominant seventh chords whose roots lie within the same diminished seventh chord are mutual substitutes. The tritone pair (e.g., C7 and F#7) yields four dominant seventh substitute relationships when the two midpoints of each tritone interval are included.
- [ch.27 p.130] Insert chromatic non-harmonic passing tones between pairs of tones that are a whole step apart in a diminished scale pattern. The result sounds like a chromatic scale but retains diminished character because of where the non-chord tones fall -- placement determines identity, not pitch content.

## aug

Coker derives the augmented triad from the whole-tone scale (extracting 1-3-5) and reduces twelve roots to only two distinct pitch collections. Enharmonic respelling conventions are required and explicitly warned against miscounting.

- [ch.19 p.106 / ch.19 p.107 / ch.18 p.104] Derive the augmented triad by extracting 1-3-5 from the whole-tone scale. Only two distinct whole-tone pitch collections exist, so two scale fingerings cover all twelve augmented triad roots. Students are warned not to miscount the triad as having six tones due to enharmonic notation -- "the correct spelling for the E Augmented Triad should be E-G-sharp-B-sharp."

## aug7

The augmented seventh chord shares its whole-tone parent with the augmented triad. Enharmonic respelling is permitted freely to maintain correct chord spelling.

- [ch.19 p.107 / ch.19 p.106] Derive the aug7 by extracting scale degrees 1-3-5-6 from the whole-tone scale. Both aug and aug7 share the same parent triad and whole-tone scale, giving a unifying principle for voice-leading and substitution. Enharmonic respelling is freely permitted to maintain correct spelling.
