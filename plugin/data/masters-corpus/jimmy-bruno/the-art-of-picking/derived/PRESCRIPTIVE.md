# Prescriptive lessons — jimmy-bruno × the-art-of-picking

## any

Bruno's entire system rests on three stroke rules applicable to every melodic and harmonic context: down when crossing to a higher string, up when crossing to a lower string, alternate on a single string. The physical preconditions — elbow-driven motion, relaxed wrist, strictly floating right hand — are as load-bearing as the rules themselves, and Bruno considers them non-negotiable regardless of what is being played. Exceptions are codified and few; everything else submits to the rule.

- [ch.1 p.4] **Directional-picking core rule.** Down-stroke to a higher string, up-stroke to a lower string, alternate strokes on one string. Economy of motion is the justification: the pick is always positioned where it next needs to be, eliminating wasted reverse travel. ("I found one concept or rule that causes the pick to travel the least amount of distance.")
- [ch.1 p.5] **No-anchor right hand.** Fingers must not touch the strings, bridge, pick guard, or any part of the guitar body. This free-floating posture is a physical precondition for the directional system to operate across any texture, reinforced again in Chapter 2 (pp. 8–9).
- [ch.1 p.5 / ch.2 p.7] **Elbow-driven motion.** The wrist stays straight but never stiff; all picking movement comes from the elbow. ("The wrist should not move or tilt. The movement should come from the elbow.")
- [ch.1 p.6] **Bidirectional start practice rule.** Every exercise must be drilled starting with both a down-stroke and an up-stroke. The difference in sound between the two starts should be minimal; the goal is symmetry. Restated for arpeggios (ch.5 p.24) and string skipping (ch.6 p.28).
- [ch.1 p.6] **Tempo prescription.** MM 60–120 is the prescribed starting bracket for all exercises, with speed increases built incrementally from there.
- [ch.1 p.6] **Sequential study order (proscriptive).** Bruno forbids skipping around the book. The exercises are designed point A to Z; jumping ahead is not permitted regardless of what technique the student wants to target.
- [ch.2 p.10 / ch.3 p.5] **In-between stroke — no ringing.** The in-between stroke (moving between two adjacent strings without crossing) must be drilled on any adjacent-string double-stop with strict muting. "Do not let notes ring!" The no-ringing requirement carries forward into consecutive-stroke drills in Chapter 3.
- [ch.3 p.5] **Precision over speed.** For consecutive-stroke drills, rhythmic evenness is the prerequisite to tempo. "At this stage, precision of the 8th notes is more important than speed."
- [ch.4 p.9] **Scale picking — uniform across all six fingerings.** The directional rule applies without modification to all six major-scale shapes (6V2, 5V2, 6V4, 5V4, 6H2, 5H2). Asterisk notation in the exercises makes the higher/lower-string rule visible at every string crossing.
- [ch.5 p.15] **Arpeggio articulation over speed.** For arpeggio playing in any chord context, note separation is the primary challenge, not velocity. "Articulation is more important than speed."
- [ch.5 p.21] **Sweep picking proscription (proscriptive).** Bruno refuses to treat sweep picking as a technique distinct from directional picking. The directional rule already subsumes it: higher string = down, lower string = up. No separate sweep-picking framework is needed or sanctioned.
- [ch.5 p.24] **Rest-start stroke freedom.** When a rest separates phrases, the player may begin the next phrase with whichever stroke they choose, breaking the directional determination. This is the one codified exception to starting-stroke selection.
- [ch.5 p.25] **Music drives picking, not the fretboard (proscriptive).** Musical intent governs fingering and picking; fingerboard convenience must never dictate the line. The picking mechanism is a servant to music from inside, not a constraint imposed by the instrument.
- [ch.6 p.26] **String-skip directional rule.** The same higher/lower rule applies to non-adjacent string skips. Applied across pairs (4 and 2), (5 and 3), (6 and 4) without modification.
- [ch.6 p.30] **Hard-skip accent override — alternate picking (sanctioned exception).** For the specific combination of up-stroke accent, in-between stroke, and string skip, Bruno himself finds standard alternate down-up easier than his own system and prescribes it. A notable moment of the master overriding his framework on experience.
- [ch.7 p.34] **Position dictates picking pattern.** Relocating a phrase to a different fingerboard position can change the stroke sequence completely. The player must re-work the picking for the new position; do not assume the pattern transfers.
- [ch.7 p.36] **Slurred triplets preferred in bebop phrasing.** Bruno prescribes slurred triplets over fully picked triplets for a horn-like articulation. Must be mastered starting from both a down-stroke and an up-stroke.
- [ch.8 p.38 / ch.8 p.40] **Articulation as inflection — slur mechanic.** Think of articulations as speech-like inflections realized by slurring: pick one note and sound two. Shifting the slur to a different beat within the same phrase changes the inflection character; the placement is a creative variable.
- [ch.8 p.39] **Long-short cadence phrase ending.** Bebop phrase endings call for a long-short rhythmic gesture on the final two notes. This is a recurring cadential articulation, not an ornament.
- [ch.8 p.41] **Same-string slur physical constraint.** Hammer-ons and pull-offs can only be placed on two notes that share a single string. This is a physical constraint, not a stylistic preference.
- [ch.8 p.42] **Slur placement — creative internalization.** The student must self-direct slur experimentation rather than follow a fixed prescription. Bruno provides exemplars but explicitly instructs: "Experiment adding your own slurs at different places."
- [ch.9 p.49 / ch.9 p.50] **Big-band consecutive down-strokes for accent (sanctioned exception).** Consecutive down-strokes are licensed to emulate big-band horn-section accent emphasis. Slurs are presented as a functionally equivalent alternative for the same phrase.
- [ch.9 p.50] **Rhythmic break licenses consecutive down-strokes (sanctioned exception).** When a rhythmic break separates two notes, consecutive down-strokes are preferred over strict alternation. This is the second codified exception to the alternation rule.
- [ch.10 p.51] **Habit formation — repetition over analysis.** Do not overanalyze right-hand movements mid-stroke. Repetition until second nature is the meta-practice prescription. Advanced players with long-established habits will struggle most and must be patient.

## triad

For triad-based material Bruno introduces his one significant in-exercise rule break: repeating three-note arpeggio patterns drilled as triplets use three consecutive downs followed by three consecutive ups rather than strict alternation. Crucially, this exception is scoped entirely to the drill; the moment a triplet figure appears inside an actual melodic line, the directional rule reasserts itself. The exception is a technical training device, not a performance prescription.

- [ch.3 p.6] **Triplet drill — three-down / three-up.** For repeating triplet arpeggio patterns across any three adjacent strings, Bruno prescribes breaking the alternation rule: three consecutive down-strokes followed by three consecutive up-strokes. "Apply to any three note chord on any three adjacent strings." The consecutive-stroke grouping aligns naturally with the triplet subdivision.
- [ch.3 p.6] **Triplet drill exception confined to exercise (proscriptive).** The three-down / three-up pattern must not be carried into actual melodic lines. "If I had to play a triplet phrase in the context of a line and not in an exercise, I would adhere to the rule." Strict directional picking returns in real phrase contexts.

## maj

Bruno singles out the major triad arpeggio as a foundational warm-up vehicle precisely because its articulation demands are high — getting clean note separation on this material transfers directly to any phrase containing an arpeggio fragment. It is both a technical drill and a diagnostic: if you can play the major triad exercise with clean separation, the mechanism works.

- [ch.5 p.19] **Major triad arpeggio as foundational warm-up.** Bruno designates the major triad arpeggio exercise as the warm-up of choice for developing note separation across all arpeggio work. "This exercise alone will facilitate the separation of notes with any phrase that contains an arpeggio or arpeggio fragment." The difficulty is articulation, not pitch complexity.

## min11

Bruno uses the minor 11 arpeggio as the primary demonstration that fingering — not picking aggression — unlocks high velocity. The adjacent-string fingering for this chord creates the conditions for consecutive same-direction strokes that make sextuplet runs physically possible; the point generalizes to any chord where fingering can be arranged to place notes on adjacent strings.

- [ch.5 p.22] **Minor 11 — sextuplet fingering enables speed.** The fingering for minor 11 places notes on adjacent strings, setting up consecutive down and up strokes across four strings and enabling sextuplet velocity (six notes per beat). Bruno explicitly credits fingering, not picking aggression, as the mechanism. Saxophone players and pianists phrase this way.

## min9

The minor 9th arpeggio is Bruno's showcase for two-octave arpeggio spans. The design principle is the same as for minor 11: adjacent-string fingering makes what looks like an impossible reach physically tractable. Speed is a byproduct of fingering architecture, not of picking harder or faster.

- [ch.5 p.24 / ch.5 p.25] **Minor 9th — adjacent-string fingering enables two-octave arpeggios.** Fingerings that place notes on adjacent strings (either higher or lower) make two-octave minor 9th spans playable. "By using fingerings that place notes on adjacent strings, either higher or lower, these large two octave arpeggios become possible." The fingering is the unlock; the picking rule follows automatically.
