# Prescriptive lessons -- jody-fisher x jazz-guitar-method-vol-3-mastering-chord-melody

## major

Fisher treats major chords as a family of interchangeable enhancements (Maj6, Maj7, Maj9) rather than discrete qualities, where adding extensions constitutes enrichment, not substitution. Chord-melody voicing begins with octave exploration to find where the melody sits comfortably on top, and passing approaches--same-quality half-step neighbors and subdominant IV chords--are the primary tools for smooth entry into major harmonies. Fisher places a hard cap on enrichment: taste and context must govern when to extend, and not every major chord warrants it.

- [ch.2 p.9,11] When arranging chord melody, find voicings that place the melody note on top of the major chord; raise the melody one octave when necessary to accommodate larger voicings. Explore the melody in single notes across every octave before committing to a voicing.
- [ch.2 p.12] Within the major family--Maj6, Maj7, Maj9--all members are functionally interchangeable; adding extensions is enhancement, not substitution. Context and taste govern how much enrichment is applied.
- [ch.2 p.12] [PROSCRIPTIVE] Not every major chord needs harmonic enrichment; enriching indiscriminately undermines the musical impact of selective enhancement.
- [ch.4 p.39] Precede a major I chord with its IV chord (root a perfect 4th higher) to handle both non-chord tones and chord tones as passing harmonization, creating smooth voice-leading into the tonic.
- [ch.4 p.38] Embellish a major chord by using another major chord a half step above or below as a same-quality passing chord--a fundamental method for chord embellishment.

## minor

Fisher treats the minor family (min7, min9, min6) with the same interchangeability principle as major: extensions are enhancements, not substitutions. The minor ii chord carries structural weight as the anchor of the ii-V7-I progression, and same-quality half-step passing chords apply symmetrically to minor as to major.

- [ch.2 p.12] All chords within the minor family are functionally interchangeable; min7, min9, and min6 may substitute for one another as enhancements, enabling flexible voicing selection over any minor harmony.
- [ch.4 p.38] When approaching a minor chord, a same-quality minor chord a half step above or below serves as a passing chord, creating smooth chromatic voice-leading into the target harmony.
- [ch.4 p.40,44] The minor ii chord is load-bearing in the ii-V7-I progression, which establishes a key through characteristic root, 3rd, and 7th movement. Backcycling extends this by preceding the ii with its own ii-V7, repeating the fourth-movement pattern systematically.

## dom7

Fisher's dominant treatment is the most technically layered section: the 3rd and 7th are the irreducible anchors for voice leading, and the dominant's reach is expanded through three distinct mechanisms--tritone substitution (from the V7 root), tritone approach (from the destination root), and secondary dominants applied to any chord in the progression. Whole-step symmetrical transposition of altered dominant voicings adds harmonic color while preserving function.

- [ch.3 p.21-22] The 3rd and 7th of the dominant chord define its quality and anchor voice leading; arrange voicings so these tones move in whole and half steps between chord changes.
- [ch.3 p.23] A dominant seventh chord--altered or unaltered--a half step above or below the target chord functions as a passing chord to smooth transitions; these may be non-diatonic.
- [ch.4 p.39] Any chord in a progression may be treated as a temporary I and preceded by its own V7 (secondary dominant), enhanced or altered as desired--this generalizes the V7-I principle across the entire progression.
- [ch.4 p.45] The V7 may be replaced by a dominant chord whose root is a tritone away; this tritone substitution maintains harmonic function and applies equally to secondary dominants.
- [ch.5 p.48] Any chord may be approached by another chord whose root is a tritone away from the destination root--distinct from V7-based tritone substitution in that the substitute is derived from the destination rather than the dominant.
- [ch.6 p.60] Dominant voicings containing 9ths and altered 5ths may be transposed by whole steps and retain dominant function, enabling harmonic color variety without changing chord quality.

## dim7

Fisher positions the diminished chord primarily as a functional substitute for the dominant seventh, grounded in the shared-note relationship between dim7 and V7b9. The fingerboard symmetry of diminished chords (repeating every three frets) is leveraged as a practical resource for voicing selection.

- [ch.3 p.30] A diminished chord a half step below the target chord's root substitutes for the V7; both chords share three notes and resolve similarly to the I chord, making dim7 a practical drop-in replacement in chord-melody arrangements.
- [ch.3 p.30] Place a diminished chord a half step below the target chord to create a passing harmony that pulls convincingly toward the destination--a foundational move for harmonizing both chord tones and non-chord tones.
- [ch.3 p.28] Diminished chords repeat every three frets on the fingerboard; this symmetry allows the arranger to choose the most ergonomic or voice-leading-friendly position for any given diminished chord.

## quartal

Fisher introduces quartal harmony as an alternative color source for harmonizing melody tones, contrasting its sound against conventional tertian voicings without prescribing specific functional roles.

- [ch.3 p.33] Chords constructed by stacking 4ths (quartal harmony) offer an alternative harmonic color for harmonizing non-chord tones and chord tones in chord-melody arrangements, providing a distinctly modern contrast to tertian voicings.

## triad

In two-part bass-chord arrangements, Fisher treats triads as the rhythmic and harmonic upper layer above a walking bass line. The physical and dynamic independence of the two layers--bass on every beat, chords as accented punches with slight delay--is what creates the illusion of two players. Scale-tone connections and half-step bass approaches give the bass line melodic logic; sustaining triad voices across bass movement preserves harmonic continuity.

- [ch.5 p.51] In two-part arrangements, put a bass note on every beat and deliver chords as accented punches; maintain realistic volume balance between the two layers as if a bassist and guitarist were playing together.
- [ch.5 p.54] Achieve rhythmic separation by playing the bass note on the downbeat and delaying the triad chord slightly after--within the same beat--to break quarter-note monotony and simulate two separate instruments.
- [ch.5 p.55] Whenever possible, let two notes of the triad ring while moving to the next bass note to sustain harmonic continuity across bass-line movement; practice slowly before applying at tempo.
- [ch.5 p.51] Connect triad voicings with scale-tone bass notes drawn from the chord's parent scale; this gives the bass line melodic logic and walking-bass realism.
- [ch.5 p.53] Approach any chord voicing from a bass note a half step above or below the lowest voice of the triad--a fundamental chromatic walking-bass technique.

## any

Fisher's cross-quality principles form the procedural and philosophical spine of the book. The sequencing rule--basic chords first, enrichment second, improvised variation third--is non-negotiable and recurs. Voice leading (stepwise motion anchored by 3rds and 7ths), diad harmonization (3rds and 6ths), octave doubling, and fingerboard memorization in all twelve keys are universal tools. The closing principle reframes the entire method: accumulation of voicings and techniques is a means to flexibility, not the artistic end.

- [ch.2 p.11] Always arrange a song with basic chords first; only after the skeletal arrangement is solid should passing chords, substitutions, and harmonizations be layered in. Applying enrichment before structural foundation is established undermines coherence.
- [ch.2 p.11] [PROSCRIPTIVE] Do not enrich or substitute chords before the skeletal arrangement is firmly in hand; the procedural sequence--basic chords first, then embellishment--is non-negotiable.
- [ch.2 p.11] [PROSCRIPTIVE] Do not carve arrangements in stone; treat them as skeletal frameworks from which to improvise melodically, harmonically, and rhythmically. Flexibility in performance is the goal.
- [ch.3 p.19] Non-chord tones in the melody may be harmonized by adding them directly to the underlying chord as extensions or alterations, renaming the chord while enhancing it--the primary method for generating enhancements organically from the melody.
- [ch.3 p.18] Harmonize both chord tones and non-chord tones with diads; 3rds and 6ths are the most common and idiomatic intervals, though any interval is possible.
- [ch.3 p.36] Double melody notes in octaves for emphasis when stronger melodic projection is desired; Wes Montgomery and George Benson are cited as authoritative models.
- [ch.3 p.21-22] Good voice leading requires individual voices to travel in whole and half steps between chord changes; prioritize the 3rd and 7th of each chord as quality-defining anchors for stepwise motion.
- [ch.2 p.11 / ch.5 p.50] Memorize chord changes and voicings in multiple fingerboard positions, learning them systematically in all twelve keys before applying bass-line approaches. Fingerboard flexibility is essential for real-time improvised harmonization.
- [ch.5 p.50] Use as few left-hand fingers as possible in voicings--often two--to keep remaining fingers free for melody and improvisation; economy of fingering is critical in two-part arrangements.
- [ch.6 p.60] Discover new voicings by moving individual voices of familiar chord shapes to higher or lower octaves; many sounds are available within shapes already known.
- [ch.6 p.62] Reharmonize sections of a tune by composing a new bass line first and letting that bass line drive harmonic choices; treat the bass line as the primary harmonic generator, not a consequence of pre-selected changes.
- [ch.6 p.63] [PROSCRIPTIVE] Knowing hundreds of voicings and techniques matters less than finding and expressing your artistic voice; how you use what you know outweighs how much you know.
