## Major Sounds resolution

## Diminished chord resolution options

> DIMINISHED SOUNDS: Resolve the last chord in this sequence into any chord. For example: (1) Dm7 — G7 — Cma’7,
> or (2) Em7 — Eb9 — Dm9 — Db9.

**Significance (p. 15).** Gives a concrete rule — diminished chords are interchangeable approach chords — and offers two specific resolution paths that demonstrate chromatic bass-line motion.

## Diminished as flat-nine substitute

> DIMINISHED PASSAGE: Use of diminished chords for seventh flat ninth chord movement, utilizing the basic pattern
> of A7 — Dm — G7 —C.

**Significance (p. 15).** States the defining rule that a diminished chord can stand in for a 7b9 chord, anchoring the diminished passage to a recognizable I–VI–II–V skeleton.

## Augmented chromatic approach

> AUGMENTED SOUNDS: The first six chords are basically G aug. Movement is used chromatically to get to the basic

**Significance (p. 15).** Defines the compositional logic of the augmented passage — sustained augmented color resolved via chromatic voice-leading into a cycle cadence.

## Chord symbol spelling convention

> These chord symbols are written so that the order of appearance of notes coincides with the spelling of the chord symbol. For example, D7+9+5 means D7 with the raised 9th next, and the raised 5th on top.

**Significance (p. 20).** Establishes the book's proprietary notation rule — symbol order equals voice order — which is essential for correctly reading every voicing in the substitution tables.

## Sustaining lead notes between chords

> NOTE: Try to sustain lead notes from one chord to the next. For example, C9 to F13, sustain the G on top.

**Significance (p. 24).** Gives a direct performance technique — common-tone sustain across chord changes — that shapes idiomatic jazz comping and voice-leading on the guitar.
