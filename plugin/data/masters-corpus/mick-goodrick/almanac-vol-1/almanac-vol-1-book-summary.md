## What Goodrick's *Almanac of Voice-Leading for Guitar* Teaches

The *Almanac* is, by design, almost entirely free of instructional prose. It makes its argument through exhaustive enumeration: every page is a table, every table is a systematic mapping, and the accumulated weight of those mappings constitutes the teaching. To read it as a reference is to use it correctly; to read it as a book about voice-leading is to understand what it is actually doing.

**Volume One: Name That Chord** catalogs tertian sonorities — triads, seventh chords, and Goodrick's "TBN" (Two-Note Base) constructs — in both Close and Spread voicings. Volume One spans pages 12–189 and organizes every entry across six cycles (cycles 2 through 7, representing the interval of motion between successive diatonic chords) and three parent scales (C Major, C Melodic Minor, and C Harmonic Minor). That grid of 6 cycles × 3 scales produces 18 distinct voice-leading environments for every voicing type. The Close and Spread representations of each sonority are treated as parallel objects with their own entries, making the relationship between register and voice-leading behavior explicit and navigable.

**Volume Two: Do Not Name That Chord** applies the identical analytical framework to quartal and cluster sonorities — sonorities that resist conventional chord naming precisely because they are not built from stacked thirds. Pages 190–378 catalog 3-part quartal voicings in Close and Spread forms, 4-part quartal voicings across six distinct voicing types, and Spread Cluster voicings across six further types. The same 18-environment grid (six cycles, three parent scales) is applied throughout, meaning the reader encounters quartal and cluster harmony subjected to exactly the same systematic pressure as tertian harmony in Volume One.

Every entry in both volumes presents three analytical layers. The **Intervallic Voice-Leading** layer identifies which chord tone moves by which interval as the cycle progresses — the raw pitch-distance grammar of the motion. The **Functional Voice-Leading** layer names the scale-degree functions in play: which role (root, third, fifth, seventh, or quartal equivalent) moves to which role, making the harmonic logic audible beneath the intervallic surface. The **M.S.R.P. string** (Most Stepwise Resolution Pattern) distills the top-voice melodic line through the cycle into its most step-wise form, giving the guitarist a compact melodic handle on what is otherwise a multi-voice contrapuntal event.

The discipline the book teaches — silently, by repetition — is that voice-leading is the same problem regardless of the sonority family. What changes across families is which voice carries the common tone, which voice steps by a second or third, and how large the remaining leaps must be. The cataloging across cycles makes the trade-off visible: some cycles (particularly cycles producing common-tone-rich motion) yield smooth voice-leading almost automatically; others force larger intervallic displacement, and the tables show exactly where and why. The Close-versus-Spread axis is fully parameterized: every sonority family has both representations with independent voice-leading tables, so the guitarist can trace how register choice reshapes the contrapuntal texture without changing the harmonic content.

Perhaps the most consequential implicit claim of the *Almanac* — buried in the structural parallelism between Volume One and Volume Two — is that quartal and cluster harmony is not a special case requiring special treatment. It obeys the same voice-leading discipline as tertian harmony; its intervallic primitives are simply different. By running 4-part fourths and spread cluster voicings through the same 18-environment grid, with the same three analytical layers, Goodrick enforces the insight that the guitarist's contrapuntal obligation does not change when the harmonic language does. The *Almanac* is, in this sense, a proof by exhaustion: once you have seen every voicing family submitted to the same analytical apparatus and survived it, the unity of voice-leading as a discipline is no longer an abstraction.
