## tertial vs quartal harmony

> Triads are 3-part chords based on tertial harmony (harmony derived from 3rds). 3-part
> 4ths are 3-part chords based on quartal harmony (harmony derived from 4ths). As it turns out,
> tertial harmony and quartal harmony are pretty much exact opposites.

**Significance (p. 9).** Conceptual definition that frames the entire system.

## triad interval totals

> Four 3rds, Two 4ths, One 5th, Two 6ths
> Primary intervals: 3rds and 6ths
> Secondary intervals: 4ths and 5ths
> Color intervals: 2nds and 7ths (missing)

**Significance (p. 10).** Interval taxonomy of triads — comparative baseline.

## 4th voicing interval totals

> Two 2nds, Four 4ths, Two 5ths, One 7th
> Primary intervals: 4ths and 5ths
> Secondary intervals: 2nds and 7ths
> Color intervals: 3rds and 6ths (missing)

**Significance (p. 10).** Interval taxonomy of 3-part 4ths — mirror-image proof of tertial/quartal opposition.

## drop-D pedal technique

> Here's a really great thing to do that is very “guitar-friendly.” After you've spent some
> time exploring the 3-part 4ths in C Major, tune the low E string down a whole step to D. Use
> the low D string as a pedal, [you could also use the A string] and play through all the cycles!

**Significance (p. 10).** Concrete guitar-specific drop-D pedal technique for 4th-voicing cycles.
