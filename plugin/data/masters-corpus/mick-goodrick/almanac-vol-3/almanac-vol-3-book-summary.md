# Book-level distillation — almanac-vol-3

_Aggregated from per-chapter summaries under `summaries/`._

## Overall Arc and Pedagogical Structure

This book is the culminating volume of a three-part method, and its architecture moves from definitional groundwork outward to comprehensive harmonic taxonomy and finally to practical voice-leading application. Chapter 1 establishes essential terminology before any technique is introduced. Chapters 2 through 9 build a closed, finite classification system—the M-Lode framework—by defining chord families, naming conventions, and derivation procedures. Chapters 10 through 12 then shift from taxonomy to application, teaching what to do with the structures once classified: how to voice-lead them through diatonic scales, which twenty forms to prioritize, and how the system extends into five parent scales. The pedagogical logic is cumulative and self-referential: each chapter assumes the vocabulary of the prior ones, and the entire structure is designed so that a student who masters the twenty 3-part chords of Chapter 11 has, in the author's explicit judgment, achieved the central goal of the book.

## The M-Lode System

The spine of the method is the M-Lode system, a complete taxonomy of chord voicings organized into families defined by interval content. Chapter 3 completes the taxonomy by adding three new 3-part categories to those established in earlier volumes: 7th chords with no 3rd, 7th chords with no 5th, and 3-part spread clusters. Together with triads, 3-part 4th voicings, and their 4-part structural counterparts, these form the full M-Lode framework. Chapter 4 establishes a structural principle linking the families: interval omissions occur only within specific chord families (triads, 3-part 4ths, and spread clusters), while four families—7th no-3rd, 7th no-5th, TBN I, and TBN II—show the most balanced interval distribution across both 3- and 4-part voicings. Chapter 12 identifies Spread Clusters as the structural element that makes the entire taxonomy finite and complete.

## Derivation as the Core Procedure

The method's characteristic procedural move is derivation: generating smaller structures by systematically omitting voices from larger ones. Chapters 5 through 9 each demonstrate one derivation pathway. Chapter 5 derives 3-part chords from 7th chords by omitting either the 3rd or the 5th, producing the two triadic subtypes that appear across four different 4-part chord families—a symmetry with direct implications for reharmonization. Chapters 6 and 7 derive 3-part chords from TBN I and TBN II structures respectively, using Drop 2, Drop 3, Drop 2&3, Drop 2&4, and Double Drop 2 Drop 3 procedures across root positions and inversions. Chapters 8 and 9 complete the loop by deriving 3-part chords from 4-part 4th chords and 4-part spread clusters.

## Terminology and Naming Conventions

Chapter 2 introduces a precise terminological distinction between events (passages using 2–6 diatonic notes) and episodes (passages using all 7 diatonic notes), each behaving differently under diatonic transposition. The chapter also advocates a specific naming convention for stacked-fourth chords, preferring sus4 notation over complex alterations. Chapter 3 addresses the parallel naming problem for clusters, proposing that they be named by literal pitch content (e.g., "C D E") rather than forced into traditional chord symbols.

## Voice-Leading Rules and the M.S.R.P. Sequence

Chapter 10 moves from taxonomy to voice-leading procedure, establishing C Major as the default presentation scale with explicit expectation that students transpose all material to melodic and harmonic minor variants. The chapter introduces a mathematical framework using abstract A-B-C labels to enumerate all possible voice-leading patterns for three- and four-part chords, revealing that prior volumes in the series employed only three of six possible patterns, leaving room for new alternates. A critical analytical distinction is drawn between intervallic motion and functional voice-leading, anchored by the M.S.R.P. sequence.

## The Five Scales and the Scope of the System

Chapter 11 identifies twenty 3-part chord types as the practical core of the method, all derived from three source scales—Major, Melodic Minor, and Harmonic Minor—and transposed to C. The author states explicitly that this chapter is the most important in the volume. The title's claim of "twenty chords" is deliberately qualified: accounting for close and spread inversions, the actual count of distinct voicings is one hundred twenty. Chapter 12 extends the framework beyond three scales to five, adding Harmonic Major and Hungarian Minor, and introduces the conceptual distinction between Specific Chromatic Voice-Leading (SCVL) and Generic Diatonic Voice-Leading (GDVL). The Grip-Slipping technique is defined as a systematic method for learning chord inversions using Drop 3 voicings within a whole-tone system.

## The Central Aesthetic Principle

One governing aesthetic claim emerges explicitly in Chapter 12: excellent voice-leading treats melody and harmony as equal partners. This is presented as a departure from classical theory conventions. The method's consistent emphasis on intervallic precision, derivation logic, and the multiplication of voicings through inversions all serve this principle: the goal is not to accompany a melody or to harmonize abstractly, but to develop guitar writing in which melodic and harmonic motion are structurally integrated.
