# Prescriptive lessons — mickey-baker × complete-course-in-jazz-guitar-vol-2

## dom7
Baker treats the dominant 7th as the gravitational center of all harmonic motion — the mandatory penultimate stop before tonic resolution, regardless of how many substitutions or elaborations precede it. Extended dominant forms (dom9, dom11, dom13, altered fifths and ninths) are all Upper Partials of the same dominant function and share its resolution obligation. The player holds ultimate authority to replace any written dominant with whichever altered or extended form the ear prefers.

- [ch.8 p.11] Baker treats the dominant 7th as the most active chord of the tonality — 'the V of a chord demands resolution, the I is final' — and as the gravitational center to which all chord cycles must eventually arrive before resolving to I.
- [ch.10 p.14] Baker prescribes treating dom7 as the floor of a range of Upper Partial alterations: 'The dominant chord can be the dominant 7th, 9th, 11th, aug. 11th or 13th. The 5th or the 9th can be Flatted, nevertheless, these are still the Upper Partials of the dominant chord.' All extended dominant forms share the resolution function of the plain dom7.
- [ch.10 p.14] Baker prescribes Forced Harmony — 'adding chords via progressions that move away from, then lead back into the tonic' — as the technique for keeping dom7 contexts active rather than static. When a passage would sit on a dominant chord as written, insert progressions that depart and return rather than sustaining the chord unchanged.
- [ch.10 p.14 / ch.9 p.12] Baker establishes that 'the II chord can be readily substituted for the V,' permitting the supertonic chord to replace the dominant in a cycle provided the substituted chord still ultimately resolves to tonic. This makes dom7's substitution role bidirectional: it can receive the II or be displaced by it.
- [ch.12 p.20] Baker prescribes using the dominant as the structural pivot of the Authentic Cadence: introductions stop on V before approaching the song's first chord, while endings 'approach the V and move on to the I, which is the final statement.' The dom7 is thus the obligatory penultimate chord in both cadential contexts.
- [ch.9 p.13] Baker establishes the governing rule for all substitution and elaboration work: 'no matter how many chords you add to the cycle, they should eventually reach the dominant and from there resolve into the tonic.' The dom7 (V) is the mandatory penultimate target regardless of how many chords are inserted into the cycle.
- [ch.10 p.14] Baker grants the player explicit license to replace written dom7 changes with altered or extended dominant substitutes when building chord-melody solos: 'you use the ideas and musical sounds that sound best to you rather than the ideas and chord cycles that were originally written for the song.' The player's ear, not the chart, governs which dominant form is used.
- [ch.12 p.20] Baker prescribes that introductions stop on the dominant without resolving: 'the introduction stops on the V (Example 29). From this point you approach the first chord of the song.' The dom7 is left hanging as a springboard into the song's first chord, while endings push through V to I.
- [ch.22 p.36-37] Baker prescribes that the Cadence Break — 'the music relaxes for one or two bars' at phrase endings — may be filled with Symmetric Cycles, Pedal Point, or original chord progressions. Dominant-quality chords (such as G13b9 over a G pedal) are the canonical harmonization above the pedal fifth at these structural pauses.
- [ch.1 p.4] Baker prescribes that when a dominant voicing is physically awkward in its notated position, the player should 'move them up to a higher chord' rather than force the fingering. This position-flexibility rule applies to all chord forms including dominant voicings and reflects the method's pragmatic fingering philosophy.
- [ch.24 p.40] Baker prescribes that in ensemble Pedal Point settings the guitar (or piano) carries dominant-quality chord variations above the bass's sustained pedal tone: 'the bass plays the Pedal Point while the piano or guitar plays the chord variations.' The dom7/dom13 family supplies the upper chord voices in this three-layer texture.

## dim7
Baker positions the diminished 7th chord at the intersection of two distinct systems: as a dominant substitute (rootless dom7b9 upper structure resolving to tonic) and as the primary vehicle of Symmetric Harmony (minor-third transposition cycling through four tonics). These two roles are treated separately but are structurally unified by the dim7's inherent symmetry. Both ascending and descending directions of the cycle must be mastered, with register governing glissando direction.

- [ch.21 p.34] Baker teaches that diminished 7th chords functioning as dominants 'aren't diminished chords at all … they are the Upper tones to a diminished or flatted 9th chord.' He presents G7b9 with root omitted beside B diminished and instructs students to 'analyze the two chords' — proving that the dim7 is a rootless dom7b9.
- [ch.21 p.34-35] Baker prescribes that the diminished chord may replace the dominant in a V-I resolution: 'notice how the diminished chord instead of the dominant, resolves into the tonic. This is another form of the Altered chord.' Exercise 52 demonstrates the move, and Exercise 51 labels it 'the equivalent to the V - I Resolution.'
- [ch.13 p.21] Baker designates the diminished 7th as the emblematic vehicle of Symmetric Harmony: 'a diminished 7th chord which moves freely up and down the finger board in minor thirds … A diminished chord is built on a succession of minor thirds … each tone in the chord is actually the root or the tonic.' Because all four inversions share the same pitch classes, the dim7 is the purest expression of the 4-tonic symmetric division.
- [ch.13 p.21 / ch.14 p.22] Baker prescribes moving the diminished 7th chord shape up or down the fingerboard in minor thirds as the fundamental Symmetric Cycle motion: 'if you play a diminished chord on the guitar and move it up a minor third, the same tones will appear but in a different arrangement.' The T1-T4 notation system labels each position.
- [ch.15 p.24] Baker prescribes the diminished chord fragment — glissandoing in minor thirds — as a viable Symmetric Cycle ending device: 'go back to lesson 11, exercise 25A. In the second bar the last two beats are diminished, notice this chord glissandos up in minor thirds.' Partial chord fragments of dim7 execute the same directional motion as full voicings.
- [ch.20 p.33] Baker notes that in chord-melody arranging, non-standard diminished fingerings are legitimate: 'in the 5th bar of exercise 49, there is a D Diminished chord which is not a standard fingering. However, it does fit well into the pattern used.' Students must invent dim7 voicings that fit the melodic line even when no familiar shape is available.
- [ch.14 p.23] Baker mandates retrograde practice of all Symmetric Cycle exercises: 'Play the exercises below backwards, starting with T1 then 4, 3, 2 and back to the 1.' The dim7 symmetric cycle must be mastered in both ascending and descending directions to build complete fingerboard facility.
- [ch.15 p.24] Baker prescribes a concrete directional rule for dim7 glissando endings: 'If the last chord ends high on the finger board, move it down in minor thirds, if it ends low, move it up.' This direction-from-register rule applies when replacing a final chord with a Symmetric Cycle in introductions and endings.

## min9
Baker establishes the minor 9th as the structural departure point of the method's core modern voice-leading motion — the min9-to-min6 resolution via passing tones — and applies this same shape as the II-chord color in II-V-I cycles. The min9 appears in both exercise and performance contexts, confirming it as an active arranging color, not merely a pedagogical device. Any min9 voicing may also be placed into a Symmetric Cycle by minor-third transposition.

- [ch.4 p.7 / ch.5 p.8] Baker prescribes the minor 9th as the starting point of the method's core modern voice-leading move: 'Each chord is moving from a minor ninth to a minor sixth chord, via passing tones.' The min9 is the departure chord; the min6 is the goal.
- [ch.7 p.10] Baker prescribes opening chord-and-song-form exercises with the minor 9th and closing with the minor 6th: 'In both cases you are starting with the major or minor 9th and closing with the major or minor 6th.' Students practice this motion applied to well-known standard tunes.
- [ch.22 p.37] Baker names Dm9 as one of the two specific chord variations played over a G pedal in C Major: 'In exercise 56 … a Dm9 and a G13b9 chord play. This is the most popular form of Pedal Point.' The min9 functions as the predominant color voice above the pedal fifth.
- [ch.14 p.22] Baker prescribes that any chord — including min9 — can be placed into a Symmetric Cycle: 'Any chord can be played in a Symetric Cycle. Take any of your favorite chords, start them in a low position on your guitar and move them up in minor thirds.'
- [ch.10 p.14] Baker prescribes that the minor II chord's Upper Partials — including the min9 extension — are all valid choices for resolving into V: 'you have the 6th, 7th, 9th, 11th, and 13th. Any of these forms of the II chord can be used to resolve into the V.' Min9 is the most frequently cited II-chord quality in exercises.
- [ch.29] Fm9 appears by name in 'Just Bobi,' demonstrating that the min9 is used as an active harmonic color in chord-melody performance — not only as a departure point in voice-leading exercises but as a sustained voicing in arrangement.
- [ch.4 p.7] Baker prescribes passing tones as the obligatory mechanism for moving from min9 to min6: '"R" is for Root, "P" is for Passing tone.' Chord diagrams throughout Chapters 4 and 5 mark each passing-tone note explicitly, and students are instructed to identify root vs. passing-tone status for every note in every voicing.

## min6
Baker treats the minor 6th as the resting-point arrival of minor-mode modern progressions — the resolution destination of min9 via passing tones — and as a usable color voicing in chord-melody arrangements. The min6 pairs symmetrically with the maj6 as the two modern tonic-I sonorities that replace plain triads in Upper Partial playing.

- [ch.4 p.7] Baker prescribes the minor 6th as the resolution target of the minor ninth via passing tones — 'chord 1, through use of passing tones, resolves into chord 15 … moving from a minor ninth to a minor sixth chord, via passing tones.' The passing tones are the voice-leading mechanism, and min6 is the harmonic goal of the minor-mode modern progression.
- [ch.7 p.10] Baker prescribes the minor 6th as the closing chord in song-form exercises: 'you are starting with the major or minor 9th and closing with the major or minor 6th.' The min6 provides the point of rest after the min9 departure.
- [ch.4 p.7 / ch.5 p.8] Baker treats the minor 6th as the parallel resting point for minor-mode tonic arrival — the destination after min9 resolves via passing tones. The two forms (min6 and maj6) are the modern tonic-I sonorities that replace the plain triad in upper-partial playing.
- [ch.29] Gm6 appears by name in 'Just Bobi' as a minor-color tonic or subdominant voicing in chord-melody context. Baker's inclusion of the min6 in performance solos confirms it as a usable Upper Partial form in arrangement, not merely a resolution target in exercises.

## maj9
Baker positions the major 9th as the parallel major-mode departure chord of the ninth-to-sixth voice-leading system, with the maj6 as its resolution target. This pairing mirrors the min9-to-min6 motion exactly, differing only in mode. The maj9 also appears as a tonic-I color in chord-melody performance and as the source of arpeggiated bass counter-melody.

- [ch.4 p.7] Baker treats the major 9th as the parallel major-mode departure chord: 'Chords 3 and 4 are resolving into chords 16 and 17. Each chord is moving from a major ninth via passing tones, to a major sixth. The only difference in these two forms is the mode.' The maj9 opens the major-mode modern progression exactly as min9 opens its minor counterpart.
- [ch.7 p.10] Baker prescribes the major 9th as the opening chord in major-mode song-form exercises, with the major 6th as its resolution: 'In both cases you are starting with the major or minor 9th and closing with the major or minor 6th.'
- [ch.29] Cmaj9 appears by name in 'Just Bobi' as a tonic-I color alongside Cmaj7 and Cmaj6, demonstrating that the maj9 is a valid tonic arrival voicing in Baker's chord-melody vocabulary — consistent with the maj9-to-maj6 voice-leading rule taught throughout the method.
- [ch.20 p.33] Baker prescribes extracting only the first (lowest) note of each arpeggiated maj9 or similar chord to form an independent counter-melody: 'in exercise 49 all the chords are played arpeggio but the first note of each chord is the most important. It forms a melodic line at the base of the chord and gives the whole example originality.'

## maj6
Baker uses the major 6th as both the tonic-I resolution target of the ninth-to-sixth system and as a practical tonic-I voicing throughout the method's harmony. Its enharmonic identity with the minor 7th built on its sixth degree makes it the cornerstone of the method's relative-chord substitution vocabulary. Even so, the maj6 must be subordinated or dropped when it conflicts with a chord-melody line.

- [ch.4 p.7 / ch.5 p.8] Baker prescribes the major 6th as the tonic resolution of the major ninth in modern progressions, reached via passing tones from maj9: 'moving from a major ninth via passing tones, to a major sixth.'
- [ch.9 p.12] Baker establishes that 'the C6 chord and the Am7 chord have the same tones. They are called relatives for this reason.' The major 6th chord is thus enharmonically equivalent to the minor 7th built on its 6th degree — a relative substitution that underpins the method's chord-substitution vocabulary.
- [ch.9 p.12 / ch.7 p.10] Baker uses the major 6th as a practical tonic-I chord form throughout the method: the C major tonal center typically manifests as a 6th or 9th chord in modern voice-leading rather than as a plain triad. The maj6 is the resting-point arrival after V resolves.
- [ch.26 p.42] Baker establishes that even the tonic major 6th chord must be subordinated or omitted when it conflicts with the melody in chord-melody arranging: 'pay strict attention to how chords are forced against the melody and, at some points, even dropped for a better melodic line.' Melody priority overrides harmonic completeness for all chord qualities including the tonic.

## min7
Baker treats the minor 7th as the base form of the II chord in II-V-I cycles and as enharmonically equivalent to its relative major 6th. The II chord carries its own Upper Partials (min6 through min13), all of which may substitute for the plain min7 in the II role. In ensemble texture, min7 shapes serve a rhythmic background-filling function rather than a melodic one.

- [ch.9 p.12] Baker teaches that Am7 and C6 share identical tones — 'They are called relatives for this reason' — making min7 a direct substitute for its relative major 6th. Baker also notes that 'the three tones that form the Dm chord are the upper three tones of the G9 chord,' extending the relative-chord concept to upper-voice extraction from dominant extensions.
- [ch.8 p.11 / ch.9 p.12] Baker prescribes the minor II chord as the preparatory chord before V in the I-II-V-I cycle, the progression 'in which we are most interested.' The II chord may be inserted before any dominant-to-tonic movement: 'it is possible to use the II chord instead of the V and resolve into the V from the II.' Min7 built on the second degree is the standard color for this role.
- [ch.10 p.14] Baker prescribes that the II chord carries its own Upper Partials: 'With the II chord of the scale the same principal applies, you have the 6th, 7th, 9th, 11th, and 13th. Any of these forms of the II chord can be used to resolve into the V which in turn resolves into the tonic.' The min7 is the base; min9, min11, min13, and min6 are all valid superstructures for the II-chord role.
- [ch.19 p.28] Baker prescribes the minor II chord (min7) as part of the rhythmic chord-and-melody background texture in small-combo settings: 'This is Rhythmic Chord and Melody Playing. It deals with background rather than melody … The guitar plays fill-ins and fragments of melody in the background.' Min7 shapes cycle through the II role while a vocalist or solo instrument carries the melody.

## aug7
Baker introduces the augmented 7th as both a dominant substitute (resolving directly to tonic like the dim7) and as the vehicle of Augmented Cycle Symmetric Harmony (major-third transposition through three tonics). The 3-tonic cycle can be expanded to six or twelve tonics by inserting intermediate chords. The aug7 is also prescribed as a non-diatonic color insertion for harmonic variety.

- [ch.21 p.34] Baker teaches that augmented chords 'may be used in a similar manner' to diminished 7th substitutes for the dominant: 'Augmented chords may be used in a similar manner. See Exercise 53.' Exercise 53 demonstrates the augmented chord resolving directly to the tonic as a V-I equivalent — a parallel to the dim7 substitution established earlier in Chapter 21.
- [ch.18 p.27] Baker defines the aug7 by extending the augmented triad: 'By adding a 7th to the Augmented chord you get still another sound (See Exercise 43).' He prescribes its use as a color device: 'many chords are used that don't appear in the original chord structure of a song. They are used to add color and originality.'
- [ch.17 p.26] Baker prescribes the augmented triad (and by extension aug7) as the vehicle for Augmented Cycles: 'Divide the 12 tone scale in three equal parts and you get three tonics, T. 1, 2, and 3. Exercise 40 demonstrates the Augmented chord in a Symetric Cycle.' Major-third transposition of aug7 reproduces the same pitch-class set, making it the 3-tonic symmetric analog to the dim7's 4-tonic cycle.
- [ch.17 p.26] Baker prescribes expanding the 3-tonic Augmented Cycle to six tonics by inserting intermediate chords: 'exercise 41 is similar to exercise 40, however, now we have six tonics, the additional tonics having been forced into the cycle.' The aug7 framework can thus accommodate up to 12 tonics via full Symmetric Harmony with parallel motion.

## dom7b9
Baker treats the dom7b9 as the conceptual parent of the diminished 7th chord — the rootless upper structure of a G7b9 is a B diminished — establishing the dom7b9 as the theoretical anchor for all diminished-as-dominant-substitute work. In Symmetric Harmony contexts, the dom7b9 (like all chord qualities) becomes a temporary tonic rather than a fixed dominant. The b9 coloring is also Baker's preferred upper-voice flavor above a pedal fifth.

- [ch.21 p.34] Baker teaches that 'they aren't diminished chords at all when used in this respect — they are the Upper tones to a diminished or flatted 9th chord.' A G7b9 with root omitted is presented side-by-side with B diminished (Exercises 51a and 51b) to prove harmonic equivalence: the dom7b9 shell is the conceptual parent, and the dim7 is its rootless upper structure.
- [ch.22 p.37] Baker prescribes G13b9 as one of the specific chord variations played above a G pedal tone in C Major: 'In exercise 56 … a Dm9 and a G13b9 chord play. This is the most popular form of Pedal Point.' The b9 coloring is explicitly named as the preferred upper-voice quality in this context.
- [ch.13 p.21] Baker establishes that in Symmetric Harmony 'every chord you play is, in actuality, the tonic. There can be as many tonics as you desire.' This principle applies to dom7b9 and all other chord qualities used in symmetric contexts — none has a fixed dominant or subdominant role; each is treated as a temporary tonic.

## dom13
Baker names the dom13 (specifically G13b9) as the dominant-quality chord of choice above a pedal fifth in C Major, appearing in both the pedagogical Pedal Point chapter and the closing performance solo. The 13th is enumerated as the outermost Upper Partial of the dominant family, sharing the same tonic-resolution function as all other dominant extensions.

- [ch.10 p.14 / ch.22 p.37] Baker lists the 13th as the outermost Upper Partial of the dominant chord. G13b9 appears by name in the performance solo 'Just Bobi' and as the chord played over a G pedal in C Major at the Cadence Break: 'a Dm9 and a G13b9 chord play. This is the most popular form of Pedal Point.' It resolves to the tonic on the same functional basis as all other dominant extensions.
- [ch.22 p.37] Baker specifies G13b9 by name as the dominant-quality chord played above the G pedal tone in C Major: 'a Dm9 and a G13b9 chord play. This is the most popular form of Pedal Point.' The dom13 with b9 alteration is the preferred dominant color above a pedal fifth in this configuration.

## dom9
Baker classifies the dom9 as a direct Upper Partial extension of the dominant, with the 9th available in natural or flatted (b9) form. Both variants retain the same tonic-resolution function as the plain dom7 and are members of the same dominant family.

- [ch.10 p.14] Baker classifies dom9 as an Upper Partial form of the dominant — one of the extensions produced by building above the 7th — with the same tonic-resolution function as plain dom7. The 9th may be natural or flatted (dom7b9); both remain members of the Upper Partials family and resolve to I.

## dom11
Baker explicitly enumerates both the natural 11th and the augmented 11th as members of the dominant's Upper Partials family, treating them as color variants of the same dominant function rather than structural departures.

- [ch.10 p.14] Baker enumerates the 11th and augmented 11th as explicit members of the dominant's Upper Partials family: 'The dominant chord can be the dominant 7th, 9th, 11th, aug. 11th or 13th.' Dom11 and dom7#11 share the same resolution function and are treated as color variants, not structural departures.
