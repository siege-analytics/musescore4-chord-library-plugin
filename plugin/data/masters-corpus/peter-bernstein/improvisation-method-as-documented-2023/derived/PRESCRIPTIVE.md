# Prescriptive lessons -- peter-bernstein x improvisation-method-as-documented-2023

## maj

Bernstein treats the major tonic (I chord) as a place of deliberate restraint rather than resolution: the root is avoided, tension is preserved via an I69 derived from the V, and overemphasis of the I is explicitly named as a momentum-killer. When the I chord is sustained over multiple measures, the prescription shifts from static voicing to active internal tension-and-resolution exploration -- the I is never a resting place, only a slower-moving zone of forward pull.

- [ch.6 p.12] **Avoid the root of the I chord.** Demonstrated on "Ornithology": Bernstein showed his preference for not playing the root of the I (one) chord, substituting instead a V-derived I69 that leaves tension partially unresolved.
- [ch.6 p.12 / ch.7 p.13] **Voice the I chord as I69 from the V.** The prescribed replacement for a rooted I voicing: an I69 derived from the V of that I, specifically to preserve forward momentum and avoid a sense of the music being dragged or held back.
- [ch.7 p.13] **Do not overemphasize the I chord** *(proscriptive)*. Overemphasis "gives the impression that the song is being dragged or held back." Under-resolve rather than settle.
- [ch.6 p.12] **Sustain-over-I: explore internal harmonic motion.** When the I chord holds for 2-4 measures, actively work tension and resolution internally rather than holding a static voicing; space and rhythm are the tools.

## min

For minor chords, as for all qualities, Bernstein's entry point is always the shell (root-3-7); extensions are added only after the melody-chord relationship is audible. No lessons here are exclusive to a generic `min` quality beyond the universal shell-first rule.

- [ch.5 p.9] **Shell voicing as entry point for minor chords.** Start with root, third, and seventh only. Listen to the melody and understand the basic relationship before deciding on extensions.

## min6

Min6 is Bernstein's **default** voicing for a minor tonic chord -- the major 6th replaces the b7 -- but it carries a hard constraint: the melody is final authority. When the melody explicitly lands on the b7 of that chord, the min6 is overridden and must not be used.

- [ch.4 p.7] **Min6 as default minor-tonic voicing.** For a minor-key I chord (e.g., Dm as tonic), prescribe the min6 (major 6th replaces minor 7th) unless the melody forces a different choice.
- [ch.4 p.7] **Do not use min6 when the melody note is the b7** *(proscriptive)*. The major 6th in a min6 voicing conflicts with a melody b7. Bernstein's worked Dm example: the default Dm6 is overridden when "the melody plays the minor 7th of the chord."

## min7

Min7 is not the default for a minor tonic but is prescribed specifically when the melody demands it -- the b7 in the melody makes min6 untenable, and quartal voicing is the prescribed vehicle to emphasize both the 7th and the 11th simultaneously.

- [ch.4 p.7] **Switch to min7 (quartal) when melody has the b7.** When the melody explicitly plays the minor 7th of the chord, move from default min6 to a Dm7-type chord, "possibly using quartal voicings to emphasize the 7th and the 11th." Melody overrides default quality.

## dom7

Bernstein's dominant-chord teaching runs on a strict progression: shells first, extensions only after melodic context is established, then tritone ii-V substitution as harmonic interest -- with rhythm placement called out as equally critical as the substitution itself. The "dress the melody note" principle provides the specific trigger for reaching for alterations like #11.

- [ch.5 p.9] **Shell voicing as entry point for dom7.** Begin with root, third, and seventh only. "He stopped me and advised me to start with basic chords first ... before making more informed decisions about incorporating chord extensions."
- [ch.5 p.9] **Use dom7#11 to dress a melody note that would otherwise double the bass.** When a melody note risks being doubled by the bass (weakening it), voice the dominant as dom7#11 so the melody note becomes the #11. Worked example: B7#11, where F becomes the #11, giving the melody note a "different outfit that enhances its impact."
- [ch.7 p.13] **Tritone ii-V substitution on resolving dominants, with rhythmic precision.** Play the ii-V a tritone from the original to add harmonic interest at resolution. Rhythmic placement within the bar is critical -- "playing it in the incorrect position within the bar can create a sense of awkwardness."
- [ch.4 p.6] **Progressive enrichment: conservative entry, then explore from chorus 3-4.** Across a full tune, start with shell voicings and reliable signature runs, then begin exploring extensions and substitutions from the third or fourth chorus onward, after melody-chord relationships are mapped.

## dom7#11

Dom7#11 appears as a targeted solution to a specific melodic problem -- not as a general default alteration -- and the trigger is always perceptible: the melody note is at risk of sounding weak because the bass doubles it.

- [ch.5 p.9] **Dom7#11 as melodic "outfit" when melody note doubles the bass.** Prescribe dom7#11 specifically when the melody note would otherwise be doubled by the bass and feel weak. The #11 gives the melody note harmonic distinction. Example: B7#11 where the F in the melody becomes the #11.
