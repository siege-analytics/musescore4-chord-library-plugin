# Prescriptive lessons — ted-greene × chord-chemistry

## maj

Greene treats the major triad as the entry point for all chord study, prescribing a strict learning sequence before any extensions are introduced. The 3rd is the chord's defining tone but is expendable for color, while the root is the bass anchor for final phrases.

- [ch.11 p.62] Omitting the 3rd — especially when replaced by a 2nd — yields a distinctive sus2 color that Greene explicitly recommends experimenting with on major-type chords.
- [ch.14 p.77] In chord melody on a minor-key song, ending on the parallel major (Picardy third) is prescribed as a standard brightening cadential technique.
- [ch.16 p.85] To expand a triad to four notes, double any of root, 3rd, or 5th; in open-triad voicings, the root must go in the bass on the final chord of a phrase.

## maj7

Greene positions maj7 as the third tier of major-family study — learned after major and maj6 — and treats it primarily as the stable resolution target of V7–I motion. Voicing liberties center on 5th omission; the 3rd, though droppable for color, remains the chord's defining characteristic.

- [ch.5 p.10] Maj7 belongs to the Major family; all chords in the family derive from the major triad father via formula extension.
- [ch.5 p.10] In any five-note chord, omit root or 5th (never both); the 5th is the preferred omission when unaltered, possibly doubling a remaining tone.
- [ch.7 p.13] Learning-order prescription: major → min → maj6 → min6 → **maj7** → min7 → dom7. Maj7 is tier three.
- [ch.8 p.17] Chord synonyms unlock substitutions otherwise invisible: maj7 chords frequently share all notes with a min7 whose root is a minor 3rd lower — exploit this for sub discovery.
- [ch.11 p.63] Maj7 (and maj9) voicings with root on top or 7th in bass function as passing chords into a major-type chord a 4th higher — a specific half-step approach role.
- [ch.11 p.63] The 3rd of a major-type chord is optional; replacing it with a 2nd produces a sus2 color Greene singles out as especially effective.
- [ch.13 p.69] The V7 → Imaj7 resolution is the primary voice-leading laboratory: the 3rd of V7 moves up a half step to the root of I; omitting the 5th of Imaj7 is acceptable when this voice leading is present.
- [ch.13 p.73] Lowering one root in a doubled-root major chord produces maj7 naturally — doubling is the conceptual precursor to the maj7 tone.
- [ch.14 p.76] In chord melody, the melody note must sit above the maj7 voicing at all times; chords land on the first note of each measure, not on every melody note.

## maj7#11

Greene treats maj7#11 as a major-family extension with one internal placement rule governing the #11's register within the voicing.

- [ch.5 p.10] The maj7#11 is a major-family extension; the #11 is placed above the 5th in any voicing that includes a 5th (as in +11, 13+11 shapes).

## min

Greene uses the plain minor primarily as a simplified substitute in two specific contexts — harmonic minor progressions and IV-chord color substitutions — rather than as a standalone teaching subject at this level.

- [ch.16 p.84] Substituting the relative minor for the IV chord of a key is a standard technique; it shares most tones and darkens the color without leaving the key.
- [ch.17 p.88] In harmonic minor contexts, plain minor may replace Im(maj7) as a simplification that preserves minor tonality at reduced complexity cost.

## min7

Min7 is the workhorse of Greene's harmonic motion system: it is the II chord in every IIm7–V7–I back-cycle, the standard upper-structure substitute over a dom7 a 4th below, and the characteristic chord of the dorian mode. Voicing follows the universal five-note omission rule, with the unaltered 5th as the preferred drop.

- [ch.5 p.10] Five-note omission rule applies: drop root or 5th (unaltered 5th preferred); do not omit both simultaneously.
- [ch.7 p.13] Learning-order prescription: tier three, alongside maj7 and dom7, after major/minor and the 6th chords.
- [ch.9 p.55] Min7 belongs to the Minor family; minor and min7 are closely related in the same way major and maj7 are — the 7th is an additive extension, not a structural break.
- [ch.10 p.58] A min7 whose root is a 5th above a dominant chord may temporarily substitute for that dominant, but only for part of its duration — the dom7 (or extension) must still appear.
- [ch.12 p.66] Back-cycling: preceding any target chord with its IIm7–V7 creates forward momentum and smooths key-change transitions.
- [ch.13 p.71] The IIm7–V7 pairing is foundational: after a min7, a dominant chord a 4th higher is expected and may be inserted if absent.
- [ch.14 p.75] The m(maj7)→m7 voice-leading rule: the major-7th tone moves up or down one fret into the root or b7 — this half-step resolution is the defining event of the progression.
- [ch.15 p.80] Ear-training substitution rule: if you hear IIm–I, reinterpret as IIm–V7–I and supply the implied dominant.
- [ch.17 p.88] Modal equivalence: D dorian minor shares all notes and chords with C major; min7 is the characteristic tonic chord of dorian.
- [ch.19 p.101] In blues measure 7, IIIm7 (the minor-quality variant) substitutes for the I chord, providing a softer, jazzier color than III7.

## min7b5

Greene restricts this chord sharply: it is the only altered minor type in common use, its b5 is non-negotiable in any voicing, and it appears both as a structural diatonic chord in modal contexts and as a natural VII chord of harmonic minor.

- [ch.5 p.10] The altered 5th must be retained in all voicings — it is the chord's defining tone. The root may still be omitted; the b5 may not.
- [ch.11 p.61] Among altered minor chords, only m7b5 sees wide use. All other altered minor forms are effectively off the menu.
- [ch.16 p.87] In specific modal scale harmonizations, Im7b5 appears as a structural diatonic chord — not a chromatic passing color but a scale-function chord.

## min6

Greene imposes a hard contextual ban: the min6 must not precede a dominant chord a 4th above, because its tones prefigure that dominant and create premature resolution.

- [ch.11 p.61] Do not use m6 when the following chord is a dominant a 4th higher — the shared tones make the m6 sound like an early arrival of the next chord, destroying harmonic syntax.

## min9

Min9 follows the standard five-note voicing policy with the unaltered 5th as the default omission, leaving root, m3, b7, and 9 as the core shell.

- [ch.5 p.10] Five-note omission: drop root or 5th; unaltered 5th is the standard candidate, yielding the four-note root–m3–b7–9 shell.

## min-maj7

Greene treats m(maj7) as an inherently transitional chord that almost demands voice leading — it is the Im chord of harmonic minor, the source chord for the m(maj7)→m7 descent, and a dom7 substitute available when that dominant is acting as a V7. Its major-7th tone is the functional lynchpin in every context.

- [ch.12 p.66] M(maj7) may substitute for a dominant chord whose root is one fret lower (e.g., Cm(maj7)/9 for B7), but only when that dominant is functioning as a V7 or V7-function chord.
- [ch.13 p.73] M(maj7) is cited among chords that "almost necessitate" voice-leading principles — its inherent tension toward resolution makes isolated use unstable.
- [ch.14 p.75] The defining rule: when pairing m(maj7) with m7 on the same root, the major-7th tone must move up or down one fret into the root or b7 of the m7.
- [ch.17 p.88] In harmonic minor progressions, Im(maj7) is the default tonic chord; plain minor may substitute as a simplification.
- [ch.20 p.107] A resolving diminished chord may replace m(maj7) provided it contains the major-7th tone — the shared maj7 tone is the structural link.

## dom7

The dominant 7th is Greene's most elaborated chord family, constituting the largest section of the book's prescriptive content. Its tritone defines functional harmony, its back-cycling mechanics drive every progression, and its altered extensions are exhaustively restricted and ranked. The 3rd and b7 form the essential shell; root and 5th are the standard omission candidates.

- [ch.5 p.10 / ch.8 p.17] Dom7 is one of the three foundational chord fathers; by sheer variety, it vastly outnumbers major and minor types combined.
- [ch.10 p.58] Altered dominants (b5, #5, b9, #9 variants) are contextually valid only when the next chord has a root a 4th higher, a half-step lower, or is a minor chord on the same root.
- [ch.10 p.59] Secondary dominant rule: when any major or minor chord is followed by a chord a 4th higher, the first chord's second half may be replaced by a dominant on the same root.
- [ch.10 p.60] Back-cycling with dominants: for any dominant target, the back-cycle chord is another dominant a 5th above; the technique chains indefinitely.
- [ch.11 p.61 / ch.15 p.81] Tritone substitution framework: all dominants a 5th apart are related; any dom7 may be replaced by the dom7 a b5 away.
- [ch.11 p.62 / ch.12 p.68] Common tones on the top note smooth transitions between dominant chords in cycle motion — use them deliberately to reduce melodic jumpiness.
- [ch.12 p.65] Any dominant chord may be moved freely in 3-fret intervals provided the final chord in the sequence resolves correctly to its target.
- [ch.14 p.76 / ch.14 p.77] Chord melody placement: dom7 chords land on the first note of each measure; chords are played every few notes, not on every melody note.
- [ch.14 p.79] Back-cycling definition: inserting a V chord immediately before any chord is called back-cycling; two back-cycles yield V and V-of-V.
- [ch.16 p.84] When a dom7 triad is needed, leave out root or 5th; the 3rd–b7 tritone shell is the irreducible core.
- [ch.17 p.88 / ch.17 p.90] Dom7 is the characteristic tonic chord of the mixolydian mode; mixolydian harmony gravitates naturally toward blues and rock idioms.
- [ch.18 p.93] Blues harmonic identity: the dominant 7th family defines the blues sound; I, IV, and V all carry dom7 quality; bar 1 is always a form of I, bar 5 always a form of IV.
- [ch.18 p.95] In chord melody, inversion choice is determined by the melody note — select whichever inversion places the target pitch on top.
- [ch.19 p.99] Turnaround law: the final chord of any turnaround is always a V7 or its b5 (tritone sub) — no exceptions.
- [ch.19 p.101] In blues measure 7, III7 (the dominant-quality variant) substitutes for the I chord, yielding a brighter, more aggressive color than IIIm7.
- [ch.20 p.105 / ch.20 p.106] Chromatic voice-leading technique: lowering the 3rd and b7 each by one fret converts any I7 into a IV9; applied progressively through a cycle, it generates a stepwise descending chromatic sequence.
- [ch.20 p.110] Rock substitution rule: in rock contexts, dominant 7th and its extensions may blanket-substitute for any major chord.
- [ch.20 p.110] Rock rhythmic cell: the I–IV–I sandwich inserts the IV7 mid-measure between two I7 chords, creating a characteristic rhythmic gesture.

## dom7alt

Altered dominants are permitted in three resolution contexts only and are expressly banned from opening a song (except 7#9). The altered 5th is structurally mandatory; the altered tone should appear on top of the voicing to project its color. Chromatic movement in 2-fret intervals is their characteristic fingerboard motion.

- [ch.5 p.10] Any altered 5th (raised or lowered) must be retained in the voicing — it is essential. The root remains omittable; the altered tone is not.
- [ch.10 p.58] Valid resolution contexts for altered dominants: (1) root of next chord is a 4th higher, (2) root of next chord is a half-step lower, (3) next chord is a minor type on the same root.
- [ch.10 p.59] Altered dominants may not replace a I7 at the opening of a song; first establish the plain I7 or extension, then introduce the alteration — the sole exception is 7#9.
- [ch.10 p.59] The altered tone's most critical placement is on top of the voicing (highest pitch), where it projects its color most effectively.
- [ch.12 p.66] Altered dominants (b5/#5) move in 2-fret intervals on the fingerboard — distinct from the 3-fret movement available to plain dominants.

## dom7#9

The 7#9 is the uniquely privileged altered dominant: it may replace a I7 even at the start of a song, and its internal voicing rule requires the #9 to sit above the 3rd at all times.

- [ch.5 p.10] Internal voicing rule: the #9 must be placed above the 3rd in the chord stack — never below it.
- [ch.10 p.58–59] The 7#9 is the only altered dominant exempt from the tonic-placement ban: it may directly replace a I7 for a bluesy sound at any point in a song, including the opening.

## dom7b9

Dom7b9 is an altered dominant governed by the standard altered-tone voicing rules; its practical application centers on smooth resolution, facilitated by common tones on top.

- [ch.6 p.12] Formula definition: C7b9b5 = 1, 3, b5, b7, b9 — the b9 is additive on the dom7 structure, parsed mechanically from the symbol.
- [ch.11 p.62] Common tones on the top note smooth resolution from dom7b9 to the target I chord; finding and retaining a shared pitch is the primary voice-leading tool.

## dom7#11

The #11 dominant is an altered dominant reserved strictly for V7 function — it may never substitute for a I7. The #11 must sit above any 5th present in the voicing, and the 9b5 voicing (which omits the 5th) is the more practical choice in most situations.

- [ch.5 p.10] Internal placement rule: the #11 goes above the 5th in the voicing (as in +11 and 13+11 shapes); the 5th must be present for this rule to apply.
- [ch.11 p.61] The +11 chord substitutes freely for any dominant followed by a chord a 4th higher — the standard V7-function context.
- [ch.11 p.61] Tonic ban: the +11 does not assume tonic function and must not replace a I7 at the start of a song.
- [ch.11 p.61 / ch.8 p.52] Practical preference: 9b5 is more commonly used than +11 because it has a less muddy sound and is more easily deployed.

## dom7b5

Dom7b5 (= 9b5 without the 9th) is the cleaner, less muddy alternative to +11 and participates fully in tritone-substitution movement.

- [ch.8 p.52] The 9b5 and +11 are structurally near-identical; 9b5 omits the natural 5th while +11 retains it alongside the b5, giving 9b5 a cleaner sound.
- [ch.12 p.63] Dom7b5 participates in tritone substitution; like all dominants, it may also be moved in 3-fret intervals provided the final chord resolves correctly.

## dom9

Dom9 is the primary blues sophistication upgrade from plain dom7 and arises naturally from dom7 through a one-fret chromatic descent of the 3rd and b7.

- [ch.18 p.95] An abundance of dom9 chords produces a noticeably bluesier sound than dom7; the 9th extension is the first and most effective color upgrade in blues contexts.
- [ch.20 p.106] Voice-leading derivation: lowering the 3rd and b7 of any I7 each by one fret generates the IV9 — the dom9 emerges from chromatic stepwise motion rather than formula memorization.

## dom9#5b5

This chord's intervallic density restricts it to a single available inversion, making it one of the most voicing-limited chords in the book.

- [ch.8 p.52] There is apparently only one usable inversion of the 9#5b5 chord; its internal interval density forecloses the standard inversion possibilities available to other extended dominants.

## dom11

The dominant 11th's defining voicing rule is structural, not merely aesthetic: the 3rd must be omitted because it clashes with the 11th and destroys the chord's characteristic open sound.

- [ch.5 p.10] In any chord containing the 11th, the 3rd is often omitted — particularly in the 11th chord itself — because the interval clash degrades the voicing.
- [ch.11 p.61] Dom11 chords (especially with no 3rd) are among the most sonically beautiful when used properly; the 3rd omission is the condition of proper use, not an option.

## dom11b9

The 11b9 is a high-tension chord whose strong pull restricts it to the primary V7 of a key and virtually nowhere else.

- [ch.12 p.64] The 11b9 is reserved for the V7 of a key — authentic primary dominant function only.

## dom13b9

Dom13b9 permits dropping either the b7 or the 3rd, but never both simultaneously — one of the two must remain to preserve the chord's identity.

- [ch.8 p.17] Either the b7 or the 3rd may be omitted from a 13b9 voicing, but not both.

## aug

Augmented chords sit outside the three main families but function as altered dominants. Their symmetry — repeating every 4 frets — enables free chromatic movement, and they are best understood as dom7#5 shells with the b7 temporarily absent.

- [ch.6 p.12] Augmented and diminished chords do not belong to any of the three families but are most like dominant chords in usage.
- [ch.5 p.10] The #5 (raised 5th) is an altered tone and may never be omitted; the root remains droppable.
- [ch.12 p.63] Augmented chords repeat every 4 frets — any aug voicing moved 4 frets is enharmonically equivalent, enabling fluid neck movement.
- [ch.12 p.63] Think of the augmented triad as a dom7#5 with no b7; adding the b7 to any aug chord completes the dominant altered shape.

## dim7

Diminished 7th chords function as altered dominants despite their structural independence. Their 3-fret symmetry gives each voicing four enharmonic identities and enables free chromatic passing motion; their primary role is as a linking chord between harmonically adjacent targets.

- [ch.6 p.12] Dim7 sits outside the three families but is used most like a dominant chord.
- [ch.8 p.17] Each dim7 voicing has four enharmonic names (one per chord tone as root) — quadrupling its substitution potential without changing any notes.
- [ch.11 p.62] Symmetry rule: any dim7 voicing repeats itself every 3 frets; free 3-fret movement on the neck keeps the same chord identity throughout.
- [ch.11 p.62] Primary prescribed role: passing chord — insert dim7 between two harmonically adjacent chords; it does not typically function as a goal chord.
- [ch.16 p.86] A VII diminished triad sounds like the dominant 7th whose root is two whole steps lower — the dim7-as-rootless-dom7b9 substitution is structural.
- [ch.19 p.98 / ch.11 p.62] Blues passing application: #IVo is the standard passing chord following a IV or IV7 and leading back to I or I7.
- [ch.20 p.106 / ch.20 p.107] The "resolving diminished" technique: a dim7 whose root is one fret above the V7's root replaces the V7.

## sus4

The suspended 4th chord is prescribed primarily as a dominant preparation device — the 4th resolves down to the 3rd of the following dom7 — though unresolved sus4 replacing the dominant outright is permitted contextually.

- [ch.5 p.10] Formula definition: any chord with the 3rd fully replaced by the 4th is suspended.
- [ch.8 p.17] When the 3rd is absent from a dom9 chord, the chord is also called "9 suspended" — establishing a synonym bridge between 9sus4 and dom9-no-3 voicings.
- [ch.11 p.62] Normative function: suspended dominants are partial replacements for dom7, implying the 4th resolves down a half step to the 3rd of the following dominant.
- [ch.11 p.62] Exception: sus4 chords may be left unresolved, directly replacing the dom7 altogether, when context supports the open sound.
